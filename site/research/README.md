## Learning and knowledge (40)

### T003 — Peer learning circles

Learning circles put facilitation around shared learning rather than requiring an expert teacher to deliver every answer. P2PU describes the facilitator as a co-learner who supports conversation, keeps the group on track, and maintains the meeting structure.

**Limit:** This describes a particular peer learning model; its accessibility does not establish equivalent outcomes for every subject or participant.

**Example situation:** Hypothetically, six neighbors could meet weekly at a library to finish an introductory statistics course, bring confusing exercises, and compare explanations before moving forward.

**Source:** [Facilitating Peer Learning](https://docs.p2pu.org/facilitation/facilitation-basics) — Peer 2 Peer University. practitioner method; **access:** search excerpt.

### T005 — Study groups

Peer-led study groups give learners a recurring setting to tackle course material together. Cornell describes undergraduate teaching assistants using relationships with students to notice confusion and adapt collaborative sessions to their needs.

**Limit:** The example involves supported undergraduate teaching assistants; an informal study group may lack their preparation, supervision, and access to instructors.

**Example situation:** Hypothetically, students struggling with calculus could reserve a weekly room, bring problems they could not solve, and take turns explaining possible solutions together.

**Source:** [Supporting Student Well-Being with Peer-Led Study Groups](https://teaching.cornell.edu/teaching-spotlight/provosts-working-group-innovation-assessment/supporting-student-well-being-peer) — Cornell University. institutional guide; **access:** search excerpt.

### T006 — Journal clubs

Journal clubs organize discussion around selected research papers. A leader chooses a coherent topic and guides participants through reading, presentation, and critique so that a publication becomes material for collective investigation.

**Limit:** Discussing published research develops interpretation; a group discussion cannot independently establish that the paper is correct or its findings generalize.

**Example situation:** Hypothetically, a laboratory could discuss one paper each fortnight, assigning different members to explain the methods, question the controls, and identify a follow-up experiment.

**Source:** [Journal Club Leader Toolkit 2024](https://www.training.nih.gov/documents/31/Journal_Club_Leader_Toolkit_2024_508checked.pdf) — National Institutes of Health. institutional guide; **access:** search excerpt.

### T007 — Socratic seminars

A Socratic seminar uses a shared text and open questions to organize inquiry. Participants examine interpretations through discussion, while the facilitator supports questioning and participation instead of treating the session as a lecture.

**Limit:** The format depends on preparation and discussion norms; it is an inquiry practice, not a voting procedure or competitive debate.

**Example situation:** Hypothetically, a reading group could discuss a short essay on work, asking which assumptions support its argument and inviting competing interpretations grounded in the text.

**Source:** [Socratic Seminars](https://www.readwritethink.org/professional-development/strategy-guides/socratic-seminars) — ReadWriteThink / NCTE. institutional guide; **access:** search excerpt.

### T008 — Teach-back

Teach-back checks an explanation by asking the recipient to describe the information or action in their own words. The explainer uses the response to identify misunderstandings and explain again where needed.

**Limit:** The source concerns healthcare communication; using the pattern elsewhere is an adaptation, and recall alone does not demonstrate practical competence.

**Example situation:** Hypothetically, a volunteer coordinator could explain how equipment checkout works, ask a newcomer to walk through the process, and clarify the misunderstood return step.

**Source:** [Teach-back](https://www.ahrq.gov/patient-safety/reports/engage/interventions/teachback.html) — Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality. institutional guide; **access:** search excerpt.

### T009 — Peer instruction

Peer instruction makes learners compare reasoning before an instructor resolves a concept question. Cornell describes a polling sequence in which participants answer individually, discuss with a neighbor, answer again, and then examine the reasoning behind correct answers and common misconceptions.

**Limit:** Peer discussion can circulate mistakes as well as insights; question design and subsequent clarification remain important parts of the method.

**Example situation:** Hypothetically, a workshop leader could ask participants to predict an outcome, compare their answers in pairs, vote again, and then discuss why their reasoning changed.

**Source:** [Incorporating Short Peer Discussion Activities for In-Class Collaborative Learning](https://teaching.cornell.edu/teaching-resources/active-collaborative-learning/collaborative-learning/incorporating-short-peer) — Cornell University. institutional guide; **access:** page.

### T010 — Deliberate practice with feedback

Deliberate practice organizes effort around improving a specific aspect of performance, with informative feedback and opportunities to correct errors. Its mechanism differs from merely accumulating time doing familiar tasks without targeted revision.

**Limit:** This influential account concerns expert performance; it does not establish that practice alone explains every difference in ability or achievement.

**Example situation:** Hypothetically, a public speaker could rehearse only the opening minute, receive feedback on unclear sentences, revise them, and repeat before practicing the entire talk.

**Source:** [The Role of Deliberate Practice in the Acquisition of Expert Performance](https://blogs.ischool.berkeley.edu/i225s14/files/2014/04/Ericsson-1993-article.pdf) — Psychological Review / Ericsson, Krampe and Tesch-Römer. research paper; **access:** search excerpt.

### T011 — Job shadowing

Job shadowing places a learner alongside someone doing their usual work for a defined period. Watching real decisions and routines exposes duties and approaches that are difficult to capture in a classroom description.

**Limit:** Observation provides exposure rather than independent practice; sensitive work and the observer’s limited access may conceal important parts of the role.

**Example situation:** Hypothetically, a new museum volunteer could follow the visitor-services coordinator for an afternoon, noting recurring questions and discussing how the coordinator handles difficult moments.

**Source:** [Federal Leadership Development Programs: Shadowing Assignments](https://www.opm.gov/policy-data-oversight/training-and-development/federal-leadership-development-programs/) — US Office of Personnel Management. institutional guide; **access:** search excerpt.

### T012 — Job rotation

Job rotation uses temporary assignments outside a person’s normal position to expose them to different responsibilities and organizational challenges. Learning comes through performing unfamiliar work, not just hearing how another department operates.

**Limit:** Temporary assignments need suitable supervision and meaningful responsibilities; moving people around without learning goals can simply disrupt ongoing work.

**Example situation:** Hypothetically, an operations employee could spend a month with customer support, handle a defined subset of cases, and bring recurring service problems back to operations.

**Source:** [Federal Supervisory and Managerial Frameworks and Guidance](https://www.opm.gov/policy-data-oversight/training-and-development/training-program-management/federal-supervisory-and-managerial-frameworks-and-guidance.pdf) — US Office of Personnel Management. institutional guide; **access:** search excerpt.

### T013 — Cross-training

Cross-training prepares team members to cover more than one role. In the cited care-team model, explicit responsibilities and standard work make it possible to share tasks while maintaining an understanding of who does what.

**Limit:** The source concerns healthcare teams; coverage must respect competence and role boundaries rather than treating every responsibility as interchangeable.

**Example situation:** Hypothetically, a small nonprofit could teach two staff members its donation-recording procedure so that leave or illness does not leave incoming contributions unrecorded.

**Source:** [Creating Patient-Centered Team-Based Primary Care](https://ce.effectivehealthcare.ahrq.gov/sites/default/files/wysiwyg/ncepcr/tools/PCMH/creating-patient-centered-team-based-primary-care-white-paper.pdf) — Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality. institutional guide; **access:** search excerpt.

### T014 — Office hours

Office hours create an identified opportunity to approach an instructor outside the main session. Cornell recommends explaining their purpose and offering accessible times and interaction modes so learners can bring questions and seek support.

**Limit:** Availability alone does not ensure use; timing, perceived approachability, and participants’ access needs can still prevent people from attending.

**Example situation:** Hypothetically, a project maintainer could reserve an hour each week when contributors bring confusing design questions, reducing the need to arrange a separate meeting each time.

**Source:** [Universal Design for Learning: Office Hours](https://teaching.cornell.edu/teaching-resources/inclusion-belonging-accessibility/universal-design-learning) — Cornell University. institutional guide; **access:** search excerpt.

### T015 — Ask-me-anything sessions

An ask-me-anything session temporarily concentrates audience questions around an available guest or host. Reddit’s implementation adds scheduling, guest collaborators, answered-question navigation, and a clear closing point to organize that exchange.

**Limit:** An open invitation to ask does not guarantee answers, representative participation, or expertise; moderation and selection still shape the discussion.

**Example situation:** Hypothetically, a neighborhood organizer could host a scheduled online question session before a street festival, answering practical questions while marking unresolved issues for later follow-up.

**Source:** [New Ways To AMA](https://redditinc.com/news/new-ways-to-ama?version=published) — Reddit. practitioner method; **access:** search excerpt.

### T017 — Show-and-tell sessions

Show-and-tell sessions make work in progress visible to people outside the immediate task. Demonstrating actual outputs and discussing learning creates an opportunity for questions and feedback before work becomes a finished announcement.

**Limit:** A demonstration can hide difficult tradeoffs if it becomes performance theatre; useful feedback requires showing uncertainties as well as polished results.

**Example situation:** Hypothetically, a community garden team could show its first irrigation prototype at a monthly gathering and ask other gardeners which beds and routines it overlooks.

**Source:** [Agile Tools and Techniques](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/agile-delivery/agile-tools-techniques) — UK Government Digital Service. institutional guide; **access:** search excerpt.

### T018 — Workshops

A workshop concentrates organized learning into a bounded event with prepared material, instructors, and participant activity. The Carpentries handbook makes preparation and instructional responsibilities explicit rather than assuming that gathering people automatically creates learning.

**Limit:** The source describes a particular training organization; workshop quality varies with curriculum, facilitation, available practice, and participants’ starting knowledge.

**Example situation:** Hypothetically, a repair club could run a two-hour workshop where participants bring a broken zipper, observe a demonstration, and practice repairs with instructor feedback.

**Source:** [Instructors Handbook](https://docs.carpentries.org/handbooks/instructors.html) — The Carpentries. institutional guide; **access:** page.

### T019 — Train-the-trainer

Train-the-trainer prepares participants to deliver a method to others through observation, practice teaching, and feedback. Knowledge spreads through successive trainers, while coaching helps maintain more than a superficial copy of the original presentation.

**Limit:** A cascade can dilute or distort material; observing a training does not by itself establish readiness to teach it independently.

**Example situation:** Hypothetically, a food-sharing network could train local coordinators to run volunteer onboarding, have each rehearse a segment, and review questions arising in their first sessions.

**Source:** [Understanding the Training of Trainers Model](https://www.cdc.gov/healthy-schools-training/php/tot/) — US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. institutional guide; **access:** search excerpt.

### T020 — Learning cohorts

A learning cohort gives a bounded group a shared curriculum and period of participation. Keeping participants together makes repeated interaction and mutual accountability possible instead of treating each learning event as an unrelated drop-in session.

**Limit:** Closed membership can support continuity while limiting entry; a scheduled cohort does not automatically become a durable community of practice.

**Example situation:** Hypothetically, ten new cooperative treasurers could complete a six-week accounting program together, compare weekly exercises, and check one another’s progress between meetings.

**Source:** [Cohort Faculty Development](https://cte.tamu.edu/programming/cohorts-faculty-dev.html) — Texas A&M University. institutional guide; **access:** search excerpt.

### T021 — Reading salons

The Reader’s shared-reading format brings people together to hear stories and poems read aloud and optionally discuss their responses. A prepared leader and accessible participation reduce the need to complete reading before attending.

**Limit:** This is a contemporary reading-group model, not a history of literary salons; other salons may require substantial advance reading.

**Example situation:** Hypothetically, a neighborhood host could invite people to a monthly evening where a short story is read aloud, followed by conversation that welcomes listeners as well as speakers.

**Source:** [Shared Reading FAQs](https://www.thereader.org.uk/shared-reading/shared-reading-faqs/) — The Reader. practitioner method; **access:** page.

### T022 — Oral history

Oral history turns recorded interviews into a preserved account of lived experience. Preparation, consent, interviewing, preservation, and decisions about access shape whose memories enter a public record and how others can use them.

**Limit:** Memories are situated interpretations rather than neutral transcripts of events; consent and access decisions can appropriately limit publication or reuse.

**Example situation:** Hypothetically, residents could interview longtime shopkeepers before a market closes, agree on which recordings may be shared, and deposit the approved interviews in a local archive.

**Source:** [Oral History Best Practices](https://oralhistory.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/2025-OHA-PrinciplesBP_Best-Practices.pdf) — Oral History Association. institutional guide; **access:** search excerpt.

### T023 — Storytelling

Story dialogue starts with someone’s concrete experience and uses structured discussion to move from description toward interpretation and possible action. Participants ask what occurred, why it matters, and what their community could do next.

**Limit:** A compelling account can illuminate experience without representing everyone; the cited facilitated method is narrower than storytelling as a whole.

**Example situation:** Hypothetically, residents could share accounts of missing the last bus, compare the consequences, and use recurring themes to design a proposal for evening transport.

**Source:** [Storytelling: Story Dialogue Technique](https://www.betterevaluation.org/sites/default/files/Toolkit_2014.pdf) — BetterEvaluation (hosted toolkit). practitioner method; **access:** search excerpt.

### T024 — Case-method discussion

Case-method discussion places participants inside a concrete decision situation and asks them to reason through possible choices. The instructor prepares both substantive questions and the discussion process, adapting as contrasting interpretations emerge.

**Limit:** A case is a selective representation of events; strong classroom reasoning does not establish that the same decision will work elsewhere.

**Example situation:** Hypothetically, cooperative members could discuss a fictional café facing rising rent, compare options using the same facts, and explain what additional information would change their choices.

**Source:** [Teaching by the Case Method](https://www.hbs.edu/teaching/case-method) — Harvard Business School. institutional guide; **access:** search excerpt.

### T025 — Simulation exercises

Simulation exercises let participants rehearse responses to a constructed situation before a real incident. Planning, conduct, and reporting make the exercise a way to examine preparedness and coordination, with different formats testing different capabilities.

**Limit:** Performance in a controlled scenario does not guarantee performance in a real crisis; exercise assumptions can omit important constraints.

**Example situation:** Hypothetically, festival organizers could rehearse a fictional power outage, assign their actual roles, and discover which decisions lack a named owner before opening day.

**Source:** [WHO Simulation Exercise Manual](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/WHO-WHE-CPI-2017.10) — World Health Organization. institutional guide; **access:** search excerpt.

### T026 — After-action reviews

An after-action review structures participants’ reflection after an event to identify what happened, what helped or hindered the response, and what to change. WHO describes multiple facilitated formats and emphasizes follow-up on learning.

**Limit:** A review records participants’ perspectives and learning needs; without follow-through, identified lessons may never change subsequent practice or resource allocation.

**Example situation:** Hypothetically, a volunteer team could meet after a neighborhood fair to compare its plans with events, identify avoidable queues, and assign two changes for next year.

**Source:** [Guidance for After Action Review](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/WHO-WHE-CPI-2019.4) — World Health Organization. institutional guide; **access:** search excerpt.

### T028 — Peer review of work

Peer assessment organizes people to evaluate one another’s work against shared expectations. Giving and receiving specific feedback makes quality criteria discussable and supplies perspectives beyond a single instructor or manager.

**Limit:** Peers need guidance and usable criteria; agreement among reviewers does not automatically establish accuracy, fairness, or an appropriate final grade.

**Example situation:** Hypothetically, grant writers in a small organization could exchange drafts and use a shared checklist to identify unsupported claims before submitting their applications.

**Source:** [Peer Assessment](https://teaching.cornell.edu/teaching-resources/assessing-student-learning/peer-assessment) — Cornell University. institutional guide; **access:** search excerpt.

### T029 — Replication

Replication asks whether a finding appears again when a study is repeated with new observations. The TOP framework distinguishes this from computational reproducibility, which examines whether available data and code regenerate reported analyses.

**Limit:** A failed or successful replication needs interpretation in context; design differences, uncertainty, and measurement choices complicate simple declarations of truth.

**Example situation:** Hypothetically, a second laboratory could repeat a published behavioral experiment with new participants and report both the estimate and differences from the original protocol.

**Source:** [Transparency and Openness Promotion Guidelines](https://www.cos.io/initiatives/top-guidelines) — Center for Open Science. institutional guide; **access:** search excerpt.

### T030 — Preregistration

Preregistration records a study’s planned questions, design, and analyses before outcomes are known. This preserves a distinction between testing advance expectations and generating new explanations after inspecting the results.

**Limit:** Preregistration does not forbid exploratory analysis or guarantee sound design; deviations and new analyses should be identified transparently.

**Example situation:** Hypothetically, a research team could register its primary outcome and analysis before collecting survey responses, then label unexpected subgroup patterns as exploratory in its report.

**Source:** [Preregistration](https://www.cos.io/initiatives/prereg) — Center for Open Science. institutional guide; **access:** search excerpt.

### T031 — Open notebooks

Open notebook science shares experimental records and raw information while research is underway. Bradley’s described solubility project uses this visibility to let geographically dispersed contributors inspect and build on ongoing experimental work.

**Limit:** The source is a colloquium description, not an effectiveness evaluation; privacy, confidentiality, and sensitive research can constrain what becomes public.

**Example situation:** Hypothetically, a materials research group could publish dated experimental notes and failed trials so another lab can inspect procedures before deciding which experiment to extend.

**Source:** [Jean-Claude Bradley: Open Notebook Science](https://istcolloq.gsfc.nasa.gov/jean-claude-bradley/) — NASA Goddard Space Flight Center. research paper; **access:** abstract.

### T035 — Knowledge maps

Knowledge mapping makes important expertise and its connections visible so an organization can identify gaps, bottlenecks, and transfer needs. The APQC overview treats mapping as a facilitated, validated activity rather than simply drawing a diagram.

**Limit:** The public overview describes the approach without establishing its impact; a map becomes misleading when ownership, expertise, or workflows change.

### T036 — Expert directories

An expert directory supports expertise location by helping people identify and contact knowledgeable colleagues. Its practical function is routing a question to a person who can help, rather than storing every answer centrally.

**Limit:** Directory entries can become stale or exaggerate expertise; a successful lookup still depends on availability, willingness, and fit with the question.

**Example situation:** Hypothetically, a distributed nonprofit could maintain opt-in profiles listing languages, grant experience, and contact preferences so a new project can find suitable internal advisers.

**Source:** [Understanding Expertise Location](https://www.apqc.org/resource-library/resource/understanding-expertise-location-0) — APQC. institutional guide; **access:** search excerpt.

### T037 — Open educational resources

Open educational resources combine learning material with public-domain status or permissions that allow reuse, adaptation, and redistribution. The social mechanism is shared permission to improve and circulate teaching material beyond its original classroom.

**Limit:** Free access alone does not make material openly reusable; users must check the actual permissions and the suitability of its content.

**Example situation:** Hypothetically, a volunteer teacher could translate an openly licensed worksheet, replace unfamiliar examples with local ones, and share the adapted version with neighboring classes.

### T038 — Translation networks

Translation networks distribute language tasks among contributors who connect otherwise separated audiences. TWB’s community includes translation, revision, voiceover, and subtitling work, showing that the network can coordinate several complementary forms of language access.

**Limit:** Volunteer participation does not eliminate the need for language competence, review, and contextual understanding, especially where mistranslation carries serious consequences.

**Example situation:** Hypothetically, a regional community network could recruit translators and reviewers to make its meeting invitations understandable in the languages most commonly spoken by local residents.

### T039 — Libraries

Libraries organize shared access to collections, information services, and opportunities for learning. The public-library manifesto places local access, literacy, cultural memory, and participation within a continuing institution rather than an individual purchase of resources.

**Limit:** The manifesto states an institutional mission, not measured outcomes; budgets, collection choices, opening hours, and access conditions shape actual provision.

**Example situation:** Hypothetically, a town could use its library to provide books, internet access, and a regular room for residents learning skills they cannot afford to study privately.

### T044 — Fishbowl discussion

A fishbowl separates active discussion or practice in an inner circle from observation in an outer circle. Rotating participation makes conversational behavior available for reflection while limiting how many people speak at once.

**Limit:** The format structures attention and participation; it does not itself ensure balanced representation or supply a rule for making decisions.

**Example situation:** Hypothetically, facilitator trainees could observe four volunteers handling a difficult discussion, then rotate into the inner circle and compare which interventions changed the conversation.

**Source:** [Communities Care: Training Facilitators](https://www.unicef.org/media/103896/file/Communities-Care-Part-4-Facilitating-Community-Discussions-Training-Facilitators-Manual-2021.pdf) — UNICEF. institutional guide; **access:** search excerpt.

### T047 — Unconferences

An unconference lets participants propose and organize sessions rather than receiving a fully predetermined program. A shared theme and scheduling process turn attendees’ interests into parallel conversations that can range from practical workshops to informal discussion.

**Limit:** Participant control does not remove the need for facilitation and logistics; confidence and familiarity with the format can affect whose sessions happen.

**Example situation:** Hypothetically, local makers could gather for a day, propose the problems they want help with, assemble a session grid, and choose which conversations to join.

**Source:** [Unconferencing: How to Prepare to Attend an Unconference](https://unconference.net/unconferencing-how-to-prepare-to-attend-an-unconference-2/) — Unconference.net / Kaliya. practitioner method; **access:** search excerpt.

### T048 — Delphi process

A Delphi process gathers judgments in repeated rounds and feeds back a summary so participants can reconsider their responses. The design makes convergence, continued disagreement, and reasons for changing judgments available for systematic examination.

**Limit:** Panel agreement is not empirical proof; recruitment, question wording, feedback, and the definition of consensus can materially affect the result.

**Example situation:** Hypothetically, a professional association could ask a panel to prioritize training needs, summarize the first round anonymously, and invite revised judgments with explanations in a second round.

**Source:** [Methodological Guidance for Conducting and Critically Appraising Delphi Panels](https://www.rand.org/content/dam/rand/pubs/tools/TLA3000/TLA3082-1/RAND_TLA3082-1.pdf) — RAND. institutional guide; **access:** search excerpt.

### T049 — Red teams

Red teaming creates a deliberate role for challenging assumptions, plans, and interpretations. The handbook distinguishes adopting a questioning mindset from establishing a separate team, giving organized criticism a place inside planning rather than relying on spontaneous dissent.

**Limit:** Challenge can become ritualized opposition unless decision makers engage with it; the military guide requires adaptation for other organizational settings.

**Example situation:** Hypothetically, a nonprofit could ask colleagues uninvolved in a campaign plan to identify its weakest assumptions and explain how the intended audience might respond differently.

**Source:** [A Guide to Red Teaming](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/a-guide-to-red-teaming) — UK Ministry of Defence. institutional guide; **access:** search excerpt.

### T050 — Premortems

A premortem asks a team to imagine that its proposed plan has already failed and generate plausible reasons. This framing gives participants permission to surface reservations before commitments make them harder to voice.

**Limit:** Imagined causes are hypotheses rather than predictions; the exercise needs follow-up to distinguish manageable risks from vivid but unlikely stories.

**Example situation:** Hypothetically, organizers planning a neighborhood dinner could imagine nobody enjoyed it, list possible reasons independently, and change the invitation and seating plan before sending invitations.

**Source:** [Premortem](https://www.gary-klein.com/premortem) — Gary Klein. practitioner method; **access:** search excerpt.

### T307 — Citizen science

Citizen science organizes public participation in a research process rather than limiting research activity to professional scientists. The toolkit connects scoping, project design, community building, data management, and sustaining the effort as interdependent tasks.

**Limit:** Participation alone does not ensure reliable data or shared authority; project design determines contributors’ roles, quality controls, and access to results.

### T308 — Participatory mapping

Participatory mapping lets community members represent places, resources, and land relationships using their own knowledge. Choices about the map’s purpose, audience, and ownership determine whether the process serves community priorities or external demands.

**Limit:** A published map can expose sensitive places or disputed claims; maps remain selective representations and do not automatically settle ownership.

### T309 — Deliberative polling

Deliberative polling brings a sampled public into informed, facilitated discussion and compares opinions before and after deliberation. Its purpose is to examine considered public judgment under specified conditions, not simply collect immediate reactions.

**Limit:** A deliberative poll is not automatically a binding vote; recruitment, information balance, participation, and question design affect what the results support.

**Example situation:** Hypothetically, a city could recruit a representative sample to examine transport options, question competing experts, discuss tradeoffs, and compare participants’ preferences before and after the event.

**Source:** [What Is Deliberative Polling?](https://deliberation.stanford.edu/what-deliberative-pollingr) — Stanford Deliberative Democracy Lab. practitioner method; **access:** search excerpt.

### T310 — Knowledge fairs

A knowledge fair gathers demonstrations and accounts of participants’ work so visitors can discover practices and potential partners. The World Bank guide presents it as a way to share experience, increase visibility, and build connections across projects.

**Limit:** Showcasing a practice does not establish its effectiveness; fairs need opportunities to question claims and follow up beyond an attractive presentation.

## Belonging and relationships (39)

### T053 — Potlucks

Potlucks distribute the provision of a shared meal among participants rather than placing all preparation on one host. Extension’s guidance treats them as organized food events that still require planning and safe handling.

**Limit:** Sharing preparation does not eliminate dietary, cost, or access barriers; food-handling guidance in this source concerns a particular program and jurisdiction.

**Example situation:** Hypothetically, neighbors could each bring a labeled dish to a monthly courtyard dinner, with organizers coordinating categories so newcomers can join without hosting everyone themselves.

**Source:** [Food Safety and Service: Hosting a Potluck](https://4-h-volunteer.extension.umn.edu/keep-youth-safe/policies-practices/food) — University of Minnesota Extension. institutional guide; **access:** search excerpt.

### T057 — Hospitality hosting

Hospitality hosting creates encounters through an agreed offer of accommodation and time together. Servas makes expectations about length of stay, meals, special needs, communication, and host availability explicit so cultural exchange has a practical structure.

**Limit:** Hospitality remains voluntary and capacity-limited; a host network’s stated goals do not establish that every visit produces trust or friendship.

**Example situation:** Hypothetically, a resident could host a visiting community organizer for two agreed nights, share meals, and compare local projects while clearly discussing privacy and household routines beforehand.

**Source:** [Information for Hosts](https://servas.org/information-hosts) — Servas International. practitioner method; **access:** search excerpt.

### T058 — Buddy systems

A buddy system gives a newcomer an identified peer who helps them settle into a group. Acas places the buddy inside a planned induction that accounts for the person’s experience, work setting, and support needs.

**Limit:** Assigning a name is insufficient if the buddy lacks time or clarity; the source concerns workplace induction rather than every pairing context.

**Example situation:** Hypothetically, a volunteer group could pair each newcomer with an experienced member for their first month, giving them someone to ask about routines before their first shift.

**Source:** [Planning an Induction Programme](https://www.acas.org.uk/inductions/planning-an-induction-programme) — Acas. institutional guide; **access:** search excerpt.

### T061 — Trial membership

Trial membership creates a defined period for prospective and existing members to experience living or working together before a fuller commitment. Apple Tree House describes a six-month trial with repeated membership check-ins before cooperative share purchase.

**Limit:** This is one community’s reported practice, not a universal standard; unclear evaluation or exit conditions can make a trial insecure.

**Example situation:** Hypothetically, a shared workshop could offer prospective members a three-month trial with agreed access, dues, and check-ins before either side commits to long-term membership.

**Source:** [Apple Tree House: How to Join](https://www.ic.org/directory/community/apple-tree-house/) — Foundation for Intentional Community / Apple Tree House. practitioner method; **access:** search excerpt.

### T062 — Introductions by a connector

A connector uses an existing relationship to introduce people who may benefit from meeting. The guide specifies checking willingness, explaining the purpose, and sharing contact details with permission, making access an agreed bridge rather than an assumed entitlement.

**Limit:** A mutual contact cannot guarantee trust or cooperation; consent and a clear purpose matter more than merely possessing someone’s contact details.

**Example situation:** Hypothetically, a librarian could ask a new resident and a local walking-group organizer whether they would welcome an introduction, then connect them with a short explanation.

**Source:** [Organizing for Effective Advocacy: Introductions](https://ctb.ku.edu/en/book/export/html/994) — University of Kansas Community Tool Box. institutional guide; **access:** search excerpt.

### T064 — Listening pairs

Listening pairs allocate each person a turn to speak while the other listens. The speaker can choose silence, a summary, or follow-up questions, separating attention to their experience from the listener’s impulse to contribute their own story.

**Limit:** The exercise structures listening but is not therapy or compulsory disclosure; participants need permission to choose what they share.

A public forum gathers people around a shared issue, using facilitation and recorded discussion to surface experiences, concerns, and possible responses. It creates a visible place for community input before subsequent decisions.

References obtain information from people familiar with a candidate's past work. OPM recommends structured questions tied to job requirements and consistent recording, turning informal endorsements into more comparable evidence for a selection decision.

**Limit:** Referees may be selective, reluctant, or biased; consistent questions improve comparability but cannot make every account complete or independent.

**Example situation:** Imagine a hiring panel asking each candidate's former collaborators the same questions about relevant responsibilities, recording concrete examples rather than simply counting enthusiastic recommendations.

**Source:** [Reference Checking](https://www.opm.gov/policy-data-oversight/assessment-and-selection/other-assessment-methods/reference-checking/) — U.S. Office of Personnel Management. institutional guide; **access:** search excerpt.

### T277 — Reputation systems

Reputation systems collect and communicate information about past interactions so unfamiliar participants can judge whom to trust. The research describes online systems as a way to carry reputational information beyond direct personal acquaintance.

**Limit:** Feedback can be manipulated or biased toward visible participants; a score may conceal context, changing behavior, or people excluded from participation.

**Example situation:** Imagine an equipment-sharing network displaying completed loans and contextual feedback, allowing disputes to be reviewed instead of reducing every borrower to an unexplained number.

**Source:** [Reputation Systems](https://cs.uwaterloo.ca/~klarson/teaching/F04-886/papers/p45-resnick.pdf) — Communications of the ACM; Resnick, Zeckhauser, Friedman, Kuwabara. research paper; **access:** search excerpt.

### T278 — Contribution records

Contribution records preserve traces of participation for later inspection. GitHub's profile documentation illustrates timelines of commits, pull requests, and issues, while distinguishing visible public activity from private work whose details may remain hidden.

**Limit:** Recorded activity is not equivalent to impact or quality; private work, maintenance, mentoring, and excluded contribution types may be underrepresented.

**Example situation:** Imagine a volunteer project reviewing a person's documented contributions before offering responsibilities, examining the work itself and asking about important contributions that its tracking system missed.

**Source:** [Contributions on your profile](https://docs.github.com/en/account-and-profile/concepts/contributions-on-your-profile) — GitHub Docs. institutional guide; **access:** search excerpt.

### T280 — Peer nominations

Peer nominations ask knowledgeable others to put a person forward for recognition. ACM's guidance emphasizes evidence matched to award criteria and relevant endorsements, making nomination a structured advocacy process rather than automatic selection.

**Limit:** Strong networks affect who gets nominated; polished endorsements can overshadow less connected contributors unless nomination access and evaluation criteria receive attention.

**Example situation:** Imagine a volunteer association inviting members to nominate overlooked contributors, asking for concrete examples and disclosing relationships before a separate panel reviews the submissions.

**Source:** [Advice for Award Nominators and Endorsers](https://awards.acm.org/binaries/content/assets/awards/advice-for-award-nominators-and-endorsers_07-2025.pdf) — Association for Computing Machinery. institutional guide; **access:** search excerpt.

### T282 — Contributor credits

Contributor credits publicly acknowledge people and the kinds of work they provided. The All Contributors specification uses prominent contributor lists and contribution categories to recognize work that conventional code-focused records can overlook.

**Limit:** Credit categories can still omit invisible labor; acknowledgement does not itself confer payment, decision rights, or agreement about relative contribution.

**Example situation:** Imagine an open-source project crediting documentation, accessibility testing, event organization, and code contributions together, inviting contributors to correct omissions before each release is published.

**Source:** [All Contributors Specification](https://allcontributors.org/en/reference/specification/) — All Contributors. practitioner method; **access:** search excerpt.

### T283 — Authorship conventions

Authorship conventions specify who qualifies for a byline and what responsibility accompanies it. ICMJE distinguishes authors from other acknowledged contributors through combined criteria, connecting recognition with participation, approval, and accountability for the work.

**Limit:** Disciplinary conventions differ; formal criteria cannot settle disputes without transparent discussion, and seniority or funding alone does not establish authorship.

**Example situation:** Imagine a research team discussing authorship roles before writing begins, revisiting them as contributions change and acknowledging substantial help that does not meet its agreed criteria.

**Source:** [Defining the Role of Authors and Contributors](https://www.icmje.org/recommendations/browse/roles-and-responsibilities/defining-the-role-of-authors-and-contributors.html) — International Committee of Medical Journal Editors. institutional guide; **access:** search excerpt.

### T284 — Merit-based selection

Merit-based selection defines advancement around relevant abilities, knowledge, and skills assessed through open competition. The cited civil-service principles frame this as a procedural commitment to fair opportunity, rather than deference to political allegiance.

**Limit:** What counts as merit remains a design choice; credentials and test scores can reflect unequal opportunity rather than relevant demonstrated capability.

**Example situation:** Imagine a cooperative recruiting an operations lead, defining necessary tasks and evaluating applicants against the same evidence criteria instead of choosing the best-connected member.

**Source:** [Merit System Principles](https://www.opm.gov/chcoc/transmittals/2020/attachments/2020%20Appointments%20and%20Awards%20Guidance%20Attachments.pdf) — U.S. Office of Personnel Management. institutional guide; **access:** search excerpt.

### T285 — Seniority rules

Seniority rules establish priority using time in a role or another specified service measure. The cited public-service orders illustrate formal ordering by promotion date, showing how an explicit rule can structure otherwise discretionary comparisons.

**Limit:** Different systems calculate seniority differently; length of service does not necessarily measure present capability or justify every allocation of opportunity.

**Example situation:** Imagine a volunteer venue allocating preferred shifts by a published service-length rule, debating exceptions for accessibility and whether newer members should receive a minimum share.

**Source:** [Public Service Orders: Seniority on Promotion](https://natlex.ilo.org/dyn/natlex2/natlex2/files/download/86499/SYC86499.pdf) — Government of Seychelles; ILO NATLEX archive. reference; **access:** search excerpt.

### T286 — Competitive examinations

Competitive examinations compare candidates through a defined assessment process for entry into positions or candidate pools. The UN program combines an entrance examination with subsequent professional development, making examination one stage within recruitment.

**Limit:** Exam performance captures only selected abilities; eligibility restrictions, preparation access, and later appointment processes limit what passing an examination establishes.

**Example situation:** Imagine a public organization recruiting analysts through a published examination, checking that the exercises resemble relevant work and explaining what successful candidates can expect afterward.

**Source:** [Young Professionals Programme](https://careers.un.org/ypp) — United Nations Careers. institutional guide; **access:** search excerpt.

### T287 — Work-sample assessments

Work-sample assessments ask candidates to perform tasks resembling the work being selected for. OPM describes simulations and exercises that provide direct evidence of relevant behavior, rather than inferring competence solely from background information.

**Limit:** Work samples can unfairly test skills intended to be taught after entry; realistic tasks also require careful design and consistent assessment.

**Example situation:** Imagine a nonprofit hiring an event coordinator by asking applicants to resolve the same fictional scheduling conflict, using a common rubric and a reasonable time limit.

**Source:** [Work Samples and Simulations](https://www.opm.gov/policy-data-oversight/assessment-and-selection/other-assessment-methods/work-samples-and-simulations/) — U.S. Office of Personnel Management. institutional guide; **access:** search excerpt.

### T288 — Auditions

Auditions evaluate performed ability through specified demonstrations before selectors. The Tanglewood chorus example combines prepared music, rhythm, scales, and diction, showing how a short performance can sample several requirements of participation.

**Limit:** Brief performances are sensitive to nerves and preparation access; selectors must decide which qualities the audition can actually reveal.

**Example situation:** Imagine a community ensemble inviting singers to prepare the same excerpt and a personal selection, explaining criteria and accommodation options before assigning audition times.

**Source:** [Tanglewood Festival Chorus Auditions](https://www.bso.org/tfc/auditions) — Boston Symphony Orchestra. institutional guide; **access:** page.

### T289 — Trial projects

Trial projects let both sides experience a bounded piece of working together before a longer commitment. Automattic describes paid, part-time trials used to evaluate work, communication, effectiveness, and mutual compatibility.

**Limit:** Trials can burden applicants with existing obligations; clear payment, scope, evaluation, and ownership arrangements are necessary to avoid extracting unpaid work.

**Example situation:** Imagine a small design studio commissioning a paid, limited trial project, agreeing on hours and evaluation criteria so the candidate can also assess collaboration.

**Source:** [How We Hire Developers](https://automattic.com/work-with-us/how-we-hire-developers/) — Automattic. practitioner method; **access:** search excerpt.

### T290 — Blind review

Blind review withholds selected identity information from evaluators while they assess work. Nature Health's double-anonymous option requires authors to conceal identifying material, making anonymity an operational preparation task rather than simply a promise.

**Limit:** Subject matter, references, or public drafts can reveal identity; anonymity does not remove all bias or substitute for relevant evaluation criteria.

**Example situation:** Imagine a writing competition removing names and biographical details before judging, asking entrants to flag identifying content and documenting how reviewers handle accidental recognition.

**Source:** [Double-blind peer review](https://www.nature.com/naturehealth/submission-guidelines/dbpr) — Nature Health. institutional guide; **access:** search excerpt.

### T292 — Sponsorship for advancement

Sponsorship for advancement involves an influential person advocating for someone else's opportunities. Catalyst distinguishes this relationship from guidance alone, focusing on access to networks and people willing to support another person's progression.

**Limit:** Sponsorship can reproduce favoritism when access is informal; transparent opportunities and scrutiny of whose talent receives advocacy remain necessary.

**Example situation:** Imagine a senior colleague putting a capable junior forward for a visible project, explaining the relevant evidence and ensuring others also have routes to consideration.

**Source:** [Catalyst Sponsorship Guide](https://www.catalyst.org/en-us/insights/2025/catalyst-sponsorship-guide) — Catalyst. institutional guide; **access:** search excerpt.

### T294 — Deputy roles

Deputy roles distribute leadership responsibilities and provide continuity when a primary leader is unavailable or leaves. Bridgespan's COO account describes interim leadership and preserved relationships without assuming that every deputy becomes the permanent successor.

**Limit:** Unclear authority can create competing leadership centers; a deputy's operational effectiveness does not automatically make them the appropriate next leader.

**Example situation:** Imagine a community center designating a deputy to handle defined decisions during the director's absence, documenting escalation limits and separately planning any permanent succession.

**Source:** [Creating the COO Position](https://www.bridgespan.org/getmedia/c60187c0-f6b7-4019-bd32-53b8872ae283/CreatingCOOPosition.pdf?ext=.pdf) — The Bridgespan Group. institutional guide; **access:** search excerpt.

### T295 — Overlapping handovers

Overlapping handovers create direct contact between outgoing and incoming responsibility holders. HSE's account combines preparation, exchange of task-relevant information, and incoming cross-checking, showing why a document alone may miss unresolved questions.

**Limit:** Overlap requires time and clear responsibility boundaries; conversation without verification can transmit misunderstandings as easily as useful tacit knowledge.

**Example situation:** Imagine an outgoing volunteer treasurer working alongside the replacement for one reporting cycle, reviewing outstanding payments together and confirming exactly when responsibility transfers.

**Source:** [Shift handover](https://www.hse.gov.uk/humanfactors/topics/shift-handover.htm) — UK Health and Safety Executive. institutional guide; **access:** search excerpt.

### T296 — Graduated responsibility

Graduated responsibility increases autonomy as demonstrated capability develops. ACGME's supervision framework links responsibility to assessed competence and the circumstances of the task, rather than advancing everyone solely according to time served.

**Limit:** The clinical example is not general permission to delegate risky tasks; supervision must reflect actual ability and consequences of error.

**Example situation:** Imagine a new event volunteer first observing bookings, then managing routine requests with review, and finally handling them independently after demonstrating reliable judgment.

**Source:** [Common Program Requirements FAQs](https://prod2.acgme.org/globalassets/pdfs/faq/common-program-requirements-faqs.pdf) — Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education. institutional guide; **access:** search excerpt.

### T297 — Leadership pipelines

Leadership pipelines develop a pool of potential successors before vacancies arise. OPM's framework connects future organizational needs, identified competencies, and development opportunities, treating continuity as an ongoing process rather than emergency replacement.

**Limit:** Early labels can entrench favorites and exclude late developers; a pipeline should not imply guaranteed promotion or a closed succession list.

**Example situation:** Imagine a growing association offering several volunteers progressively broader responsibilities, reviewing future needs and keeping leadership opportunities open beyond the initial development cohort.

**Source:** [Succession Planning Process](https://www.opm.gov/policy-data-oversight/human-capital-framework/reference-materials/leadership-knowledge-management/successionplanning/) — U.S. Office of Personnel Management. institutional guide; **access:** search excerpt.

### T298 — Founder transition agreements

Founder transition agreements clarify what a departing founder may continue doing and what passes to successors. INTRAC emphasizes board responsibility and explicit boundaries when founders remain involved, reducing ambiguity around continuing influence.

**Limit:** A written boundary cannot ensure compliance; successor authority and independent board oversight matter when a founder retains strong informal relationships.

**Example situation:** Imagine a nonprofit founder remaining available for donor introductions after stepping down, with the board specifying reporting relationships, decision limits, and a review date.

**Source:** [Leadership Transition: Overcoming the Threat of Founder's Syndrome](https://www.intrac.org/app/uploads/2024/12/Praxis-Series-Paper-No-9-Leadership-Transition-Overcoming-the-Threat-of-Founders-Syndrome.pdf) — INTRAC. institutional guide; **access:** search excerpt.

### T299 — Emeritus roles

Emeritus roles distinguish past contribution from current active participation. Apache's published process lets members move into emeritus status and describes a route back through election, making the status a defined governance category.

**Limit:** Emeritus titles differ across institutions; honorary recognition must not leave unclear whether former leaders retain votes, access, or operational authority.

**Example situation:** Imagine a long-serving association organizer stepping into an honorary advisory role, with members documenting which responsibilities end and how any future return would be decided.

**Source:** [ASF Members: Emeritus Members](https://www.apache.org/foundation/members) — Apache Software Foundation. institutional guide; **access:** search excerpt.

### T300 — Exit interviews

Exit interviews ask departing participants about their experience and reasons for leaving. Glasgow's guidance explores role expectations and communication, turning departure into a chance to identify conditions that current participants may hesitate to discuss.

**Limit:** Departing people may still fear consequences or withhold criticism; collecting feedback is useful only if patterns receive credible follow-up.

**Example situation:** Imagine a volunteer coordinator offering a departing organizer a private conversation about workload and collaboration, explaining who will see the notes and how recurring issues will be addressed.

**Source:** [Exit Interviews](https://www.gla.ac.uk/myglasgow/pod/mgrs-admin/mgr-guidance/leaverguidance/exit-int/) — University of Glasgow. institutional guide; **access:** search excerpt.

### T301 — Institutional memory interviews

Institutional memory interviews preserve participants' accounts of how an organization worked and what they learned. NASA's oral history project includes people in many roles, capturing experience that formal project records may not convey.

**Limit:** Recollections are selective and can conflict; interviews need consent, contextual records, and multiple perspectives rather than being treated as unquestionable institutional truth.

**Example situation:** Imagine a neighborhood association interviewing longtime organizers about failed campaigns and turning points, archiving recordings with permission alongside documents that help later members assess the accounts.

**Source:** [Johnson Space Center Oral History Project](https://history.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/static/history/alsj/JSC-OralHist.html) — NASA. history; **access:** search excerpt.

### T302 — Backup ownership

Backup ownership gives more than one trusted person the access needed to keep a shared project operating. The Open Source Guides recommend organizational accounts and a backup administrator to reduce dependence on one individual.

**Limit:** Additional access creates security and accountability responsibilities; a nominal backup is ineffective if permissions, knowledge, or availability are never checked.

**Example situation:** Imagine a volunteer website adding a second authorized administrator, testing recovery access and documenting renewal responsibilities before the original maintainer leaves for several months.

**Source:** [Leadership and Governance](https://opensource.guide/pcm/leadership-and-governance/) — GitHub Open Source Guides. institutional guide; **access:** search excerpt.

### T303 — Stewardship terms

Stewardship terms specify how long a person holds entrusted authority and whether renewal is possible. Wikimedia's trustee description illustrates fixed renewable terms, making duration and continuation explicit parts of institutional governance.

**Limit:** Term limits can refresh leadership but also remove experience; duration alone does not establish accountability during a person's time in office.

**Example situation:** Imagine a community land organization giving trustees fixed terms, scheduling staggered renewals and documenting handovers so leadership changes do not leave essential responsibilities unattended.

**Source:** [Our 2026 Trustee Search](https://wikimediafoundation.org/our-2026-trustee-search/) — Wikimedia Foundation. institutional guide; **access:** search excerpt.

### T305 — Lineage recognition

Lineage recognition locates a person's recognized role within a chain of teachers and predecessors. San Francisco Zen Center's account names a teacher's Dharma transmission and lineage, making continuity part of how authority is described.

**Limit:** Lineage recognition has tradition-specific meanings; it is neither a universal competence test nor a guarantee that a recognized teacher behaves responsibly.

**Example situation:** Imagine a practitioner choosing a study community and asking how it understands teacher lineage, while separately examining teaching conduct, accountability arrangements, and suitability for their needs.

**Source:** [Steve Weintraub](https://www.sfzc.org/teachers/steve-weintraub/) — San Francisco Zen Center. history; **access:** search excerpt.

### T306 — Oaths of office

Oaths of office mark entry into a role through an explicit commitment to its duties or governing order. The Senate's history shows an institutionalized promise whose wording has changed with political circumstances.

**Limit:** A solemn promise cannot enforce itself; accountability still depends on institutions, review, and consequences when the officeholder violates their duties.

**Example situation:** Imagine a newly elected association board publicly affirming its responsibilities and conflict rules at the start of a term, while explaining the mechanisms members can use to challenge misconduct.

**Source:** [Oath of Office](https://www.senate.gov/about/origins-foundations/senate-and-constitution/oath-of-office.htm) — United States Senate. history; **access:** search excerpt.

### T337 — Peer juries for awards

Peer juries assess submissions using relevant experience and stated criteria. The NEA process illustrates rotating, conflict-free advisory panels whose recommendations pass through further decision stages, distinguishing expert assessment from final award authority.

**Limit:** Peer judgment can reproduce disciplinary preferences; conflict rules, varied perspectives, and clarity about final authority remain necessary even with experienced reviewers.

**Example situation:** Imagine a local arts fund appointing a rotating panel of practitioners and community members, disclosing conflicts and recording how published criteria guide its recommendations.

**Source:** [Grant Review Process](https://www.arts.gov/grants/grant-review-process) — National Endowment for the Arts. institutional guide; **access:** search excerpt.

### T338 — Recallable stewardship

Recallable stewardship makes continued authority conditional on review rather than permanent possession. Wikimedia stewards undergo community comment and steward deliberation, with defined procedures for removing access when continuation is no longer supported.

**Limit:** This example combines community input with peer deliberation rather than direct popular recall; effective accountability depends on accessible evidence and fair procedures.

**Example situation:** Imagine a community platform periodically reviewing administrators' use of permissions, allowing documented concerns and specifying who can suspend or remove access after a fair review.

**Source:** [Stewards: Confirmations and Removal of Access](https://meta.wikimedia.org/wiki/Stewards) — Wikimedia community, Meta-Wiki. practitioner method; **access:** page.

## Systems and institutions (5)

### S002 — Socialism

Socialism covers several proposals for social or worker control of production. Ownership, workplace authority, and allocation are separate design questions: some versions retain markets, while others propose central or participatory planning.

**Limit:** State ownership alone does not establish worker control. This is a family of contested institutional proposals, not one uniform economic design.

**Example situation:** Imagine a town debating whether its energy supplier should be privately owned, municipally owned, or controlled by workers and residents. Ask who actually controls investment and receives the surplus.

**Source:** [Socialism](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/socialism/) — Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy. reference; **access:** page sections reviewed.

### S003 — Economic planning

Economic planning coordinates production and resource allocation through explicit plans. Central planning concentrates decisions in an authority; participatory proposals distribute deliberation among worker and consumer councils, with repeated attempts to reconcile production and demand.

**Limit:** Information, incentives, and revision matter in either design. Planning within one organization is not equivalent to planning an entire economy.

**Example situation:** Imagine a region coordinating housing, transport, and electricity expansion. Compare who sets priorities, how local needs enter the plan, and what happens when forecasts prove wrong.

**Source:** [Socialism, section 4.1: Central and Participatory Planning](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/socialism/#CentPartPlan) — Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy. reference; **access:** page section reviewed.

### S007 — Technocracy

Technocracy gives expertise a central claim to governing authority. Distinguish experts advising elected decision makers from experts controlling decisions themselves: technical knowledge and the authority to choose public goals are different things.

**Limit:** Expertise cannot by itself settle whose interests count. The source is a review of expertise and democracy, not an endorsement of expert rule.

**Example situation:** Imagine a transport board selecting a route. Engineers can estimate capacity and cost, but deciding which neighborhoods benefit raises questions of representation and accountability.

**Source:** [Expert Knowledge in Democracies: Promises, Limits, and Conflict](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-polisci-033123-020420) — Annual Review of Political Science. research review; **access:** search excerpt.

### S008 — Monarchy

Monarchy places a monarch at the head of a state, but the office does not specify how much governing power its holder has. The British constitutional model separates the monarch’s role from parliamentary lawmaking.

**Limit:** This institutional self-description concerns the United Kingdom. It cannot stand in for absolute monarchies or establish monarchy’s comparative benefits.

**Example situation:** Imagine comparing two constitutions that both name a king. Examine who can make laws, appoint governments, and overrule decisions before treating their political systems as equivalent.

**Source:** [The role of the Monarchy](https://www.royal.uk/the-role-of-the-monarchy) — The Royal Household. institutional account; **access:** page reviewed.

### S028 — Polycentric governance

Polycentric governance combines multiple centers of decision making at different scales. Ostrom proposes local, regional, and national experimentation linked by information, monitoring, and learning, rather than relying entirely on one global authority.

**Limit:** Multiple authorities still need coordination and accountability. The paper develops an approach to climate governance, not proof that decentralization always works.

**Example situation:** Imagine neighboring towns sharing a watershed. Each manages local runoff while a basin council coordinates monitoring and resolves problems that cross municipal borders.

**Source:** [A Polycentric Approach for Coping with Climate Change](https://dlc.dlib.indiana.edu/dlc/items/ce7cf738-5200-46b4-8376-921914080e19) — Elinor Ostrom / World Bank; Indiana University repository. research working paper; **access:** abstract reviewed.

## Social dynamics (19)

### D014 — Homophily

Homophily is the tendency for similar people to be connected. Shared settings, geography, existing relationships, and the persistence of ties can all contribute, shaping whose information and opportunities reach whom.

**Limit:** Similarity in a network does not by itself show deliberate exclusion. Opportunity to meet and preferences about relationships must be distinguished.

**Example situation:** Imagine recruiting a new club entirely through members’ friends. The invitations travel easily, but inspecting who hears about the club reveals whole parts of the neighborhood missing.

**Source:** [Birds of a feather: Homophily in social networks](https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/652661) — McPherson, Smith-Lovin and Cook / Annual Review of Sociology. research review; **access:** abstract reviewed.

### D015 — Reciprocity

Reciprocity connects helping with expectations about later responses. In direct reciprocity a partner can return help; in indirect reciprocity, reputation can influence how other people treat someone who helped or refused to help.

**Limit:** Theoretical mechanisms for cooperation do not guarantee that a favor will be returned. Obligations and reputations can also become unequal or coercive.

**Example situation:** Imagine neighbors lending tools. Some return favors directly, while others help a third neighbor because they know the original lender has been generous.

**Source:** [Evolution of indirect reciprocity](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16251955/) — Nowak and Sigmund / Nature. research review; **access:** abstract in search results.

### D016 — Trust formation and repair

A foundational model distinguishes perceived ability, benevolence, and integrity as contributors to trust. Repair then depends partly on what went wrong: two studies found that responses and later evidence affected trust differently after alleged competence and integrity violations.

**Limit:** These studies do not justify dishonest denial or promise relationship repair. Trust formation and repair require more than choosing an effective statement.

**Example situation:** Imagine a collaborator missing a deadline. Find out whether people doubt their ability, believe they concealed a problem, or both; a new schedule alone may not address the actual concern.

**Source:** [Removing the Shadow of Suspicion: The Effects of Apology Versus Denial for Repairing Competence- Versus Integrity-Based Trust Violations](https://ink.library.smu.edu.sg/lkcsb_research/2368/) — Kim, Ferrin, Cooper and Dirks / Journal of Applied Psychology. research paper; **access:** abstract reviewed.

**Source:** [An Integrative Model of Organizational Trust](https://journals.aom.org/doi/10.5465/amr.1995.9508080335) — Mayer, Davis and Schoorman / Academy of Management Review. theoretical research paper; **access:** search excerpt.

### D017 — Free riding

Free riding occurs when someone benefits from a shared contribution without contributing correspondingly. In public-goods experiments, participants sometimes paid to punish low contributors, and the availability of punishment changed cooperation.

**Limit:** Laboratory findings do not establish that punishment is the right response in a community. Inability to contribute is different from strategic non-contribution.

**Example situation:** Imagine a shared garden where everyone can take vegetables but few people water it. Examine access, ability, and expectations before concluding that absent volunteers are exploiting the others.

**Source:** [Cooperation and Punishment in Public Goods Experiments](https://ideas.repec.org/a/aea/aecrev/v90y2000i4p980-994.html) — Fehr and Gächter / American Economic Review; RePEc abstract. research paper; **access:** abstract reviewed.

### D018 — Social loafing

Social loafing describes reduced individual effort when work is performed collectively. Experiments using shouting and clapping separated motivational losses from simple coordination difficulties by varying what participants believed about the group around them.

**Limit:** Small laboratory tasks are not every kind of teamwork. Reduced output can also result from coordination costs, inaccessible tasks, or unclear responsibilities.

**Example situation:** Imagine five people assigned to prepare a meeting, with no named responsibilities. Nobody starts the agenda because each expects the others to handle it.

**Source:** [Many Hands Make Light the Work: The Causes and Consequences of Social Loafing](https://web.mit.edu/curhan/www/docs/Articles/15341_Readings/Group_Dynamics/Latane_et_al_1979_Many_hands_make_light_the_work.pdf) — Latané, Williams and Harkins / Journal of Personality and Social Psychology. research paper; **access:** PDF sections reviewed.

### D019 — Volunteer burnout

Volunteer exhaustion can be connected to how participation is organized. Interviews and surveys with 64 former volunteers identified overload alongside limited voice, cliques, disconnection, and loss of confidence in leadership among reasons for leaving.

**Limit:** This study reports former volunteers’ accounts in one organizational setting. It does not establish a diagnosis or a universal causal explanation for departure.

**Example situation:** Imagine a neighborhood organizer who increasingly covers every unfilled shift. Before asking for more commitment, examine workload, decision power, backup coverage, and whether stepping back is genuinely possible.

**Source:** [Why People Leave Community Service Organizations: A Mixed Methods Study](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11266-018-0005-z) — Milbourn, Black and Buchanan / Voluntas. research paper; **access:** abstract reviewed.

### D020 — Status hierarchies

Status concerns the respect and deference people receive from others. A laboratory experiment and national survey in this paper suggest that people can value their relative standing, not simply the absolute amount of respect they receive.

**Limit:** Status is not identical to wealth or formal office. Findings from these studies do not imply that everyone pursues rank in every setting.

**Example situation:** Imagine a project with equal voting rights where everyone waits for one veteran to speak. Map whose judgment carries weight alongside the formal organization chart.

**Source:** [Striving for superiority: The human desire for status](https://irle.berkeley.edu/publications/working-papers/striving-for-superiority-the-human-desire-for-status/) — Anderson and Hildreth / UC Berkeley IRLE. research working paper; **access:** abstract reviewed.

### D021 — Preference falsification

Preference falsification means publicly expressing a preference different from one’s private preference under perceived social pressure. Kuran uses the concept to explain how visible consensus can conceal dissent and sometimes change abruptly.

**Limit:** This is an explanatory model, not a license to assume people are lying. Silence, uncertainty, and mistaken beliefs about others are distinct phenomena.

**Example situation:** Imagine every team member praising a plan in the meeting while privately wanting it changed. A confidential discussion may reveal disagreement that the public show of support concealed.

**Source:** [Rationally Speaking 198: Timur Kuran on Private Truths and Public Lies](https://sites.duke.edu/timurkuran/files/2018/03/rs198transcript-Kuran-Rationall-Speaking-2017.pdf) — Timur Kuran / Rationally Speaking; Duke author archive. author interview; **access:** PDF sections reviewed.

### D022 — Pluralistic ignorance

Pluralistic ignorance arises when people misread others’ private views and treat the apparent group norm as real. In four campus studies, students judged other students more comfortable with drinking practices than they themselves were.

**Limit:** The studies concern a particular campus context. A mistaken belief about others differs from deliberately misrepresenting one’s own preference.

**Example situation:** Imagine a group where everyone dislikes late-night messages but assumes everyone else expects immediate replies. Comparing private preferences could expose a norm nobody actually wants.

**Source:** [Pluralistic Ignorance and Alcohol Use on Campus: Some Consequences of Misperceiving the Social Norm](https://collaborate.princeton.edu/en/publications/pluralistic-ignorance-and-alcohol-use-on-campus-some-consequences/) — Prentice and Miller / Journal of Personality and Social Psychology. research paper; **access:** abstract in search results.

### D023 — Group polarization

Group polarization describes discussion moving a group toward a more extreme version of its initial tendency. Sunstein examines this as a risk for deliberation: agreement after talking need not mean the group considered the full range of views.

**Limit:** This is a tendency under particular conditions, not an inevitable consequence of discussion or a reason to treat all agreement as suspicious.

**Example situation:** Imagine a campaign group whose members already favor escalation. Compare privately stated positions before and after discussion, and examine which alternatives never entered the room.

**Source:** [On a Danger of Deliberative Democracy](https://hls.harvard.edu/bibliography/on-a-danger-of-deliberative-democracy/) — Cass Sunstein / Harvard Law School bibliography. research paper; **access:** abstract in search results.

### D024 — Groupthink

Groupthink is a lens for examining pressure toward agreement and failures to scrutinize decisions. A reanalysis of classic historical cases using newer records challenges parts of the original explanation, including its emphasis on group cohesion.

**Limit:** A bad decision or a cohesive team is not sufficient evidence of groupthink. The causal account and retrospective diagnosis are contested.

**Example situation:** Imagine a committee dismissing objections as disloyalty. Investigate what happened to contrary evidence and who could challenge the proposal, rather than labeling the group from the outcome alone.

**Source:** [Revisiting the Bay of Pigs and Vietnam Decisions 25 Years Later: How Well Has the Groupthink Hypothesis Stood the Test of Time?](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9705804/) — Kramer / Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes. research paper; **access:** abstract in search results.

### D025 — Goal displacement

Goal displacement happens when a means becomes an end. Merton describes how devotion to procedures can make rule-following more important than the purpose those procedures originally served, particularly when unusual cases require adaptation.

**Limit:** Rules can protect consistency and restrain arbitrary power. This critique does not show that abandoning procedure will serve the original purpose better.

**Example situation:** Imagine an aid organization rejecting every application missing one document, even when the document cannot be obtained after a disaster. Compliance has crowded out the reason the process exists.

**Source:** [Bureaucratic Structure and Personality](https://www.d.umn.edu/cla/faculty/jhamlin/4111/Merton/Social%20Theory%20and%20Social%20Structure%20Bureaucratic%20Structure%20a.htm) — Robert K. Merton; University of Minnesota Duluth teaching archive. theoretical research essay; **access:** page reviewed.

### D026 — Metric gaming

Metric gaming is one way a measured target can diverge from the underlying goal. The paper distinguishes several failures of optimizing proxies; not every failure requires deliberate cheating, even when the measured score improves.

**Limit:** This is a conceptual taxonomy, not a universal empirical law. Deliberate gaming should be distinguished from noisy measurement and unintended proxy failures.

**Example situation:** Imagine a help desk rewarded for closing tickets. If staff close unresolved requests to improve the count, investigate the actual customer outcome alongside the dashboard.

**Source:** [Categorizing Variants of Goodhart’s Law](https://arxiv.org/abs/1803.04585) — Manheim and Garrabrant / arXiv. theoretical preprint; **access:** abstract reviewed.

### D027 — Capture

Capture describes an institution being steered toward the interests it is supposed to oversee. Stigler’s theory examines how organized industries may obtain regulation that benefits them, instead of treating regulation as automatically serving a general public interest.

**Limit:** This is a theory of regulatory incentives, not evidence that every regulator is captured. Consulting affected stakeholders alone does not establish capture.

**Example situation:** Imagine an industry helping draft licensing rules that burden new entrants while exempting established firms. Follow who shaped the rule and who gains from it.

**Source:** [The Theory of Economic Regulation](https://www.rasmusen.org/zg601/readings/Stigler.1971.pdf) — George Stigler / Bell Journal of Economics and Management Science. theoretical research paper; **access:** PDF search excerpt.

### D028 — Principal-agent problems

A principal-agent problem arises when someone delegates authority to another whose interests may differ. Jensen and Meckling analyze incentives, monitoring, commitments, and the remaining losses when delegation cannot perfectly align those interests.

**Limit:** The model does not assume every delegate is dishonest. Monitoring has costs, and the principal’s objectives may themselves be disputed.

**Example situation:** Imagine members hiring a manager for a shared workspace. Members want affordable access while the manager is rewarded only for revenue; the arrangement may encourage decisions members dislike.

**Source:** [Theory of the Firm: Managerial Behavior, Agency Costs and Ownership Structure](https://doi.org/10.1016/0304-405X%2876%2990026-X) — Jensen and Meckling / Journal of Financial Economics. theoretical research paper; **access:** abstract and primary-paper search excerpt.

### D029 — Path dependence

Path dependence means that the sequence of earlier events helps shape later possibilities. Arthur’s model shows how increasing returns to adoption can amplify early contingencies and make a technological path difficult to leave.

**Limit:** This is a model with particular assumptions. An institution having a history does not by itself demonstrate lock-in or prove that change is impossible.

**Example situation:** Imagine an organization retaining awkward software because its training, archives, and partners all depend on it. The original choice now shapes the cost of every alternative.

**Source:** [Competing Technologies, Increasing Returns, and Lock-In by Historical Events](https://ideas.repec.org/a/ecj/econjl/v99y1989i394p116-31.html) — W. Brian Arthur / Economic Journal; RePEc abstract. theoretical research paper; **access:** abstract in search results.

### D030 — Norm drift

Norm drift is used here as an editorial label for changing informal expectations. Related experiments on convention formation show that network structure can affect how shared conventions emerge without a central organizer imposing agreement.

**Limit:** The experiment concerns simple conventions, not every moral or organizational norm. It supports a related mechanism rather than validating a general theory called norm drift.

**Example situation:** Imagine a discussion group whose written rule promises quick replies, but whose members gradually treat a week as normal. Compare the formal rule with expectations expressed in everyday interactions.

**Source:** [The spontaneous emergence of conventions: An experimental study of cultural evolution](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4343158/) — Centola and Baronchelli / PNAS. research paper; **access:** abstract and article sections reviewed.

### D031 — Network brokerage

Network brokers connect people across gaps between groups. Burt argues that these connections expose people to different approaches; a study of managers in an electronics company found brokerage associated with ideas judged valuable and several career advantages.

**Limit:** The reported associations do not establish that brokerage alone caused better ideas. Brokers can also concentrate access and become bottlenecks.

**Example situation:** Imagine a librarian connecting a repair club with a school that needs equipment fixed. The useful contribution is seeing an opportunity that neither group could see from its own network.

**Source:** [Structural Holes and Good Ideas](https://doi.org/10.1086/421787) — Ronald Burt / American Journal of Sociology. research paper; **access:** abstract in search results.

### D032 — Boundary maintenance

Boundary maintenance concerns how groups distinguish members, outsiders, and legitimate participation. Lamont and Molnár review boundaries across identity, inequality, professions, and communities, emphasizing relations between symbolic distinctions and social divisions.

**Limit:** The review supplies an analytical lens, not a justification for exclusion. Formal access and informal recognition should be examined separately.

**Example situation:** Imagine a supposedly open professional meetup where only established members understand the jargon or receive introductions. The invitation is public, but everyday interactions still mark who belongs.

**Source:** [The Study of Boundaries in the Social Sciences](https://www.annualreviews.org/content/journals/10.1146/annurev.soc.28.110601.141107) — Lamont and Molnár / Annual Review of Sociology. research review; **access:** abstract reviewed.
