## The Royal Society: a journal outlives its founder

**Question:** What lets an intellectual network become a durable institution?

**Setting:** 1660–nineteenth century · England

**Technologies:** Constitutions and charters (T146), Patronage (T228), Peer review of work (T028), Succession planning (T293), Institutional archives (T033)

Henry Oldenburg began Philosophical Transactions in 1665 as a personal publishing venture within the Royal Society’s scientific network. Individual successors continued the journal before the Society took responsibility in 1752. Its survival depended on recurring work: selecting papers, maintaining correspondence, editing issues, and finding money to publish them.

### Outcome: Bounded success

A durable publication and scientific institution emerged, but through repeated changes rather than an unchanged founding design. Survival did not mean commercial self-sufficiency: publishing drew on support for much of its history.

### Documented observations

- The Royal Society formed in 1660 and received a royal charter in 1662. [royal-history](https://royalsociety.org/about-us/who-we-are/history/)

- Oldenburg launched Philosophical Transactions in 1665; the Society assumed responsibility in 1752. [royal-journal](https://royalsociety.org/journals/publishing-activities/publishing350/history-philosophical-transactions/)

- Reviewing and publication arrangements changed over time. Publishing cost the Society more than it earned during the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. [royal-journal](https://royalsociety.org/journals/publishing-activities/publishing350/history-philosophical-transactions/) [royal-money](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26495578/)

### Possible explanations

- **plausible:** Royal recognition helped make the network legitimate and attractive. [gft](https://samoburja.com/gft/) [royal-history](https://royalsociety.org/about-us/who-we-are/history/) **Alternative:** Correspondence, experiments and useful publications may have mattered more to participants than royal prestige.

- **supported-in-case:** Transferring recurring editorial and financial work to an organization helped continuity. [royal-journal](https://royalsociety.org/journals/publishing-activities/publishing350/history-philosophical-transactions/) [royal-money](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26495578/) **Alternative:** Individual successors had already kept the journal alive before the Society took ownership; organizational ownership was one stage in a longer process.

### Research connections

- **Institutionalization and knowledge succession — Historical explanation.** A journal can preserve a founder’s purpose only if later people can inherit the work, contacts, budget, and authority needed to keep publishing. The name and prestige alone do not perform those tasks. [gft](https://samoburja.com/gft/) [royal-journal](https://royalsociety.org/journals/publishing-activities/publishing350/history-philosophical-transactions/) **Transfer limit:** The Royal Society had unusually durable recognition and support. A new journal without those resources faces a different succession problem.

### Questions to take into practice

- Who will perform the recurring maintenance after the founder leaves?

- Can recognition attract resources without substituting for substantive evaluation?

### What remains uncertain

- How much did royal recognition contribute to the journal’s survival compared with its scientific usefulness and financial support? Its long history also leaves a separate question about who could participate and whose work was excluded.

## Lysenkoism: when authority can overrule evidence

**Question:** What happens when scientific standing depends on political loyalty?

**Setting:** 1930s–1960s · Soviet Union

**Technologies:** Patronage (T228), Peer review of work (T028), Replication (T029), Status hierarchies (D020), Capture (D027)

Lysenko’s rise joined political sponsorship to control of scientific appointments and institutions. The 1948 academy session made opposition to established genetics official policy. This is a case of organizational power suppressing correction, rather than merely a scientist holding a mistaken theory.

### Outcome: Failure on the stated objective

Political intervention suppressed geneticists and damaged scientific institutions. Reversing official doctrine did not immediately restore the people, appointments, and research traditions that had been disrupted.

### Documented observations

- Stalin-backed intervention at the 1948 academy session prohibited genetics research and promoted Lysenko’s position. [lysenko-history](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6499510/)

- Research traditions were interrupted as geneticists and institutions came under political pressure. [lysenko-history](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6499510/)

- Institutional arrangements associated with Lysenkoism persisted beyond the rejection of its scientific claims, complicating recovery. [lysenko-after](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/full/10.1086/696937)

### Possible explanations

- **supported-in-case:** Political control made scientific disagreement costly and helped shield favored claims from correction. [lysenko-history](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6499510/) **Alternative:** Career competition, economic pressures and institutional rivalry also contributed; political ideology alone is incomplete.

- **plausible:** Changing official doctrine is insufficient if appointments and institutional dependencies persist. [lysenko-after](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/full/10.1086/696937) **Alternative:** Recovery may also have been slowed by the loss of trained people and interrupted research, even where appointments or rules had changed.

### Research connections

- **Capture and suppression of error correction — Documented historical process.** When the authority judging a scientific claim also controls the careers of its critics, a mistaken claim can survive challenges that would otherwise expose it. [lysenko-history](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6499510/) [lysenko-after](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/full/10.1086/696937) **Transfer limit:** The decisive feature here was repression of criticism. Disagreement about research priorities is a different situation unless comparable control over dissent is present.

### Questions to take into practice

- Can evidence overturn the claim of a powerful sponsor?

- What happens to a dissenter’s job, access and ability to publish?

### What remains uncertain

- How much did the later recovery depend on changes in appointments, protection for criticism, or rebuilding research networks? Those processes need to be distinguished from the formal rejection of Lysenko’s ideas.

**GFT reading note:** {"chapter": "Honors Fuel Achievement", "pages": "41–42", "interpretation": "The manuscript uses Lysenko to illustrate prestige distortion. Institutional suppression is well documented; broader famine attribution is not established here."}

## The Manhattan Project: coordination at enormous human cost

**Question:** Can mission achievement be separated from the legitimacy and costs of the mission?

**Setting:** 1942–1945 · United States

**Technologies:** Project teams (T163), Delegated authority (T115), Confidentiality agreements (T097), Public provisioning (T270)

The Manhattan Project coordinated scientific teams, public resources, contractors, and large sites under wartime military authority to develop atomic weapons. Its leaders could mobilize resources on an extraordinary scale. The same authority could impose decisions on people outside the project, including communities displaced to make room for its facilities.

### Outcome: Mixed outcomes

The project achieved its weapons-development objective. That technical achievement came with destructive consequences and costs borne by people who did not choose the mission, including displacement at Oak Ridge.

### Documented observations

- General Leslie Groves’s responsibilities included obtaining resources, recruiting expertise, and selecting project sites. [manhattan-organization](https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/manhattan-project-site-selection.htm)

- Acquiring the Oak Ridge site displaced existing rural communities. [manhattan-displacement](https://www.nps.gov/mapr/learn/historyculture/oakridge-displacement.htm)

### Possible explanations

- **plausible:** A clear mandate and concentrated authority reduced some coordination and resource-allocation obstacles. [manhattan-organization](https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/manhattan-project-site-selection.htm) [gft](https://samoburja.com/gft/) **Alternative:** Exceptional wartime funding, urgency and accumulated scientific knowledge may explain much of the speed.

- **plausible:** Secrecy and administrative power shifted important costs onto people unable to contest the mission. [manhattan-displacement](https://www.nps.gov/mapr/learn/historyculture/oakridge-displacement.htm) **Alternative:** Urgency, the scale of the facilities, and the site chosen may have contributed to displacement alongside the project’s secrecy and concentrated authority.

### Research connections

- **Mission-oriented coordination — Historical explanation.** Technical leadership needed political authorization and access to resources. Concentrating those powers also limited the ability of affected communities to challenge decisions. [manhattan-organization](https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/manhattan-project-site-selection.htm) [gft](https://samoburja.com/gft/) **Transfer limit:** Wartime authority and exceptional public funding changed which actions were possible and who could contest them. A civilian project must establish its mandate and accountability under different conditions.

### Questions to take into practice

- Whose definition of success is being used?

- Can a project secure resources while preserving challenge and accountability?

### What remains uncertain

- How much of the project’s speed came from its authority structure, and how much from exceptional funding, urgency, and prior scientific work? The experience of displaced residents and workers also matters when judging what the organizational achievement cost.

## Apollo: a clear goal still needed a coalition

**Question:** What makes a large mission survive beyond the person who announced it?

**Setting:** 1961–1972 · United States

**Technologies:** Project teams (T163), Coalitions (T203), Contracts (T267), Design reviews (T181), Delegated authority (T115)

Apollo combined a public deadline with contractors, administrative coordination and continuing political support. A successful landing depended on more than an inspiring goal or one exceptional engineer. The case examines how a coalition can sustain a bounded mission while remaining vulnerable to changing priorities afterward.

### Outcome: Bounded success

Apollo achieved the lunar landing in 1969, within the decade set by the 1961 goal. Its coalition sustained a specific mission; continuing a program afterward required renewed agreement about its purpose and cost.

### Documented observations

- Apollo achieved the 1969 landing within the goal announced in 1961. [apollo-management](https://www.nasa.gov/history/SP-4219/Chapter8.html)

- The mission depended on cooperation across technical teams, administrators, contractors, and political supporters. [apollo-management](https://www.nasa.gov/history/SP-4219/Chapter8.html)

- NASA coordinated extensive contracted work rather than producing the entire system in-house. [apollo-retrospective](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/04/sp-4503-apollo.pdf)

### Possible explanations

- **supported-in-case:** A shared concrete objective helped align institutions with different interests. [apollo-management](https://www.nasa.gov/history/SP-4219/Chapter8.html) **Alternative:** Cold War competition and exceptional public expenditure also supplied commitment.

- **plausible:** Contracting let NASA combine dispersed capabilities while retaining systems-level integration. [apollo-retrospective](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/04/sp-4503-apollo.pdf) **Alternative:** The ability to integrate contractors may have mattered more than contracting itself; distributing work can also fragment accountability.

### Research connections

- **Coalition analysis of program implementation — Historical analysis.** A deadline coordinates work only while people with different responsibilities continue to supply decisions, funding, and capabilities. Apollo’s coalition made the goal executable. [apollo-management](https://www.nasa.gov/history/SP-4219/Chapter8.html) **Transfer limit:** The visibility and political urgency of a lunar landing helped mobilize resources that routine public services cannot assume.

### Questions to take into practice

- Which coalition will still support the project after its founder or political sponsor leaves?

- Who integrates work across contractors and resolves tradeoffs?

### What remains uncertain

- How much did the management arrangements contribute compared with Cold War priorities and exceptional expenditure? Achieving the deadline does not reveal what a different contracting or funding arrangement would have cost.

## Challenger: warnings that did not reach the decision

**Question:** How can an organization have expertise yet fail to use it?

**Setting:** 1986 · United States

**Technologies:** Design reviews (T181), Handoff protocols (T190), Public decision records (T159), Whistleblowing channels (T322), Goal displacement (D025)

Before Challenger’s launch, engineers raised concerns that did not reach senior decision-makers intact. Management reversed an initial recommendation against launch, and the decision proceeded without key managers knowing the extent of the dispute. The failure was not simply a lack of expertise: the organization failed to carry that expertise into the decision.

### Outcome: Failure on the stated objective

Challenger was lost and seven people died. The investigation identified failures in communication and management alongside the physical failure. Understanding the accident requires both the technical vulnerability and the process through which unresolved concerns were discounted.

### Documented observations

- The commission found incomplete or misleading information, conflict between engineering data and management judgments, and safety problems bypassing key managers. [challenger-commission](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/search.jsp?R=19860015255)

- An initial recommendation against launch was reversed by management; senior managers were not aware of the dispute when the launch went ahead. [challenger-history](https://www.nasa.gov/history/SP-4219/Chapter15.html)

### Possible explanations

- **supported-in-case:** Escalation and decision procedures failed to preserve the substance of the engineering objection. [challenger-commission](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/search.jsp?R=19860015255) [challenger-history](https://www.nasa.gov/history/SP-4219/Chapter15.html) **Alternative:** The physical vulnerability and environmental conditions were necessary parts of the accident, not merely background.

- **plausible:** Organizational incentives may have made uncertain warnings easier to discount. [challenger-history](https://www.nasa.gov/history/SP-4219/Chapter15.html) **Alternative:** Managers may have interpreted uncertain evidence through established routines as well as responding to organizational incentives. The decision record is needed to distinguish those influences.

### Research connections

- **Organizational error correction and voice — Investigation findings about the decision process.** An objection can be raised yet fail to affect a decision if its content, uncertainty, or author’s judgment disappears during escalation. Having a reporting channel is different from preserving what it carries. [challenger-commission](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/search.jsp?R=19860015255) **Transfer limit:** A reporting procedure alone cannot be assumed to prevent accidents. Decision-makers also need authority, time, and a defined response to unresolved concerns.

### Questions to take into practice

- Can decision makers see the strongest unresolved objection in its author’s terms?

- Who can pause the decision, and who owns the response to that pause?

### What remains uncertain

- Why did the warnings lose force as they moved through the organization? Schedule pressures, accepted routines, and judgments about uncertain evidence may have contributed in different ways.

## COTS: public capability helps build private capability

**Question:** What does a market need before suppliers can finance delivery?

**Setting:** 2006–2013 · United States

**Technologies:** Contracts (T267), Public provisioning (T270), Design reviews (T181), Project teams (T163)

NASA used milestone-based development partnerships to help firms build cargo transport capability. Firms retained substantial initiative, while NASA supplied funding, expertise, interfaces, and future demand. The program also ended a partnership when the supplier could not obtain financing, making both the support and the limits of the arrangement visible.

### Outcome: Mixed outcomes

SpaceX and Orbital completed demonstration missions; Rocketplane Kistler did not. Staged commitments produced working capability from some participants, but financing remained uncertain and negotiated service prices exceeded initial proposals.

### Documented observations

- SpaceX completed its COTS demonstration in 2012 and Orbital completed its demonstration in 2013. [cots-program](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2016/08/sp-2014-617.pdf)

- NASA terminated Rocketplane Kistler after missed milestones; the oversight account records $32.1 million spent before termination. [cots-audit](https://oig.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/ig-13-016.pdf)

- Suppliers’ financing depended partly on credible future demand, and negotiated service prices grew beyond their initial proposals. [cots-program](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2016/08/sp-2014-617.pdf)

### Possible explanations

- **participant-explanation:** Staged payments and credible customer demand helped turn emerging capability into an investable undertaking. [cots-program](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2016/08/sp-2014-617.pdf) **Alternative:** Firm capability, founder capital and the availability of private finance also differed between participants.

- **plausible:** Terminating one partner and selecting another limited dependence on a single supplier. [cots-program](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2016/08/sp-2014-617.pdf) [cots-audit](https://oig.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/ig-13-016.pdf) **Alternative:** The result could also reflect which firms were selected and what capabilities they already possessed; replacing a partner does not by itself explain overall program costs.

### Research connections

- **Complementarity of public procurement and entrepreneurial capacity — Program history and oversight findings.** An emerging supplier needs more than a promising prototype. Funding, access to expertise, and a credible customer can make the next stage possible while leaving important risks with the firm. [cots-program](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2016/08/sp-2014-617.pdf) [cots-audit](https://oig.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/ig-13-016.pdf) **Transfer limit:** Space transport combines unusually high entry costs with a small number of major customers. A market with many small buyers offers a different basis for financing.

### Questions to take into practice

- Does a supplier have a credible customer after the prototype?

- What can be stopped or replaced without abandoning the entire mission?

### What remains uncertain

## Roman adoption: an heir is also a political alliance

**Question:** How did adoption help an emperor establish a successor?

**Setting:** First–second centuries CE · Roman Empire

**Technologies:** Adoption (S022), Succession planning (T293), Dynasties (S009), Patronage (T228)

Imperial adoption made selected adults members of a ruling family and recognizable successors. It could bring someone into the line of succession without making biological descent the only route. The selection still took place within elite family networks and political negotiations, rather than an open competition for the most capable ruler.

### Outcome: Mixed outcomes

Adoption helped establish recognizable succession arrangements, including the Antonine line. Naming an heir could coordinate expectations, but the heir still needed the support and resources required to govern.

### Documented observations

- Hadrian adopted Antoninus Pius, who adopted Marcus Aurelius and Lucius Verus. [roman-museum](https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/the-antonine-dynasty-138-193)

- The Antonine dynasty drew on wealthy provincial and Italian family connections. [roman-museum](https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/the-antonine-dynasty-138-193)

### Possible explanations

- **plausible:** Designating an heir could align the expectations of incumbent, successor and supporters. [gft](https://samoburja.com/gft/) [roman-museum](https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/the-antonine-dynasty-138-193) **Alternative:** Selection may instead ratify existing military and political power rather than create cooperation.

- **plausible:** Familiar family status helped make a negotiated succession socially intelligible. [gft](https://samoburja.com/gft/) **Alternative:** Legal adoption without elite acceptance would not guarantee a peaceful transfer.

### Research connections

- **Credible commitment and succession — Historical interpretation.** Naming an heir gives supporters a person around whom to coordinate. Whether they actually accept the successor depends on relationships and resources beyond the legal designation. [gft](https://samoburja.com/gft/) [roman-politics](https://academic.oup.com/book/36344/chapter-abstract/319849468) **Transfer limit:** Succession within a restricted imperial elite differs from open hiring or democratic selection: the eligible candidates and the means of securing support were fundamentally different.

### Questions to take into practice

- Does naming a successor transfer informal support as well as a title?

- Are apparent merit criteria explaining selection or rationalizing it afterward?

### What remains uncertain

- How much did adoption itself secure cooperation, and how much did it formalize alliances that already existed? Military backing, family connections, and the exclusion of other candidates complicate any claim that the arrangement selected rulers by merit.

## The Tetrarchy: a succession design that needed its designer

**Question:** What breaks when a coordinating founder leaves?

**Setting:** 293–324 CE · Roman Empire

**Technologies:** Succession planning (T293), Deputy roles (T294), Dynasties (S009)

Diocletian divided rule among senior and junior emperors, combining present authority with an intended succession path. The arrangement addressed a vast empire’s coordination problem, yet its succession mechanism did not endure intact after retirement. Administrative reforms and the four-ruler system must be evaluated separately.

### Outcome: Mixed outcomes

The four-ruler succession arrangement did not endure after Diocletian’s retirement, while parts of the wider administrative transformation lasted much longer. The failure of one succession mechanism did not erase every other reform.

### Documented observations

- The arrangement named two senior emperors, the Augusti, and two subordinate emperors, the Caesars. [tetrarchy-museum](https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/the-roman-empire-27-b-c-393-a-d)

- The tetrarchy was short-lived, while broader changes in government helped shape the later empire. [tetrarchy-history](https://academic.oup.com/book/47281)

### Possible explanations

- **plausible:** A succession system dependent on the founder’s arbitration was vulnerable when that arbitration disappeared. [gft](https://samoburja.com/gft/) [tetrarchy-history](https://academic.oup.com/book/47281) **Alternative:** Rival dynastic claims and control of military resources may explain instability better than complexity alone.

- **plausible:** Giving juniors current authority created useful experience but also an independent power base. [gft](https://samoburja.com/gft/) **Alternative:** Regional administrative demands may have required distributed authority regardless of succession concerns.

### Research connections

- **Founder dependence and power succession — Historical hypothesis.** A founder may resolve disputes that the formal succession rules leave open. Retirement tests whether successors can obtain cooperation through the rules without that person’s intervention. [gft](https://samoburja.com/gft/) [tetrarchy-history](https://academic.oup.com/book/47281) **Transfer limit:** The emperors controlled military and territorial resources. Ordinary deputy roles do not carry the same independent capacity to contest a succession.

### Questions to take into practice

- Can successors use the rules to resolve a dispute without the founder?

- Which parts of a reform survived even when its most visible arrangement collapsed?

### What remains uncertain

- Did the succession order fail mainly because the founder could no longer arbitrate, because rivals commanded military support, or because dynastic claims overrode the intended rules? The sequence of alliances and disputes matters for separating these explanations.

## Japanese adopted heirs: succession beyond the bloodline

**Question:** Does keeping family control require choosing a biological heir?

**Setting:** Postwar listed firms; study published 2013 · Japan, with Danish comparison

**Technologies:** Adoption (S022), Succession planning (T293), Merit-based selection (T284), Dynasties (S009)

Japanese business families can bring adults into the family through adoption or marriage, widening succession beyond biological children while retaining family control. Comparisons of listed firms suggest that these non-blood heirs can perform differently from biological heirs. The distinction matters: family succession is not one uniform way of selecting a leader.

### Outcome: Mixed outcomes

In the Japanese firm study, non-blood heirs performed better than blood heirs on the measures examined. Research on Danish firms found poorer performance following family CEO succession. The populations and succession arrangements differ, so the findings raise questions about how candidates are selected rather than settling whether family control is generally better.

### Documented observations

- A study of Japanese listed firms grouped adopted sons, sons-in-law, and adopted sons-in-law as non-blood heirs and found favorable performance comparisons with blood heirs. [japan-heirs](https://randallmorck.ca/wp-content/uploads/2020/02/13-adoptive-expectations-rising-sons-in-japanese-family-firms.pdf)

- In a Danish study, family CEO succession was associated with poorer performance in an analysis using firstborn-child gender to help distinguish succession choices from other firm differences. [denmark-heirs](https://www.nber.org/papers/w12356)

### Possible explanations

- **plausible:** Allowing an outsider to become a family successor can widen the candidate pool while preserving continuity. [japan-heirs](https://randallmorck.ca/wp-content/uploads/2020/02/13-adoptive-expectations-rising-sons-in-japanese-family-firms.pdf) **Alternative:** Firms able to attract strong candidates may already differ in ways that drive performance.

- **plausible:** The threat of replacement may change effort by biological heirs and professional managers. [japan-heirs](https://randallmorck.ca/wp-content/uploads/2020/02/13-adoptive-expectations-rising-sons-in-japanese-family-firms.pdf) **Alternative:** Succession results do not separately identify selection from these proposed incentive effects.

### Research connections

- **Succession, selection and agency — Empirical comparisons in different institutional settings.** A succession rule changes who can become a candidate and what incentives existing candidates face. Bringing an outsider into the family can widen selection while preserving relationships associated with family control. [japan-heirs](https://randallmorck.ca/wp-content/uploads/2020/02/13-adoptive-expectations-rising-sons-in-japanese-family-firms.pdf) [denmark-heirs](https://www.nber.org/papers/w12356) **Transfer limit:** Adoption and marriage carry legal, cultural, and personal commitments. A favorable business-performance comparison does not tell us how the people entering those relationships experienced them.

### Questions to take into practice

- Can the successor pool widen without losing necessary trust and knowledge?

- What evidence distinguishes good selection from a favorable inherited business?

### What remains uncertain

- Were stronger candidates selected, did the prospect of competing for succession change behavior, or were firms attracting non-blood heirs already different? The perspectives of spouses and candidates excluded from succession are also missing from the performance comparison.

## Confucian transmission: preserving a tradition by changing it

**Question:** When is adaptation a form of preservation rather than a failure to copy?

**Setting:** Late Zhou through Han and later reception · China and East Asia

**Technologies:** Lineage recognition (T305), Education (S017), Institutional archives (T033), Ideology (S015), Storytelling (T023)

Teaching, texts, ritual practice, and political sponsorship carried Confucian traditions across generations. Transmission also changed the tradition: later teachers and governments selected and interpreted texts for purposes that differed from those of earlier participants. Preserving an influential tradition was not the same as reproducing one founder’s design unchanged.

### Outcome: Mixed outcomes

Confucian traditions became influential long after Confucius’s lifetime. Their later place in curricula and government depended on interpretation and adaptation. The surviving accounts do not let us reconstruct every aspect of Confucius’s own intentions or teaching with equal confidence.

### Documented observations

- Sources for Confucius’s life postdate his lifetime and differ in character and purpose. [confucius-iep](https://iep.utm.edu/confucius/)

- By the Han, texts associated with Confucius had become authoritative in several domains and connected with government teaching positions. [confucius-sep](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/confucius/)

- Later Neo-Confucian interpretation placed the Analects within a different curricular and conceptual arrangement. [confucius-sep](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/confucius/)

### Possible explanations

- **plausible:** Teaching lineages and reusable texts enabled ideas to survive a founder’s limited immediate influence. [confucius-sep](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/confucius/) [gft](https://samoburja.com/gft/) **Alternative:** State adoption and reinterpretation by later thinkers may have been more decisive than fidelity to the founder.

- **plausible:** Ritual practice can preserve coordination knowledge that a written rule alone misses. [gft](https://samoburja.com/gft/) [confucius-iep](https://iep.utm.edu/confucius/) **Alternative:** Ritual can also preserve rank and convention without preserving the function attributed to it.

### Research connections

- **Cultural transmission and institutional reconstruction — Historical scholarship with a hypothesis about the role of practice.** A text can survive while its interpretation changes; a ritual can continue while the relationships around it are transformed. Transmission therefore involves people who teach, interpret, and authorize practice, as well as preserved documents. [confucius-sep](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/confucius/) [confucius-iep](https://iep.utm.edu/confucius/) [gft](https://samoburja.com/gft/) **Transfer limit:** The persistence of a tradition does not show that every surviving practice still performs its earlier function, or that it originated with a single founder.

### Questions to take into practice

- Which parts of a tradition are practiced, and which only quoted?

- What adaptations made transmission possible, and who authorized them?

### What remains uncertain

- Which practices continued from earlier communities, and which were later reconstructions given ancient authority? Texts written after Confucius’s lifetime make it difficult to separate his intentions from the meanings later readers attached to him.

## Protestant literacy: competing explanations for prosperity

**Question:** Can an ideology create useful capabilities without its official purpose being economic?

**Setting:** Historical German lands; nineteenth-century Prussian comparison · Central Europe

**Technologies:** Ideology (S015), Education (S017), Congregations (T311)

Religious expectations about reading could encourage skills useful outside religious life. Historical research on Prussian counties found evidence consistent with literacy explaining much of the prosperity difference associated with Protestantism. A separate study of long-run city growth found no Protestant growth advantage, raising a question about which economic outcomes the proposed mechanism can explain.

### Outcome: Mixed outcomes

Prussian county results are consistent with literacy explaining much of an observed prosperity gap. A separate city-growth study finds no Protestant growth advantage over its long period. Different units and outcomes prevent treating either finding as a universal verdict on religion and prosperity.

### Documented observations

- Becker and Woessmann use county data and distance to Wittenberg as an instrument for Protestantism. [protestant-literacy](https://storre.stir.ac.uk/handle/1893/1653)

- They report results consistent with literacy accounting for much of the economic difference. [protestant-literacy](https://storre.stir.ac.uk/handle/1893/1653)

- Cantoni studies population in 272 cities from 1300–1900 and reports no growth effect of Protestantism. [protestant-counter](https://academic.oup.com/jeea/article-abstract/13/4/561/2319778)

### Possible explanations

- **supported-in-case:** Religious reading expectations generated skills useful outside religious practice. [protestant-literacy](https://storre.stir.ac.uk/handle/1893/1653) **Alternative:** The interpretation depends on instrument validity and the separation of religion from other geographically patterned influences.

- **plausible:** Different outcome measures and time scales explain part of the apparent disagreement. [protestant-literacy](https://storre.stir.ac.uk/handle/1893/1653) [protestant-counter](https://academic.oup.com/jeea/article-abstract/13/4/561/2319778) **Alternative:** Further comparison might reveal genuinely incompatible results rather than merely different measurements.

### Research connections

- **Human capital versus cultural work-ethic explanations — Quantitative historical studies with different outcomes and comparisons.** A religious norm might affect economic life through a practical capability such as reading, without economic improvement being its purpose. That is a different explanation from claiming that belief directly produces a stronger work ethic. [protestant-literacy](https://storre.stir.ac.uk/handle/1893/1653) [protestant-counter](https://academic.oup.com/jeea/article-abstract/13/4/561/2319778) **Transfer limit:** The studies concern particular historical populations and periods. Their results do not establish that changing religious affiliation would produce the same capabilities or outcomes elsewhere.

### Questions to take into practice

- Which observable skill mediates the claimed cultural effect?

- Would the explanation survive a different measure of success or period?

### What remains uncertain

- Could geography or other historical differences explain both religious affiliation and prosperity? The literacy explanation depends on separating those influences. It also remains important to understand why a result about county prosperity does not appear as a general advantage in long-run city growth.

## FOGBANK: documentation is not a living capability

**Question:** How do institutions discover that a capability they once had no longer exists?

**Setting:** 2000s; public audit in 2009 · United States

**Technologies:** Institutional archives (T033), Succession planning (T293), Cross-training (T013), Tacit knowledge dependence (D011)

When a defense program needed to resume a discontinued production activity, the organization no longer had all the knowledge, expertise, and facilities needed to perform it. Reestablishing the capability took additional time and money. The episode shows how an organization can retain a requirement and records of earlier work while losing the practical ability to carry it out.

### Outcome: Failure on the stated objective

Reestablishing the production capability delayed the program by a year and required $69 million to address the problem, according to the public audit. Missing knowledge was part of the failure, alongside weaknesses in managing a known program risk.

### Documented observations

- The public audit identified missing knowledge, expertise, and facilities, together with ineffective execution of a risk-management strategy. [fogbank-audit](https://www.gao.gov/assets/a286696.html)

- The audit reported a one-year delay and $69 million associated with resolving the production problem. [fogbank-audit](https://www.gao.gov/assets/a286696.html)

### Possible explanations

- **supported-in-case:** Capability disappeared when knowledge, people and supporting infrastructure were not maintained together. [fogbank-audit](https://www.gao.gov/assets/a286696.html) **Alternative:** Budgeting and execution failures contributed; attributing everything to unwritten knowledge would be incomplete.

- **plausible:** Periodic demonstrations by successors could reveal hidden capability loss earlier than document reviews. [gft](https://samoburja.com/gft/) **Alternative:** This proposed intervention was not evaluated in the audit and could be costly or impractical in some settings.

### Research connections

- **Organizational memory and tacit knowledge — Audit findings about a specific loss of capability.** Documentation is useful only alongside people and facilities able to turn it into action. Separately changing staff, budgets, and infrastructure can leave responsibility for the combined capability unclear. [fogbank-audit](https://www.gao.gov/assets/a286696.html) [gft](https://samoburja.com/gft/) **Transfer limit:** Specialized production can have unusually expensive restart requirements. The size of this loss cannot be used to estimate the cost of ordinary staff turnover.

### Questions to take into practice

- What critical activity has not been performed successfully by the next generation?

- Who owns continuity risk when facilities, staff and budgets change separately?

### What remains uncertain

- Which losses were most consequential: experienced personnel, practical knowledge, facilities, or failures to act on the known risk? Demonstrations by successor teams might have exposed the gap earlier, but their cost and usefulness were not tested in this episode.

## Bell Labs: discovery and diffusion need different arrangements

**Question:** Can the institution producing breakthroughs also obstruct their wider use?

**Setting:** 1920s–1960s; 1956 decree studied · United States

**Technologies:** Project teams (T163), Open licensing (T268), Public provisioning (T270), Markets (T211)

Bell Labs paired sustained industrial research with a powerful incumbent business. The 1956 antitrust settlement required royalty-free licensing of existing patents. This case separates an organization’s ability to invent from the legal and competitive conditions under which others can build on those inventions.

### Outcome: Mixed outcomes

The laboratory produced important discoveries, while the patent study finds increased follow-on innovation after compulsory licensing outside telecommunications. The sectoral exception matters: access to knowledge did not remove every market barrier.

### Documented observations

- Bell Labs’ research capacity was connected to the telephone system and to an organization combining research with development work. [bell-history](https://www.nokia.com/bell-labs/about/history/)

- The 1956 decree required royalty-free licensing of existing Bell patents. [bell-antitrust](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/pol.20190086)

- A comparison with non-Bell patents found lasting increases in follow-on patenting outside telecommunications, but not inside the sector. [bell-antitrust](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/pol.20190086)

### Possible explanations

- **plausible:** Stable resources and links between research and engineering supported discovery. [bell-history](https://www.nokia.com/bell-labs/about/history/) **Alternative:** Selection of exceptional researchers and the field’s scientific opportunities could explain much of the output.

- **supported-in-case:** Patent access alone was insufficient where other barriers continued to constrain entry. [bell-antitrust](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/pol.20190086) **Alternative:** Differences in opportunities and conditions across sectors may also contribute to the contrast; licensing was not the only feature that varied.

### Research connections

- **Knowledge spillovers and barriers to entry — A comparison of affected patents with a control group.** Permission to use an invention removes one barrier. Firms may still need customers, equipment, distribution, or access to a market dominated by an incumbent. [bell-antitrust](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/pol.20190086) **Transfer limit:** Follow-on patenting captures only part of innovation. It does not show every useful improvement, commercial application, or benefit to the public.

### Questions to take into practice

- Does the research organization enable outsiders to build on its work?

- Which barriers remain after formal access is opened?

### What remains uncertain

- Did wider licensing change Bell’s own incentive to invest in research over the longer term? Which remaining barriers explain the different result inside telecommunications? More follow-on patents do not, by themselves, measure every benefit or cost to users.

## Xerox PARC: invention needs a route into the world

**Question:** Why can a productive research organization fail to capture some of what it creates?

**Setting:** 1970s–1990s · United States

**Technologies:** Project teams (T163), Autonomous microenterprise units (T167), Markets (T211), Boundary-spanning roles (T205)

PARC developed prototypes and technical communities whose influence spread beyond Xerox. Some technologies found a route to users through spin-offs with different customers and revenue models. A productive research organization still needed a way to connect its inventions to development, distribution, and a viable business.

### Outcome: Mixed outcomes

PARC’s inventions had substantial influence, while Xerox and the spin-offs captured value unevenly. In a comparison of selected spin-offs, successful ventures changed their business models more than unsuccessful ventures. The later outcome does not reveal which opportunities were clearly promising when the decisions were made.

### Documented observations

- PARC engineers developed the Alto through intensive prototyping and use within their technical community. [parc-engineers](https://spectrum.ieee.org/xerox-parc/laser-printer-history)

- Among the Xerox technology spin-offs compared in the study, successful ventures changed their business models, while failed ventures showed less search and learning. [parc-business](https://www.hbs.edu/ris/Publication%20Files/01-002_07351ae8-58be-44e5-a6d8-205cbf5b4424.pdf)

### Possible explanations

- **supported-in-case:** A successful existing business model can narrow which new uses an organization can recognize and support. [parc-business](https://www.hbs.edu/ris/Publication%20Files/01-002_07351ae8-58be-44e5-a6d8-205cbf5b4424.pdf) **Alternative:** Markets, complementary technology and timing may make apparently promising uses unviable regardless of management interpretation.

- **plausible:** Spin-offs can create room for a different customer and revenue model. [parc-business](https://www.hbs.edu/ris/Publication%20Files/01-002_07351ae8-58be-44e5-a6d8-205cbf5b4424.pdf) **Alternative:** Spin-offs also lose resources and coordination benefits; selected survivors exaggerate their apparent advantage.

### Research connections

- **Business models as an organizational learning mechanism — A qualitative comparison of selected ventures.** A prototype can be technically impressive while lacking a customer, distribution channel, service arrangement, or source of revenue. Testing those connections is part of learning what the invention can become. [parc-business](https://www.hbs.edu/ris/Publication%20Files/01-002_07351ae8-58be-44e5-a6d8-205cbf5b4424.pdf) **Transfer limit:** A retrospective account benefits from knowing what later succeeded. It cannot show that every rejected experiment would have been sensible to fund at the time.

### Questions to take into practice

- Who owns the work of turning a prototype into a usable service?

- Can a new idea test a different business model without satisfying all assumptions of the old one?

### What remains uncertain

- How many apparently promising spin-offs failed, and which inventions were commercialized successfully inside Xerox? Without both sides of that comparison, it is easy to mistake the most memorable successes elsewhere for a complete account of Xerox’s choices.

## NUMMI: changing the work changed the culture

**Question:** Can people learn a new way of organizing without replacing the workforce?

**Setting:** 1984–2010 · Fremont, California, and GM/Toyota networks

**Technologies:** Joint ventures (T330), Cross-training (T013), Learning through participation (D007), Standard operating procedures (T188), Communities of practice (T004)

Toyota and GM used the NUMMI joint venture to combine car production with organizational learning. Training and changes in daily management accompanied reported improvements among a workforce previously regarded as difficult. The plant later closed, showing why improving work and sustaining a business need to be examined separately.

### Outcome: Mixed outcomes

Participants reported improvements in quality and attendance, and research traced learning from Toyota through NUMMI to GM. The plant nevertheless closed after GM’s withdrawal and Toyota’s assessment that continued operation was not viable.

### Documented observations

- John Shook, who participated in the venture, described changes in training and management accompanied by lower absenteeism and better quality. [nummi-shook](https://www.lean.org/downloads/35.pdf)

- Knowledge developed through the Toyota–GM partnership was transferred beyond NUMMI into GM. [nummi-study](https://cmr.berkeley.edu/2005/08/47-4-learning-through-alliances-general-motors-and-nummi/)

- Toyota announced the end of production after GM’s withdrawal, citing economic viability. [nummi-close](https://global.toyota/en/detail/309984)

### Possible explanations

- **participant-explanation:** Changing routines and how problems receive help can change the experience that sustains workplace norms. [nummi-shook](https://www.lean.org/downloads/35.pdf) **Alternative:** Restart conditions, selection and economic circumstances may contribute to the reported improvement.

- **plausible:** Learning transfers through relationships and participation, not simply copying visible artifacts. [nummi-study](https://cmr.berkeley.edu/2005/08/47-4-learning-through-alliances-general-motors-and-nummi/) **Alternative:** Training, management decisions, and formal documentation may have worked together with relationships; their separate contributions are difficult to isolate.

### Research connections

- **Organizational learning and knowledge transfer — Participant accounts and research on knowledge transfer.** People learn an unfamiliar work system through what happens when they identify a problem, ask for help, or attempt a task. The daily response can change expectations more than a statement about organizational culture. [nummi-shook](https://www.lean.org/downloads/35.pdf) [nummi-study](https://cmr.berkeley.edu/2005/08/47-4-learning-through-alliances-general-motors-and-nummi/) **Transfer limit:** The joint venture had access to Toyota’s training and GM’s organization. Another workplace would need to establish who can teach the practice and support it after training ends.

### Questions to take into practice

- What changes in the daily handling of a problem?

- Can another part of the organization reproduce the practice with its own people?

### What remains uncertain

- How did workers experience the changes, and how much of the reported improvement came from the work system rather than restart conditions or selection? GM’s withdrawal and Toyota’s commercial judgment also leave questions about how benefits and costs were distributed between the partners.

Read participant account of training, production-system changes and reported quality/attendance improvements.

## Surgical checklists: a form is not an implementation

**Question:** Why can a promising practice produce different results at scale?

**Setting:** 2007–2014 studies · Eight-country pilot and Ontario, Canada

**Technologies:** Checklists (T189), Handoff protocols (T190), After-action reviews (T026)

A surgical checklist organizes pauses, shared confirmation, and critical tasks. An early eight-hospital study found improved outcomes after its introduction; a later Ontario study did not find significant improvements after broad adoption. The contrast raises a concrete question: what must change in daily teamwork for a checklist to affect care?

### Outcome: Mixed outcomes

The pilot reported lower mortality and complications. Ontario’s 101-hospital comparison found no statistically significant reduction. Both were before-and-after studies, so differences cannot automatically be attributed to implementation quality or the checklist itself.

### Documented observations

- The eight-hospital study compared 3,733 patients before and 3,955 after introduction and reported reductions in death and complications. [checklist-pilot](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19144931/)

- The Ontario study analyzed more than 200,000 procedures across 101 hospitals and found no significant mortality or complication improvement. [checklist-ontario](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24620866/)

- An earlier systematic review reported favorable pooled results but variable compliance and a need for implementation research. It predates Ontario. [checklist-review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22968074/)

### Possible explanations

- **plausible:** Actual team use and integration into work may matter more than formally adopting a checklist. [checklist-review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22968074/) **Alternative:** Different baseline risks, outcomes and observation methods could also explain the contrast.

- **plausible:** Before-and-after improvements may partly reflect concurrent changes or observation effects. [checklist-pilot](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19144931/) [checklist-ontario](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24620866/) **Alternative:** A real benefit could still exist; absence of randomization limits attribution rather than disproving effectiveness.

### Research connections

- **Implementation fidelity and context — Several empirical studies with differing results.** Official adoption does not show whether staff paused, checked the relevant information, and acted on a problem. Those behaviors connect the checklist to the outcome it is intended to improve. [checklist-pilot](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19144931/) [checklist-ontario](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24620866/) [checklist-review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22968074/) **Transfer limit:** A checklist’s value depends on the work, baseline practice, and response to an identified problem. Results from surgery cannot supply an effect size for a checklist in a different setting.

### Questions to take into practice

- Was the practice performed meaningfully or merely recorded as completed?

- What baseline and comparison would show improvement rather than a change in reporting?

### What remains uncertain

- Were teams using the checklist differently, or did the hospitals start from different levels of risk and safety practice? Changes in patient mix, concurrent improvements, and measurement could also affect the comparison. The before-and-after designs cannot cleanly separate those explanations.

- **checklist-pilot:** [A Surgical Safety Checklist to Reduce Morbidity and Mortality in a Global Population](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19144931/). Haynes and colleagues / New England Journal of Medicine · primary-research · accessed as abstract. Full abstract retrieved through search after direct page access failed. Eight-hospital before-and-after study, not randomized.

- **checklist-ontario:** [Introduction of Surgical Safety Checklists in Ontario, Canada](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24620866/). Urbach and colleagues / New England Journal of Medicine · primary-research · accessed as abstract. Full abstract retrieved through search. Administrative before-and-after study across 101 hospitals; lack of significant improvement is not proof of zero effect.

## Athens: citizens governing, but not everyone was a citizen

**Question:** How did assembly participation, lottery, elected expertise, and scrutiny work together—and whom did they exclude?

**Setting:** 508–322 BCE; institutional description mainly fourth century BCE · Athens, ancient Greece

**Technologies:** Democracy (S004), Assembly democracy (S031), Direct democracy (S032), Assemblies (T101), Sortition (T108), Elections (T109), Jury trials (T319)

Classical Athens combined citizen assemblies, a council selected by lot, elected offices, and large popular juries. In the fourth century BCE, eligible male citizens could participate directly without becoming professional politicians. Most inhabitants were excluded from citizenship, however, and even among citizens only a fraction attended any particular assembly.

### Outcome: Mixed outcomes

The institutions enabled substantial direct participation among eligible male citizens and distributed many offices beyond professional politicians. That achievement coexisted with slavery, exclusions based on gender and citizenship, uneven attendance, and oligarchic interruptions. Broad participation within the citizen body did not mean political equality for the whole population or agreement about the quality of decisions.

### Documented observations

- Adult male citizenship, rather than residence alone, determined assembly eligibility; enrollment included checks of age, freedom, and parentage. [blackwell](https://generales.uprrp.edu/humanidades/wp-content/uploads/sites/5/2018/09/AthenianDemocracy.ABriefOverview.pdf)

- The council and many offices used allotment, while some responsibilities used election; incoming magistrates underwent scrutiny rather than receiving unchecked authority. [aristotle](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/athenian_const.3.3.html) [blackwell](https://generales.uprrp.edu/humanidades/wp-content/uploads/sites/5/2018/09/AthenianDemocracy.ABriefOverview.pdf)

- The eligible citizen body is estimated at around 30,000 in the fourth century, with meetings attended by thousands rather than everyone. Assembly pay helped make attendance possible, although urban residents remained better placed to attend. [canevaro](https://www.pure.ed.ac.uk/ws/files/77013395/Canevaro_Deliberation_Annales_FINAL.pdf)

### Possible explanations

- **plausible:** Rotating opportunities to govern, combined with scrutiny, plausibly reduced the permanence of an officeholding class among eligible citizens. [aristotle](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/athenian_const.3.3.html) [blackwell](https://generales.uprrp.edu/humanidades/wp-content/uploads/sites/5/2018/09/AthenianDemocracy.ABriefOverview.pdf) **Alternative:** Wealth, rhetorical skill, military prominence, and repeated informal leadership could sustain elite influence without permanent formal office.

- **supported-in-case:** Payment plausibly made attendance less costly for citizens who would otherwise give up earnings to participate. [canevaro](https://www.pure.ed.ac.uk/ws/files/77013395/Canevaro_Deliberation_Annales_FINAL.pdf) **Alternative:** Proximity to the meeting place and the political salience of particular decisions also affected who appeared.

### Research connections

- **Deliberation and political participation in classical Athenian assemblies — Historical interpretation using incomplete literary and institutional evidence.** Participation depended on an interacting institutional package, not only a voting rule. [canevaro](https://www.pure.ed.ac.uk/ws/files/77013395/Canevaro_Deliberation_Annales_FINAL.pdf) **Transfer limit:** Eligibility, travel distances, and the resources supporting participation differed sharply from those in a modern electorate. The institutions also changed across Athens's democratic periods.

### Questions to take into practice

- A right to attend and a practical opportunity to attend are different: citizenship rules, payment, and distance all shaped who was in the assembly.

- Lottery and rotation spread offices, while scrutiny and elected roles added other forms of accountability and expertise.

### What remains uncertain

- Estimates of meeting attendance vary, making it difficult to establish how consistently different parts of the citizen body participated.

- Lottery, payment, and scrutiny operated together within an economy and imperial position unlike those of most modern communities; their separate contributions remain difficult to establish.

## Porto Alegre: a budget people could shape—and a process politicians could hollow out

**Question:** What makes participation consequential, and what allows it to lose that force?

**Setting:** 1989–2017, with earlier gains and later decline distinguished · Porto Alegre, Brazil

**Technologies:** Participatory budgeting (T232), Assemblies (T101)

Porto Alegre linked neighborhood participation to municipal investment priorities through recurring assemblies and budgeting arrangements. Residents could press for infrastructure that had bypassed poorer areas. Early gains were followed by weakened political backing, implementation problems, and suspension in 2017: residents' ability to influence investment depended on more than having meetings.

### Outcome: Mixed outcomes

The early process mobilized poorer communities and helped improve access to smaller-scale infrastructure. Water, sanitation, and other services were also changing through a broader municipal policy program, so the gains cannot be assigned to participatory budgeting alone. As political support and implementation weakened, participation lost influence over investment; the process was suspended in 2017.

### Documented observations

- The city tied participatory priorities to investment decisions and used the process to involve communities previously poorly served by infrastructure. [wri](https://www.wri.org/research/porto-alegre-participatory-budgeting-and-challenge-sustaining-transformative-change)

- By 2002, extensive water, sanitation, waste, environmental-information, and education policies were operating alongside participatory budgeting. [menegat](https://doi.org/10.1177/095624780201400215)

- Political support declined and the process was suspended in 2017, following weakened financial and implementation commitments. [wri](https://www.wri.org/research/porto-alegre-participatory-budgeting-and-challenge-sustaining-transformative-change)

### Possible explanations

- **supported-in-case:** Participation became consequential when priority-setting connected to a funded and responsive municipal implementation system. [wri](https://www.wri.org/research/porto-alegre-participatory-budgeting-and-challenge-sustaining-transformative-change) [menegat](https://doi.org/10.1177/095624780201400215) **Alternative:** The governing party's redistributive agenda and capable service agencies could have driven some improvements independently of citizen meetings.

- **supported-in-case:** The later weakening suggests that participation depended on renewed political backing and funding, not simply on keeping its formal procedures. [wri](https://www.wri.org/research/porto-alegre-participatory-budgeting-and-challenge-sustaining-transformative-change) **Alternative:** Wider fiscal constraints could have reduced investment even under a government strongly committed to the participatory process.

### Research connections

- **Participatory governance embedded in state capacity — Case-based institutional explanation, not an isolated treatment effect.** Public voice connects to distribution through budgeting, administration, and political support. [wri](https://www.wri.org/research/porto-alegre-participatory-budgeting-and-challenge-sustaining-transformative-change) [menegat](https://doi.org/10.1177/095624780201400215) **Transfer limit:** Organized civil society, service agencies, and political commitments gave the assemblies ways to influence spending. An assembly without those connections would lack much of that capacity.

### Questions to take into practice

- Participation changes investment only when the priorities residents set reach funded projects and responsible implementing agencies.

- Attendance can remain visible while completion stalls; project delivery and whose needs are served reveal a different part of the result.

- A change in governing coalition can weaken the commitments that made a participatory process consequential.

### What remains uncertain

- The early improvements were intertwined with wider municipal reforms, leaving uncertainty about how much each contributed.

- The decline raises a durability question: which commitments can protect residents' influence when budgets tighten or a governing coalition changes?

Rebecca Abers and colleagues / World Resources Institute · review · accessed as sections. Key findings and retrospective explanation of the rise, weakening, and 2017 suspension.

## Ireland: a citizens' assembly inside a larger route to constitutional change

**Question:** How can a small deliberating group inform a decision that remains with the wider electorate?

**Setting:** 2016–2018 · Ireland

**Technologies:** Citizens' assemblies (T102), Sortition (T108), Referendums (T107)

Ireland's assembly brought 99 selected citizens together under an appointed chair to consider several issues, beginning with the Eighth Amendment. Evidence, deliberation, and recommendations fed into parliamentary consideration and a national referendum. The assembly helped develop proposals; it neither represented every resident nor possessed the authority to amend the constitution by itself.

### Outcome: Bounded success

The assembly produced recommendations that entered parliamentary consideration and were followed by a constitutional referendum in 2018. The electorate approved the amendment. Assembly discussion showed substantial cognitive complexity in the exchanges examined, but the referendum result also emerged from a wider political campaign and changing public attitudes.

### Documented observations

- The parliamentary mandate specified an appointed chair and 99 referendum-eligible citizens selected to broadly reflect Irish society. [report](https://citizensassembly.ie/wp-content/uploads/2023/02/FirstReport_EIGHTAMENDMENT.pdf)

- A comparative analysis of assembly and parliamentary committee debate found deeper cognitive complexity among assembly members in the discussions studied. [suiter](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/14789299211020909)

- The referendum recorded 1,429,981 votes for the constitutional proposal and 723,632 against, with turnout of 64.13%. [referendum](https://www.referendum.ie/archive/referendum-on-the-regulation-of-termination-of-pregnancy-thirty-sixth-amendment-of-the-constitution-bill-2018/index.html)

### Possible explanations

- **supported-in-case:** Structured exposure to evidence in a less strategically adversarial setting contributed to nuanced discussion. [suiter](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/14789299211020909) **Alternative:** Participant selection, different tasks, and the contrasting public roles of legislators could explain part of the measured difference.

- **plausible:** A defined path through parliament and referendum made the recommendations politically actionable. [report](https://citizensassembly.ie/wp-content/uploads/2023/02/FirstReport_EIGHTAMENDMENT.pdf) [referendum](https://www.referendum.ie/archive/referendum-on-the-regulation-of-termination-of-pregnancy-thirty-sixth-amendment-of-the-constitution-bill-2018/index.html) **Alternative:** Long-running campaigning and changing public attitudes might have produced reform through another institutional route.

### Research connections

- **Epistemic deliberation and cognitive complexity — Comparative case evidence using coded discourse, not randomized institutional assignment.** Reasoning quality can be assessed through how people recognize different considerations and connect them, separately from whether they change their preference or reach agreement. [suiter](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/14789299211020909) **Transfer limit:** Nuanced discussion among selected participants does not show how the wider public formed its views or whose experiences the panel missed.

### Questions to take into practice

- The assembly could develop recommendations, while parliament and the electorate retained consequential decisions; these different roles gave the recommendations a route to action without making the panel sovereign.

- The quality of the discussion is a separate question from whether an observer favors the resulting constitutional change.

- A small deliberating body still needs its reasoning exposed to wider public disagreement.

### What remains uncertain

- How much the assembly changed referendum voters' views, compared with campaigning and longer-term attitude changes, remains uncertain.

- Referendum eligibility excluded some residents affected by abortion policy; recruitment, attrition, and agenda choices also shaped whose experience entered the discussion.

## Iceland: crowdsourcing a constitution without securing adoption

**Question:** What is the difference between contributing to a draft and controlling its adoption?

**Setting:** 2010–2013 · Iceland

**Technologies:** Constitutions and charters (T146), Referendums (T107), Public consultations (T105)

Following Iceland's financial crisis, a multistage constitutional process invited public input and produced a proposed constitution. Online discussion accompanied a council's drafting work rather than replacing it with an unfiltered public document. A 2012 advisory referendum supported using the proposal as a basis, but the process did not deliver an enacted replacement constitution.

### Outcome: Mixed outcomes

The process generated a substantive draft and online exchanges in which participants responded to one another, yet did not secure adoption. Participation was self-selected and uneven. Producing a draft through public contributions did not give those contributors control over the parliamentary decisions needed to turn it into a constitution.

### Documented observations

- The public platform used moderated, non-anonymous threads with topic-setting submissions and engagement from council members. [popescu](https://delibdemjournal.org/article/id/974/)

- The three longest online discussion threads contained responsive exchanges, but participation was concentrated among a self-selected group whose demographic composition was uncertain. [popescu](https://delibdemjournal.org/article/id/974/)

- An advisory referendum supported using the draft as a basis for a new constitution, but no final parliamentary adoption vote followed in the process's 2010–2013 period. [palsdottir](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/14789299231197157)

### Possible explanations

- **plausible:** Clear topics, moderation, and visible links to drafters plausibly encouraged substantive exchanges. [popescu](https://delibdemjournal.org/article/id/974/) **Alternative:** Highly interested, educated participants may have generated these qualities even without the platform's specific design.

- **plausible:** Participation without an enforceable or politically secured adoption pathway can produce influential text without institutional change. [palsdottir](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/14789299231197157) **Alternative:** Disagreement over provisions and legal process, rather than a general hostility to participation, could also explain resistance.

### Research connections

- **Deliberative quality versus decisiveness in participatory constitution-making — Qualitative and coded case evidence; dimensions explicitly distinguished.** A discussion may be deliberative without being representative, decisive, or just. [popescu](https://delibdemjournal.org/article/id/974/) **Transfer limit:** The three longest online threads reveal what sustained discussion could look like, but not what less active participants experienced or whether the public as a whole was represented.

### Questions to take into practice

- Public input can influence a draft without giving contributors authority over its adoption.

- An advisory referendum can endorse a direction while leaving later parliamentary decisions unresolved.

- A high comment count can coexist with influence concentrated among relatively few contributors.

### What remains uncertain

- The most sustained threads may have differed from shorter exchanges and from the experience of people who did not participate.

- The relative contributions of substantive disagreement, legal-process objections, and political resistance to the adoption failure remain difficult to separate.

### Sources

- **popescu:** [Judging Deliberation: An Assessment of the Crowdsourced Icelandic Constitutional Project](https://delibdemjournal.org/article/id/974/). Delia Popescu and Matthew Loveland / Journal of Deliberative Democracy · primary-research · accessed as sections. Methods, discussion, and representation limits reviewed; long-thread selection constrains inference.

- **palsdottir:** [Why Do Politicians Not Act Upon Citizens' Deliberations? Evidence From Iceland](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/14789299231197157). Valgerður Björk Pálsdóttir, Sergiu Gherghina and Paul Tap / Political Studies Review · primary-research · accessed as search-excerpt. ## Oregon: citizens writing a guide for other voters

**Question:** Can a deliberating panel improve public knowledge without deciding for the electorate?

**Setting:** 2010–2018 evaluated panels; commission hiatus continuing in the 2026 official record · Oregon, United States, with comparison trials elsewhere

**Technologies:** Citizens' assemblies (T102), Sortition (T108), Referendums (T107)

Citizens' Initiative Review convened a stratified sample of voters to question advocates and experts about ballot measures, deliberate, and write a short public statement. The panel informed voters rather than replacing the referendum. Oregon placed statements in its official voters' pamphlet, but the commission later went on hiatus despite evidence that reading the statements improved policy knowledge.

### Outcome: Mixed outcomes

Across survey experiments covering reviews in Oregon and elsewhere, readers of panel statements gained policy knowledge. That result concerns understanding after exposure to the statement, not whether every voter read it or whether the resulting policies were better. Oregon's commission had been on hiatus since 2018 as of the 2026 official record, separating the information product's value from the institution's ability to keep operating.

### Documented observations

- Panels considered evidence and competing arguments over four to five days, then produced factual findings and pro/con arguments for voters. [gastil](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0288188)

- Experiments spanning thirteen reviews and 10,872 registered voters found a positive effect of reading the statements on policy knowledge. [gastil](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0288188)

- As of 2026, the commission had been on hiatus since 2018, although the official voters' pamphlet procedures still provided for its statements. [manual](https://sos.oregon.gov/elections/Documents/voters-pamphlet-permanent-administrative-order-ELECT-1-2026.pdf)

### Possible explanations

- **supported-in-case:** A short synthesis vetted by peers can reduce the effort needed to learn about a ballot measure. [gastil](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0288188) **Alternative:** Part of the improvement may arise from receiving any concise relevant information, rather than deliberation or peer authorship specifically.

- **plausible:** Formal authorization is insufficient for durability if an institution lacks the resources or support to keep operating. [manual](https://sos.oregon.gov/elections/Documents/voters-pamphlet-permanent-administrative-order-ELECT-1-2026.pdf) **Alternative:** Inactivity could also reflect administrative or strategic choices; the existence of a hiatus does not identify which organizational commitment failed.

### Research connections

- **Deliberative mini-publics as trusted information intermediaries — Survey-experimental evidence for reader knowledge, narrower than overall democratic improvement.** The panel's output connects a small deliberative process to a much larger electorate. [gastil](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0288188) **Transfer limit:** Exposing research participants to a statement differs from ensuring that voters encounter and trust it in a real campaign.

### Questions to take into practice

- A panel can help other voters understand an issue while leaving the final vote with the electorate.

- Publication and readership are separate: a useful statement must also reach people amid competing campaign information.

- Legal authorization can remain in place while the organization needed to deliver the process stops operating.

### What remains uncertain

- The pooled knowledge result combines Oregon with other jurisdictions; effects specific to Oregon may differ.

- The causes of the commission's hiatus remain unresolved, including the relative roles of funding, organizational capacity, and political support.

### Sources

- **gastil:** [Deliberative panels as a source of public knowledge: A large-sample test of the Citizens' Initiative Review](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0288188). John Gastil and colleagues / PLOS ONE · primary-research · accessed as sections. Abstract and design/results overview; multiple experiments, not an adoption-outcome trial.

- **manual:** [State Voters' Pamphlet Manual: ELECT 1-2026](https://sos.oregon.gov/elections/Documents/voters-pamphlet-permanent-administrative-order-ELECT-1-2026.pdf). Oregon Secretary of State · institutional-record · accessed as search-excerpt. Commission section explicitly records hiatus and the statutory panel procedure.

## Nepal irrigation: better hardware was not enough

**Question:** Why could farmer-managed systems outperform more technically sophisticated agency-managed systems?

**Setting:** Comparative studies published in 1994 and 2012 · Nepal, multiple irrigation systems

**Technologies:** Commons governance (T245)

In Nepal, farmer-managed irrigation systems often operated with simpler physical works than agency-managed systems while devising and enforcing their own rules. Yet they could perform better. Water delivery depended not only on engineering but on who maintained the works, how water was allocated, and whether users could make one another keep their commitments.

### Outcome: Mixed outcomes

In the systems examined, farmer-managed irrigation had higher average performance and stronger local rule enforcement than agency-managed irrigation. The comparison points to the importance of organization alongside infrastructure. Differences in water availability, geography, and system size make it difficult to attribute the whole advantage to local management.

### Documented observations

- Farmer-managed systems tended to outperform state-operated systems despite comparatively low-tech infrastructure. [ostrom](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3233/HSM-1994-13305)

- Enforcement was stronger in the farmer-managed systems with locally devised rules than in the agency-managed systems with rules made by outside officials. [rules](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-institutional-economics/article/abs/rules-and-collective-action-an-institutional-analysis-of-the-performance-of-irrigation-systems-in-nepal/16500439FDC02AB318ED1891BE4ED2B9)

- The rules operated in communities with different beliefs and biophysical conditions; management type alone did not describe all the conditions affecting cooperation. [rules](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-institutional-economics/article/abs/rules-and-collective-action-an-institutional-analysis-of-the-performance-of-irrigation-systems-in-nepal/16500439FDC02AB318ED1891BE4ED2B9)

### Possible explanations

- **supported-in-case:** Locally workable rules and credible enforcement helped sustain contributions and coordination. [rules](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-institutional-economics/article/abs/rules-and-collective-action-an-institutional-analysis-of-the-performance-of-irrigation-systems-in-nepal/16500439FDC02AB318ED1891BE4ED2B9) **Alternative:** Water availability, system size, geography, and selection into management type could explain part of the performance difference.

- **plausible:** Infrastructure projects can disappoint when they alter physical works without preserving users' incentives to maintain and allocate water. [ostrom](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3233/HSM-1994-13305) [rules](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-institutional-economics/article/abs/rules-and-collective-action-an-institutional-analysis-of-the-performance-of-irrigation-systems-in-nepal/16500439FDC02AB318ED1891BE4ED2B9) **Alternative:** Insufficient funding, staff, or maintenance quality within agencies may matter more than external rulemaking itself.

### Research connections

- **Institutional Analysis and Development; institutional grammar applied to irrigation rules — Comparative observational research, with ADICO rule analysis in the later study.** Rules in use shape users' incentives to contribute maintenance, respect allocations, and respond to violations. [ostrom](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3233/HSM-1994-13305) [rules](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-institutional-economics/article/abs/rules-and-collective-action-an-institutional-analysis-of-the-performance-of-irrigation-systems-in-nepal/16500439FDC02AB318ED1891BE4ED2B9) **Transfer limit:** Local management still needs to handle water flows, infrastructure, and disputes beyond an individual community. Strong local organization and public technical support can serve different parts of the same system.

### Questions to take into practice

- Upgrading physical works can leave water delivery unreliable if maintenance and allocation responsibilities no longer fit users' incentives.

- Rules written by an agency and rules people actually enforce can describe different operating systems.

- Average performance can conceal different outcomes for users near the water source and those at the tail end.

### What remains uncertain

- The systems were not randomly assigned to management arrangements, so geography, water supply, and prior community organization may explain some of the observed difference.

- The distribution of gains between head-end and tail-end users remains important even where average system performance is higher.

- **ostrom:** [The Performance of Self-Governing Irrigation Systems in Nepal](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3233/HSM-1994-13305). Elinor Ostrom, Wai Fung Lam and Myungsuk Lee / Human Systems Management · primary-research · accessed as abstract. Comparative performance puzzle; no numerical causal effect claimed.

- **rules:** [Rules and collective action: an institutional analysis of the performance of irrigation systems in Nepal](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-institutional-economics/article/abs/rules-and-collective-action-an-institutional-analysis-of-the-performance-of-irrigation-systems-in-nepal/16500439FDC02AB318ED1891BE4ED2B9). ## Törbel: a commons with boundaries, private property, and exclusions

**Question:** How can collective ownership work together with private holdings—and reproduce inequalities?

**Setting:** Historical commoner institutions described in twentieth-century research · Törbel, Valais, Switzerland

**Technologies:** Commons governance (T245)

Törbel's alpine economy combined private household land with collectively managed pasture, forest, and irrigation resources. Rules linked summer grazing to the animals households could support in winter and organized maintenance obligations. This was neither open access nor an entirely communal economy: belonging, property, and participation rights determined who could benefit and influence decisions.

### Outcome: Mixed outcomes

Collective management of alpine resources endured alongside private household holdings. Grazing access and maintenance obligations linked households to the commons, while membership boundaries excluded outsiders. Durability therefore coexisted with unequal rights; the arrangement was not open access or an equal claim for every resident.

### Documented observations

- Privately held fields coexisted with common alpine resources; grazing access was constrained by households' winter-support capacity and linked to maintenance responsibilities. [ostrom](https://aikon.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/12/Elinor-Ostrom-Governing-the-Commons_-The-Evolution-of-Institutions-for-Collective-Action-Cambridge-University-Press-2015.pdf)

- Household property, collective work, and membership rights operated together rather than forming separate private and communal economies. [haller](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0308275X251315266)

- Membership boundaries meant that access to common resources did not extend automatically to every resident or newcomer. [haller](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0308275X251315266)

### Possible explanations

- **supported-in-case:** Tying resource access to locally supportable livestock plausibly limited an individual's ability to expand grazing costs onto others. [ostrom](https://aikon.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/12/Elinor-Ostrom-Governing-the-Commons_-The-Evolution-of-Institutions-for-Collective-Action-Cambridge-University-Press-2015.pdf) [haller](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0308275X251315266) **Alternative:** Ecological limits, household labor, and limited markets also constrained herd expansion, making the grazing rule's separate contribution difficult to establish.

- **plausible:** Durable commons may need to renegotiate their relationships with markets and public authorities as well as maintain their own rules. [haller](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0308275X251315266) **Alternative:** Continuing local obligations and ecological constraints may preserve some arrangements even as outside institutions change; their relative importance differs across places.

### Research connections

- **Ostrom's common-pool resource governance, qualified by historical political ecology — Historical case synthesis and critical comparative interpretation.** Boundaries and fitted rules can support coordination while leaving distributional and power questions unresolved. [ostrom](https://aikon.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/12/Elinor-Ostrom-Governing-the-Commons_-The-Evolution-of-Institutions-for-Collective-Action-Cambridge-University-Press-2015.pdf) [haller](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0308275X251315266) **Transfer limit:** A membership rule that protects a resource can be exclusionary; institutional survival does not establish an ethically acceptable franchise.

### Questions to take into practice

- Grazing rights were connected to household capacity and collective maintenance, making access depend on obligations as well as ownership.

- A well-maintained commons can still exclude people from benefits or a voice in its rules.

- Private holdings and shared resources can support one another rather than requiring a wholly private or wholly communal economy.

### What remains uncertain

- The historical arrangement does not settle how Törbel's present-day resource use and participation have changed with markets, labor availability, and public policy.

- The extent to which excluded households bore costs or found alternative access is important to judging the distribution of benefits.

### Sources

- **ostrom:** [Governing the Commons: The Evolution of Institutions for Collective Action](https://aikon.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/12/Elinor-Ostrom-Governing-the-Commons_-The-Evolution-of-Institutions-for-Collective-Action-Cambridge-University-Press-2015.pdf). Elinor Ostrom / Cambridge University Press · review · accessed as search-excerpt. Törbel passages on wintering, maintenance, and private/common property; full PDF fetch timed out.

- **haller:** [Swiss commoners' struggles for the right balance: Ontologies, identities and responses to market and state pressures](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0308275X251315266). Tobias Haller / Critique of Anthropology · primary-research · accessed as sections. Historical Törbel discussion and contemporary comparative framing; current cases are Uri and Olivone.

## Valencia: local water justice backed by wider authority

**Question:** How do user institutions combine local knowledge with public legal recognition?

**Setting:** Historical irrigation institutions; UNESCO recognition in 2009 and historical reassessment in 2015 · Valencia region, Spain

**Technologies:** Commons governance (T245), Courts (T318), Elections (T109)

Valencian irrigation communities developed arrangements for allocating scarce water and resolving disputes. The Water Tribunal is a visible part of this wider system, recognized in Spanish law and associated with elected irrigator representatives. Local institutions interacted with municipal and higher authorities; their long history included changes in property, membership, and political power.

### Outcome: Mixed outcomes

User institutions endured and retained meaningful local autonomy within a wider legal order. That continuity does not establish that irrigators always had equal power: claims of uniformly peasant, democratic, and unchanged governance across the thirteenth to eighteenth centuries remain contested.

### Documented observations

- Irrigators' tribunals, including Valencia's Water Tribunal, have legal recognition, elected members, and oral dispute-settlement procedures. [unesco](https://ich.unesco.org/doc/src/06859-EN.pdf)

- Irrigation communities exercised local autonomy while interacting with municipal and higher authorities. [peris](https://revista-asyd.org/index.php/asyd/en/article/view/234)

- Claims that the thirteenth-to-eighteenth-century institutions were uniformly peasant, democratic, or unchanged remain historically contested. [peris](https://revista-asyd.org/index.php/asyd/en/article/view/234)

### Possible explanations

- **plausible:** Locally knowledgeable dispute resolution, backed by legal recognition, plausibly lowers the difficulty of enforcing irrigation agreements. [unesco](https://ich.unesco.org/doc/src/06859-EN.pdf) [peris](https://revista-asyd.org/index.php/asyd/en/article/view/234) **Alternative:** Landownership power and public authorities' coercive capacity may also sustain compliance without equal influence among users.

- **supported-in-case:** The institutional history may be better understood as recurrent adaptation than as preservation of a single ancient blueprint. [peris](https://revista-asyd.org/index.php/asyd/en/article/view/234) **Alternative:** Some procedural continuities may remain important even when membership, property relations, and surrounding government change.

### Research connections

- **Nested commons governance in the Maass–Glick–Ostrom tradition — Historically reassessed explanatory framework, not a controlled outcome test.** Local self-organization and larger-scale authority can complement each other. [peris](https://revista-asyd.org/index.php/asyd/en/article/view/234) **Transfer limit:** Legal recognition and long survival leave separate questions about who could bring a claim, whose interests shaped decisions, and whether judgments distributed water fairly.

### Questions to take into practice

- Local dispute settlement can depend on recognition and enforcement beyond the community itself.

- A continuing procedure can operate through major changes in who owns land and who holds influence.

- Elected irrigator representatives do not by themselves reveal the position of tenants, smallholders, or users outside the recognized membership.

### What remains uncertain

- How accessible, timely, and impartial the tribunal is for different present-day users remains a separate question from its historical endurance.

- Changes in landownership and representation make it difficult to infer a continuous distribution of power from the survival of a named institution.

## Alanya: taking turns at the best fishing spots

**Question:** When can a lottery and rotation replace competition for a scarce location?

**Setting:** Late 1960s–1989 · Alanya, Mediterranean coast of Turkey

**Technologies:** Commons governance (T245), Sortition (T108)

Alanya's small-boat fishers faced a narrow band of productive net-setting locations. They developed a locally agreed system that named sites, assigned starting positions by lot, and rotated access. The arrangement addressed interference and competition among participating fishers; it was not a comprehensive solution to every source of pressure on marine stocks.

### Outcome: Mixed outcomes

The historical account describes workable coordination and more evenly shared access to valuable sites. Its scope was limited: outside recreational users lacked equivalent rules, legal exclusion powers were uncertain, and informal enforcement included coercive pressure. Favorable coastal geography also reduced interference from trawlers, complicating any claim that the rules alone caused success.

### Documented observations

- Licensed local fishers could join the allocation regardless of cooperative membership; a lottery established starting positions and rotation shared valuable sites. [berkes-chapter](https://library.uniteddiversity.coop/Cooperatives/Multi-Stakeholder_Co-ops/Making_the_Commons_Work-Theory_Practice_and_Policy.pdf)

- The agreement was deposited with local authorities, but fishers doubted they could legally remove a violator's access; informal pressure was important. [berkes-chapter](https://library.uniteddiversity.coop/Cooperatives/Multi-Stakeholder_Co-ops/Making_the_Commons_Work-Theory_Practice_and_Policy.pdf)

- The local allocation arrangement addressed participating small-boat fishers' use of sites, while competition involving other fishing sectors exceeded that agreement's boundaries. [berkes-1986](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/0308597X86900540)

### Possible explanations

- **supported-in-case:** Rotation made accepting a less desirable site today more tolerable by offering future access to better sites. [berkes-chapter](https://library.uniteddiversity.coop/Cooperatives/Multi-Stakeholder_Co-ops/Making_the_Commons_Work-Theory_Practice_and_Policy.pdf) **Alternative:** Small-group familiarity and the ability to observe neighboring nets may have been equally important to compliance.

- **plausible:** The arrangement's apparent success partly depended on a problem boundary favorable to local control. [berkes-1986](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/0308597X86900540) [berkes-chapter](https://library.uniteddiversity.coop/Cooperatives/Multi-Stakeholder_Co-ops/Making_the_Commons_Work-Theory_Practice_and_Policy.pdf) **Alternative:** Other fishing communities might develop broader agreements; heterogeneous users do not make coordination inherently impossible.

### Research connections

- **Common-pool resource appropriation rules and conditional local self-governance — Comparative field interpretation with limited outcome measurement.** Sharing opportunities over time can reduce a scramble for a spatially scarce resource. [berkes-1986](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/0308597X86900540) **Transfer limit:** More orderly site allocation does not establish sustainable fish stocks. It also leaves questions about excluded users and coercive methods of securing compliance.

### Questions to take into practice

- Rotation let participants accept a poor location today because their turn at better sites would come later.

- The fishers could coordinate access to net-setting sites without controlling every source of pressure on fish stocks.

- Informal enforcement included pressure whose fairness and legitimacy matter separately from whether the allocation system functioned.

### What remains uncertain

- Whether the rotation persisted under later changes in fishing pressure, membership, and coastal use is unresolved.

- The historical allocation account does not establish the resulting condition of fish stocks or whether excluded users benefited or lost access.

### Sources

- **berkes-chapter:** [Success and Failure in Marine Coastal Fisheries of Turkey (in Making the Commons Work)](https://library.uniteddiversity.coop/Cooperatives/Multi-Stakeholder_Co-ops/Making_the_Commons_Work-Theory_Practice_and_Policy.pdf). Fikret Berkes / ICS Press · primary-research · accessed as sections. Alanya operational rules and enforcement, printed pages 170–171; adjacent fisheries reveal limits.

## Montreal Protocol: rules, finance, and measurements that can expose cheating

**Question:** How can international cooperation combine obligations with assistance and independent feedback?

**Setting:** 1987–2022 assessment; unexpected emissions episode in the 2010s · Global

**Technologies:** Grants (T229), Diplomacy (S013)

The Montreal Protocol connected controls on ozone-depleting substances with scientific assessment and financial assistance for eligible developing countries. Its social architecture includes differentiated obligations and a fund for compliance costs. Atmospheric measurements supplied an external check on reported activity, including evidence of unexpected emissions that complicated the otherwise strong record of reducing controlled substances.

### Outcome: Bounded success

By the 2022 assessment, controlled ozone-depleting substances were declining and ozone recovery was progressing. Unexpected CFC-11 emissions showed that agreed phase-outs had not eliminated noncompliance. Subsequent declines were encouraging, but observing-network gaps and emissions from stored chemicals continued to complicate judgments about complete compliance and recovery timing.

### Documented observations

- Article 10 establishes financial and technical cooperation, including a Multilateral Fund covering agreed incremental costs for eligible parties. [article10](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol/articles/article-10-financial-mechanism)

- Atmospheric measurements showed declining controlled ozone-depleting substances and progress toward ozone recovery. [assessment](https://csl.noaa.gov/assessments/ozone/2022/executivesummary/)

- Unexpected CFC-11 emissions triggered investigations and subsequently declined, while gaps in monitoring left uncertainty about emissions elsewhere. [assessment](https://csl.noaa.gov/assessments/ozone/2022/executivesummary/)

### Possible explanations

- **plausible:** Financial assistance plausibly made compliance more feasible and politically acceptable where transition costs otherwise threatened participation. [article10](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol/articles/article-10-financial-mechanism) **Alternative:** Available substitutes, commercial incentives, and domestic regulation also supported phase-outs; the financial mechanism operated alongside these changes.

- **supported-in-case:** Independent atmospheric measurement allowed the regime to detect problems that production reports alone could miss. [assessment](https://csl.noaa.gov/assessments/ozone/2022/executivesummary/) **Alternative:** Detection does not by itself explain subsequent reductions; enforcement, publicity, and producer decisions may have jointly contributed.

### Research connections

No dedicated theory connection assessed.

### Questions to take into practice

- Shared obligations became more feasible when the agreement also addressed eligible countries' transition costs.

- Atmospheric measurements provided a check beyond countries' production reports.

- Unexpected emissions required investigation and response after the treaty was already operating; signing an agreement was not the end of coordination.

### What remains uncertain

- Observing-network gaps leave uncertainty about the location and scale of some emissions.

- Stored chemicals can continue to leak after new production is controlled, so phase-out dates and atmospheric recovery dates need not coincide.

- The relative contributions of finance, substitutes, commercial incentives, and domestic enforcement remain difficult to separate.

## Alaska fishing quotas: ending the race while narrowing entry

**Question:** How can a rule improve operations while redistributing access across people and generations?

**Setting:** 1995–2023 outcomes, reviewed in 2020 and 2024 · Alaska halibut and sablefish fisheries, United States

**Technologies:** Tradable quotas (T244)

Alaska's individual fishing quota program assigned shares of allowable harvest, replacing a compressed competitive season with more individually planned fishing. Designers added protections intended to retain small vessels, working owners, crews, and coastal communities. Over time, fishing became less compressed while access to quota and participation in the fleet consolidated.

### Outcome: Mixed outcomes

Fishing seasons lengthened and some conditions improved for remaining crews. Quota ownership and vessel participation consolidated, with unequal consequences across communities and generations. Entry barriers and transfer provisions mattered alongside changing catches and prices; the program did not produce one uniform experience for everyone formerly connected to the fisheries.

### Documented observations

- Fishing shifted from very short seasons to seasons lasting more than eight months. [retrospective](https://spo.nmfs.noaa.gov/sites/default/files/pdf-content/mfr821-21.pdf)

- Conditions improved for some remaining crews, while access and benefits became uneven across generations and communities despite protective provisions. [retrospective](https://spo.nmfs.noaa.gov/sites/default/files/pdf-content/mfr821-21.pdf)

- Fleet and quota-holder consolidation continued through the period reviewed in 2024, alongside changes in environmental conditions, catch limits, prices, and ownership caps. [review2024](https://meetings.npfmc.org/CommentReview/DownloadFile?fileName=D5+IFQ+Program+Review+-+Copy.pdf&p=e3822051-cff0-405b-9c9c-ba192bb3c41c.pdf)

### Possible explanations

- **supported-in-case:** Secure individual allocations reduced incentives to rush for a common seasonal catch before competitors took it. [retrospective](https://spo.nmfs.noaa.gov/sites/default/files/pdf-content/mfr821-21.pdf) **Alternative:** Other operational regulations and market changes also influenced timing and safety, so the changes cannot be assigned entirely to individual quota allocations.

- **supported-in-case:** Transferable rights and valuable scarce quotas can shift access toward capital and away from new entrants even when total harvest is controlled. [retrospective](https://spo.nmfs.noaa.gov/sites/default/files/pdf-content/mfr821-21.pdf) [review2024](https://meetings.npfmc.org/CommentReview/DownloadFile?fileName=D5+IFQ+Program+Review+-+Copy.pdf&p=e3822051-cff0-405b-9c9c-ba192bb3c41c.pdf) **Alternative:** Declining catch availability and rising operating costs can make entry harder independently of ownership rules.

### Research connections

- **Catch-share incentives versus distributional objectives — Longitudinal descriptive evaluation, with explicit confounding influences.** A rule addressing the race to fish simultaneously creates assets whose allocation affects livelihoods. [retrospective](https://spo.nmfs.noaa.gov/sites/default/files/pdf-content/mfr821-21.pdf) [review2024](https://meetings.npfmc.org/CommentReview/DownloadFile?fileName=D5+IFQ+Program+Review+-+Copy.pdf&p=e3822051-cff0-405b-9c9c-ba192bb3c41c.pdf) **Transfer limit:** Reduced vessel numbers are neither automatically a social failure nor proof of healthy fish stocks; affected groups and ecological outcomes need distinct measures.

### Questions to take into practice

- Initial quota allocation created valuable assets, making later entry a different problem from the original allocation.

- Improved conditions among remaining participants can coexist with lost access for people who leave or cannot enter.

- Protective ownership and vessel rules need to be judged by their effects across communities, not only by whether they exist.

### What remains uncertain

- Quota ownership, changing catch availability, and rising operating costs all affected entry; their separate contributions are difficult to establish.

- Longer seasons and fewer vessels describe operational changes, not a demonstrated quota-caused recovery of biological stocks.

Marysia Szymkowiak, Sarah Marrinan and Stephen Kasperski / NOAA Marine Fisheries Review · primary-research · accessed as sections. Abstract, methods cautions, and capacity/distribution findings reviewed.

## New Zealand quotas: owning fishing assets is not the same as being able to fish

**Question:** When do formally allocated rights fail to translate into practical access or self-determination?

**Setting:** 1986 quota system; 1992 settlement and subsequent development examined through 2024 · Aotearoa New Zealand

**Technologies:** Tradable quotas (T244)

New Zealand's quota system divides allowable commercial harvest into shares and annual catch entitlements. Its development intersected with Māori rights claims and a settlement transferring assets and governance powers. The case distinguishes a formal allocation from the practical ability to use it.

### Outcome: Mixed outcomes

The system limits and allocates catches, and Māori entities acquired valuable assets. Yet small-scale fishers continued to face regulatory and market barriers. Collective ownership of quota therefore did not necessarily translate into fishing livelihoods for particular households, and neither measure alone establishes ecological recovery or self-determination.

### Documented observations

- The official system allocates annual catch entitlement according to quota ownership and the commercial catch limit for a stock. [mpi](https://www.mpi.govt.nz/fishing-aquaculture/fisheries-management/quota-management-system)

- Initial allocation disadvantaged some part-time Māori fishers; later settlement arrangements transferred fishing rights and assets to Māori entities. [bodwitch](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/marine-science/articles/10.3389/fmars.2024.1297975/full)

- Regulatory and market-access constraints continued after those reallocations, limiting some fishers' ability to turn rights into practical fishing opportunities. [bodwitch](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/marine-science/articles/10.3389/fmars.2024.1297975/full)

### Possible explanations

- **supported-in-case:** Collective asset income can coexist with limited fishing opportunities for particular households. [bodwitch](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/marine-science/articles/10.3389/fmars.2024.1297975/full) **Alternative:** Collective dividend strategies may deliberately prioritize all beneficiaries rather than maximize employment for a smaller subset of fishers.

- **plausible:** Tradability does not alone explain conservation gains, since limits and initial allocation changes can affect pressure before a quota market operates. [mpi](https://www.mpi.govt.nz/fishing-aquaculture/fisheries-management/quota-management-system) [bodwitch](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/marine-science/articles/10.3389/fmars.2024.1297975/full) **Alternative:** Market reallocation may still improve later efficiency; establishing that requires separating its effect from catch limits and enforcement.

### Research connections

- **Indigenous self-determination and the distinction between formal rights and access to benefits — Comparative qualitative research, not a universal rejection of quota systems.** Formal ownership and practical access are different. [bodwitch](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/marine-science/articles/10.3389/fmars.2024.1297975/full) **Transfer limit:** Treaty obligations and colonial history preclude treating iwi simply as investor groups.

### Questions to take into practice

- An entity can hold a valuable right while individual households lack the means to use it.

- Income from collectively owned assets, fishing work, food access, and political authority are different benefits and can move in different directions.

- Allocation criteria can preserve historical exclusions even when they appear administratively neutral.

### What remains uncertain

- Biological outcomes vary by stock and cannot be inferred from the value of quota assets or their ownership.

- Different iwi and households may experience the system differently; a single national average would conceal differences in assets, strategy, and practical access.

- **mpi:** [Quota Management System](https://www.mpi.govt.nz/fishing-aquaculture/fisheries-management/quota-management-system). New Zealand Ministry for Primary Industries · institutional-record · accessed as search-excerpt. Official operational explanation of quota, annual catch entitlement, and catch limits.

- **bodwitch:** [Indigenous self-determination in fisheries governance: implications from New Zealand and Atlantic Canada](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/marine-science/articles/10.3389/fmars.2024.1297975/full). Hekia Bodwitch and colleagues / Frontiers in Marine Science · primary-research · accessed as sections. ## Nepal community forests: environmental gains without equal voice

**Question:** Can decentralization reduce forest loss and poverty while leaving local elites in charge?

**Setting:** National outcomes 2000–2012; leadership research published in 2015 · Nepal, national comparison and Baglung district leadership study

**Technologies:** Commons governance (T245)

Nepal transferred day-to-day forest management responsibilities to community forest user groups. Deforestation and poverty declined on average in a national comparison, while a separate examination of leadership in Baglung found executive roles dominated by relatively wealthy and higher-caste people. Environmental and livelihood gains could coexist with unequal decision power.

### Outcome: Mixed outcomes

The national analysis estimated improvements in both forest and poverty outcomes on average, with substantial variation. The Baglung study found executive roles dominated by relatively wealthy and higher-caste people. These findings are compatible: a program can improve aggregate outcomes while still reproducing inequalities in voice and access.

### Documented observations

- A national analysis combining poverty, forest-change, and community-forest information estimated relative reductions of 37% in deforestation and 4.3% in poverty associated with community forestry over 2000–2012. [national](https://hummedia.manchester.ac.uk/institutes/gdi/research/Deforestation-poverty-Nepal-research-brief.pdf)

- Benefits varied, with poorer areas less effective at reducing deforestation and larger or older community forests showing greater gains. [national](https://hummedia.manchester.ac.uk/institutes/gdi/research/Deforestation-poverty-Nepal-research-brief.pdf)

- In Baglung, executive committees were dominated by relatively wealthy and higher-caste members. Prior NGO leadership experience was associated with greater access to these roles for disadvantaged people. [leadership](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/forpol/v52y2015icp27-38.html)

### Possible explanations

- **plausible:** Local management authority may help users establish resource rules that fit local conditions and support joint environmental and livelihood gains. [national](https://hummedia.manchester.ac.uk/institutes/gdi/research/Deforestation-poverty-Nepal-research-brief.pdf) **Alternative:** Selection into community forestry and unobserved changes in livelihoods or government support could explain some estimated gains.

- **supported-in-case:** Leadership experience may help disadvantaged people cross barriers to formal decision roles. [leadership](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/forpol/v52y2015icp27-38.html) **Alternative:** People obtaining NGO experience may already differ in confidence, networks, or resources; the association does not establish a training effect.

### Research connections

- **Decentralized commons governance with distributional and elite-capture analysis — National observational impact analysis combined with a distinct local leadership study.** Environmental effectiveness and democratic inclusion are separate dimensions of institutional performance. [national](https://hummedia.manchester.ac.uk/institutes/gdi/research/Deforestation-poverty-Nepal-research-brief.pdf) [leadership](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/forpol/v52y2015icp27-38.html) **Transfer limit:** The Baglung sample cannot be treated as a census of all Nepalese user groups, nor can national averages describe every household.

### Questions to take into practice

- Reduced deforestation, reduced poverty, and a more inclusive leadership are different outcomes; improvement in one does not establish improvement in the others.

- Membership and meeting attendance can coexist with leadership concentrated among advantaged people.

- Poorer communities may need resources and support that better-established groups already possess.

### What remains uncertain

- Selection into community forestry and changes in livelihoods or government support may explain part of the estimated national gains.

- The national comparison and Baglung leadership findings concern different populations and periods; they do not establish which particular groups combined environmental gains with elite control.

### Sources

- **national:** [Community forest management simultaneously reduces poverty and deforestation in Nepal](https://hummedia.manchester.ac.uk/institutes/gdi/research/Deforestation-poverty-Nepal-research-brief.pdf). Johan Oldekop and colleagues / University of Manchester · primary-research · accessed as full-text. Authors' two-page summary of the Nature Sustainability study; relative percentages, heterogeneity, and design overview.

- **leadership:** [How can poor and disadvantaged households get an opportunity to become a leader in community forestry in Nepal?](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/forpol/v52y2015icp27-38.html). ## Uganda forest monitoring: watching one place can move the damage elsewhere

**Question:** What happens when a local intervention changes where people extract resources rather than how much?

**Setting:** Randomized trial reported in 2021 · 110 villages, Uganda

**Technologies:** Commons governance (T245)

Community forest monitoring was introduced in a randomized trial across Ugandan villages. Resource use was measured inside and outside monitored areas, allowing changes beyond the intervention boundary to become visible. Watching particular forest areas did not necessarily reduce the pressure on forests overall.

### Outcome: Failure on the stated objective

The trial detected no improvement in its main forest-use index. Forest loss increased in unmonitored areas relative to controls, consistent with some extraction moving outside the monitored boundary, although displacement is not the only possible explanation. Across a broader six-country study including this trial, monitoring produced modest average benefits, so the Ugandan result differed from the pooled result.

### Documented observations

- The trial randomized monitoring across 110 villages and combined ground measurements with satellite observations. [uganda](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34253600/)

- The main forest-use index showed no treatment effect, while unmonitored areas near treatment and neighboring villages experienced relative increases in forest loss. [uganda](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34253600/)

- A harmonized six-country study, which includes the Uganda experiment, found modest average improvements in resource use, knowledge, and satisfaction across settings. [multisite](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34253604/)

### Possible explanations

- **plausible:** Fear of sanctions may have shifted some extraction beyond monitored boundaries instead of reducing demand. [uganda](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34253600/) **Alternative:** Other changes affecting unmonitored areas could have contributed to the increase. Random assignment identifies effects of the intervention more clearly than it identifies why forest loss shifted.

- **plausible:** Monitoring works differently when users can move pressure to nearby unobserved resources. [uganda](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34253600/) [multisite](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34253604/) **Alternative:** Differences in implementation, resource type, authority, or incentives may explain cross-site variation rather than spatial leakage alone.

### Research connections

- **Commons monitoring as a testable institutional component; spillover and displacement effects — Randomized treatment evidence for measured outcomes, weaker identification of the displacement mechanism.** A design condition can have conditional effects, and local improvement is not equivalent to net environmental improvement. [uganda](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34253600/) [multisite](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34253604/) **Transfer limit:** The trial tests a particular externally supported monitoring intervention, not a complete long-standing commons institution.

### Questions to take into practice

- An intervention's environmental effects can extend beyond the area where it operates; observing only the monitored forest would miss part of this result.

- Installing a monitoring arrangement and reducing total extraction are different achievements.

- Nearby unmonitored resources can become attractive when the risks or costs of extraction rise inside the monitored area.

### What remains uncertain

- Whether the increase outside monitored areas reflected displaced extraction, other behavioral changes, or additional local factors remains uncertain.

- The durability of the effects after the trial and their consequences for households dependent on forest resources remain unresolved.

### Sources

- **uganda:** [Can community monitoring save the commons? Evidence on forest use and displacement](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34253600/). Sabrina Eisenbarth, Louis Graham and Anouk Rigterink / PNAS · primary-research · accessed as abstract. Randomized Uganda results and explicitly tentative displacement explanation; figure descriptions also inspected.

- **multisite:** [Adoption of community monitoring improves common pool resource management across contexts](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34253604/). Tara Slough and colleagues / PNAS · primary-research · accessed as abstract. Harmonized trials in six countries; contains Uganda trial and therefore is not independent confirmation.

## Sierra Juárez: winning community control did not remove market constraints

**Question:** How do collective resource institutions emerge through political struggle rather than simply being designed?

**Setting:** 1940–1983 concessions and transition; later community management examined through 2020 · Sierra Juárez, Oaxaca, Mexico

**Technologies:** Commons governance (T245), Coalitions (T203)

Sierra Juárez forest communities confronted state-supported forestry concessions and later gained greater control over management. An imposed association of communities struggled, while community mobilization and changing forestry laws helped open a route to local control. Acquiring authority was only one step: communities still had to organize production and find viable markets.

### Outcome: Mixed outcomes

Community organization helped change control over forest resources and supported durable management arrangements. Earlier imposed associations struggled with unequal exploitation and financial management. Later communities faced transport costs, market competition, and limited timber income, so stronger political control did not bring uniform commercial success.

### Documented observations

- State concessions placed forestry under FAPATUX, while an imposed association of forest communities failed amid unequal exploitation and financial-management problems. [history](https://www.halacsolcha.org/index.php/halac/article/view/992)

- Community mobilization, the ending of concessions, and the development of local management marked major changes in control over the region's forests. [appropriation](https://revistas.chapingo.mx/textual/article/download/r.textual.2020.75.04/r.textual.2020.75.04/1239)

- Stronger community governance coexisted with persistent production costs, transport difficulties, and limited income gains from timber and certification. [appropriation](https://revistas.chapingo.mx/textual/article/download/r.textual.2020.75.04/r.textual.2020.75.04/1239)

### Possible explanations

- **supported-in-case:** Locally grounded collective organization made it possible to contest externally assigned control and sustain management after concessions. [appropriation](https://revistas.chapingo.mx/textual/article/download/r.textual.2020.75.04/r.textual.2020.75.04/1239) **Alternative:** Changes in national law, political opportunities, and external support also enabled the transition; organization alone was not sufficient.

- **plausible:** An imposed multi-community organization struggled when resource contributions and benefits differed without accepted arrangements for managing those differences. [history](https://www.halacsolcha.org/index.php/halac/article/view/992) **Alternative:** Administrative weaknesses, state-company incentives, and market conditions could explain failures apart from intercommunity inequality.

### Research connections

- **Community appropriation and historically situated forest governance — Historical and policy analysis rather than an experimentally isolated governance effect.** Control over resources is produced through conflict, law, and collective organization, not only internal operational rules. [history](https://www.halacsolcha.org/index.php/halac/article/view/992) [appropriation](https://revistas.chapingo.mx/textual/article/download/r.textual.2020.75.04/r.textual.2020.75.04/1239) **Transfer limit:** Community land institutions and the legal history of concessions shaped which claims could succeed. Internal rules alone would not give another community equivalent authority over a resource.

### Questions to take into practice

- The authority to manage a resource may have to be won through political organization and legal change before local procedures can matter.

- Control over forests and the ability to earn sustainable income from them are separate problems.

- A multi-community organization needs accepted ways to handle unequal resource contributions and benefits.

### What remains uncertain

- The regional trajectory combines communities with different resources and histories; their gains and difficulties were not identical.

- The relationship between changes in control and subsequent forest-cover outcomes remains distinct from the evidence of political and organizational change.

- Unequal benefits, administrative weaknesses, and state-company incentives may all have contributed to the imposed association's failure.

Gabriela Acosta Espino and Alma Amalia González Cabañas / HALAC · history · accessed as abstract. Concessions, FAPATUX, and imposed association; archival references visible but full archival evidence not independently checked.

## Bologna: giving citizen projects a recognized relationship with city government

**Question:** Can legal agreements turn informal care for shared places into sustained public collaboration?

**Setting:** 2014 regulation onward; analysis published in 2026 · Bologna, Italy

**Technologies:** Commons governance (T245), Community agreements (T083)

Bologna's 2014 regulation created a framework for citizens and the municipality to agree on caring for and regenerating urban commons. Collaboration pacts made responsibilities and support discussable within a recognized public process. The framework supported extensive participation, while delivering and sustaining environmental or economic changes remained a further challenge.

### Outcome: Mixed outcomes

More than 800 pacts were activated after 2014. By the 2026 assessment, governance and participation were more developed than environmental and economic implementation. The agreements created a durable way to organize collaboration, but their number alone did not show whether assets were maintained, benefits were distributed equally, or climate goals were achieved.

### Documented observations

- The regulation defines collaboration between citizens and the municipality for the care and regeneration of tangible, intangible, and digital urban commons. [regulation](https://www.comune.bologna.it/media/files/bolognaregulation.pdf)

- More than 800 pacts had been activated since 2014, and the collaboration arrangements had connections to Bologna's climate-contract work by 2026. [study](https://cris.unibo.it/bitstream/11585/1054294/1/_Full%20paper%20published.pdf)

- The qualitative and indicator-based assessment found environmental and economic implementation less developed than governance and participation; it identified a pattern rather than a causal effect of the pacts. [study](https://cris.unibo.it/bitstream/11585/1054294/1/_Full%20paper%20published.pdf)

### Possible explanations

- **plausible:** A recognized agreement framework plausibly reduces uncertainty about how officials and citizen groups can work together. [regulation](https://www.comune.bologna.it/media/files/bolognaregulation.pdf) **Alternative:** Bologna's existing civic organizations and administrative capacity may explain much of the observed collaboration.

- **supported-in-case:** Participation infrastructure can expand faster than the money, technical capability, and maintenance needed for downstream outcomes. [study](https://cris.unibo.it/bitstream/11585/1054294/1/_Full%20paper%20published.pdf) **Alternative:** Some environmental effects may take longer to appear or may not be captured by the indicators used.

### Research connections

- **City as a Commons and collaborative urban governance — Conceptual framework applied through qualitative trajectory analysis and an interpretive indicator model.** Government provided a recognized framework for residents to share stewardship, rather than acting only as an owner or purchaser of services. [study](https://cris.unibo.it/bitstream/11585/1054294/1/_Full%20paper%20published.pdf) **Transfer limit:** Unpaid participation requires time and resources that residents possess unequally. A pact can add civic capacity, but it can also shift work onto residents that a public service would otherwise provide.

### Questions to take into practice

- An agreement to begin work needs resources, maintenance responsibilities, and a way to handle withdrawal if it is to sustain a shared place.

- Signed pacts show that collaboration is taking place; completed and maintained outcomes reveal what it delivers.

- The distribution of unpaid work matters when judging whether collaboration adds capacity or transfers public obligations to residents.

### What remains uncertain

- The persistence and quality of maintenance across individual pacts remain different questions from the number activated.

- Environmental changes may take longer to appear or fall outside the chosen indicators, complicating comparisons with participation outcomes.

- How unpaid labor, municipal support, and benefits are distributed across neighborhoods remains unresolved.

Purpose and definitions reviewed; legacy document endpoint now redirects, so current legal text requires separate verification.

## Chile: an inclusive drafting process still had to persuade the electorate

**Question:** How can a constitution-making process lose the consent needed to enact its result?

**Setting:** 2021–2023, focusing on the 2022 proposal and its rejection · Chile

**Technologies:** Constitutions and charters (T146), Elections (T109), Referendums (T107)

Chile elected a constitutional convention and subjected its proposed replacement constitution to a national referendum. The process expanded representation and participation in drafting, but the electorate rejected the 2022 text. A differently organized second process also ended in rejection in 2023, making it difficult to reduce the episode to one procedural flaw or ideological explanation.

### Outcome: Failure on the stated objective

Both processes ended without an enacted replacement constitution. Voters rejected the texts through the final decision assigned to them: 61.86% voted to reject in the final preliminary 2022 tally, and 55.76% voted against the second proposal in the equivalent 2023 bulletin. Fragmented drafting and difficult bargaining may help explain the first result, but the second rejection under a different process makes a single procedural explanation inadequate.

### Documented observations

- Servel's final preliminary 2022 tally reported 61.86% voting to reject the convention's proposal. [servel2022](https://www-qa.servel.cl/2022/09/05/servel-realiza-balance-del-plebiscito-constitucional/)

- The first convention combined party-less independents, a comparatively weak right-wing bloc, and decentralized public drafting. [analysis](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11127-023-01046-z)

- Servel's final preliminary 2023 bulletin reported 55.76% voting against the second proposal. [servel2023](https://www.servel.cl/Ch/app/boletin-final-sobre-resultados-parciales-de-la-eleccion-del-consejo-constitucional-2/noticias/)

### Possible explanations

- **contested:** Weak coordination and limited cross-bloc bargaining may have produced a draft insufficiently aligned with a wider electorate. [analysis](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11127-023-01046-z) **Alternative:** Substantive opposition to provisions, campaign dynamics, distrust, and changing turnout could produce rejection even under a more coordinated process.

- **plausible:** Representativeness inside a convention does not automatically create a durable ratification coalition outside it. [analysis](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11127-023-01046-z) [servel2022](https://www-qa.servel.cl/2022/09/05/servel-realiza-balance-del-plebiscito-constitucional/) [servel2023](https://www.servel.cl/Ch/app/boletin-final-sobre-resultados-parciales-de-la-eleccion-del-consejo-constitucional-2/noticias/) **Alternative:** A sufficiently popular substantive package might win without strong party coordination; this case does not isolate either mechanism.

### Research connections

- **Constitution-making rules, political contingency, and negotiated consent — Interpretive single-case explanation; causal claims remain contested.** Procedures shape bargaining incentives, but their effects depend on the actors and political moment. [analysis](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11127-023-01046-z) **Transfer limit:** A rejected text does not reveal which alternative procedure would have secured approval. More coordination among political leaders could change bargaining without necessarily making the result more legitimate.

### Questions to take into practice

- A convention's ability to produce a text and its ability to assemble a wider ratification coalition are separate achievements.

- Rejection of a proposed constitution can be an exercise of democratic participation rather than its failure.

- The people represented in drafting and the electorate deciding adoption may differ in turnout, priorities, and political alignment.

### What remains uncertain

- The relative contributions of disputed provisions, campaign dynamics, trust, turnout, and drafting arrangements remain contested.

- The two rejections do not establish an ideal degree of transparency or party control in constitution-making.

## Andhra Pradesh: exposing corruption was not the same as deterring it

**Question:** What must connect public scrutiny to consequences for wrongdoing?

**Setting:** Social audits institutionalized from 2006; evaluations published in 2014–2015 · Andhra Pradesh, India, under the boundaries and program arrangements studied

**Technologies:** Social audits (T324)

Andhra Pradesh institutionalized social audits of a rural employment program, allowing beneficiaries and auditors to inspect records and raise irregularities publicly. The audits made wrongdoing more visible, but exposure was not reliably connected to consequences. Officials could adapt to scrutiny, leaving a gap between detecting irregularities and deterring them.

### Outcome: Mixed outcomes

Audits detected irregularities and showed evidence of beneficiary learning, with a modest reduction in leakage per labor-related irregularity. Repeated audits did not deter irregularities overall, and material-related problems increased. Public scrutiny created information, but the evaluated system did not consistently convert that information into prevention or effective accountability.

### Documented observations

- Andhra Pradesh established formally organized, recurring social audits of employment-program works from 2006. [guide](https://accountabilityindia.in/publication/a-guide-to-conducting-social-audits-2/)

- The panel analysis detected no statistically significant employment-generation effect and estimated a modest decline in leakage per labor-related irregularity. [evaluation](https://gala.gre.ac.uk/id/eprint/22033/)

- Material-related irregularities increased, and repeated audits did not produce overall deterrence. [evaluation](https://gala.gre.ac.uk/id/eprint/22033/)

### Possible explanations

- **supported-in-case:** When detection is not followed by credible consequences, officials can learn to shift irregularities toward less visible or less beneficiary-sensitive channels. [evaluation](https://gala.gre.ac.uk/id/eprint/22033/) **Alternative:** Changes in reporting or audit skill may alter measured irregularities without equivalent changes in underlying corruption.

- **plausible:** Beneficiary involvement may improve scrutiny most where participants directly understand what they should receive. [guide](https://accountabilityindia.in/publication/a-guide-to-conducting-social-audits-2/) [evaluation](https://gala.gre.ac.uk/id/eprint/22033/) **Alternative:** Different transaction complexity, documentation, and auditor expertise could explain the contrast between labor and material irregularities.

### Research connections

- **Social accountability as an information–response chain, with strategic adaptation — Panel-based evaluation and institutional process documentation; not randomized audit assignment.** Voice and information are intermediate outputs; deterrence also requires enforceable responsibility for responding. [evaluation](https://gala.gre.ac.uk/id/eprint/22033/) [guide](https://accountabilityindia.in/publication/a-guide-to-conducting-social-audits-2/) **Transfer limit:** The pattern concerns one program and period. Consequences after detection, the complexity of transactions, and beneficiaries' ability to recognize problems all affect what public scrutiny can accomplish.

### Questions to take into practice

- Public findings need an actor responsible for correction and a deadline for responding; otherwise exposure can leave the underlying incentives unchanged.

- Meetings and complaints reveal activity, while recovery, correction, and repeated offenses reveal what follows from it.

- Officials can adapt to scrutiny by moving irregularities toward transactions that beneficiaries find harder to observe.

### What remains uncertain

- Improved detection can increase recorded irregularities even if underlying corruption does not rise, complicating interpretation of the observed changes.

- Longer-term deterrence and the consequences of participation for complainants remain unresolved.

Farzana Afridi and Vegard Iversen / India Policy Forum · primary-research · accessed as abstract. Panel findings, changing irregularities, and follow-up interpretation; repository abstract reviewed.

## Ansari XPRIZE: a winning flight is not an industry

**Question:** What can a prize accomplish when developing the winning invention costs more than the reward?

**Setting:** 1996–2004; commercialization follow-up through 2023 · Mojave, California, United States

**Technologies:** Prize competitions (T230), Patronage (T228)

The Ansari XPRIZE offered $10 million for a privately funded spacecraft that could reach space twice within two weeks. Paul Allen financed Burt Rutan’s team to build SpaceShipOne, which won in 2004. The reward gave the team a public target, but a wealthy backer made the attempt possible. Turning the achievement into a commercial passenger service took much longer.

### Outcome: Mixed outcomes

The team achieved the flight target and its technology was licensed to Virgin Galactic. The demonstration succeeded; commercial service followed in 2023, after a separate development program. The prize alone could neither pay for the winning project nor carry it through that transition.

### Documented observations

- SpaceShipOne won the $10 million competition in 2004 by completing the required repeat flights. [museum](https://airandspace.si.edu/collection-objects/spaceshipone/nasm_A20050459000)

- Allen put the project’s total cost at $28 million in his memoir—nearly three times the prize money. The team therefore needed substantial funding before it could collect the reward. [cost](https://www.thespacereview.com/archive/1829-1.html)

- Virgin Galactic licensed the technology and began commercial service in 2023, nineteen years after the prize-winning flights. [organizer](https://impactmaps.xprize.org/competitions/ansari)

### Possible explanations

- **plausible:** The target and public deadline may have coordinated a well-financed team more than the purse financed its engineering. [cost](https://www.thespacereview.com/archive/1829-1.html) [organizer](https://impactmaps.xprize.org/competitions/ansari) **Alternative:** Allen’s existing ambitions and Rutan’s capabilities might have produced a similar demonstration without a prize.

- **plausible:** The demonstration may have made subsequent licensing easier by reducing uncertainty about feasibility. [museum](https://airandspace.si.edu/collection-objects/spaceshipone/nasm_A20050459000) [cost](https://www.thespacereview.com/archive/1829-1.html) **Alternative:** The purchaser could have valued the team, intellectual property, or publicity even without the qualifying flights.

### Research connections

No dedicated theory connection assessed.

### Questions to take into practice

- A prize can set a clear target while leaving entrants to find the money and expertise needed to reach it. A promising team without a backer may never get to compete.

- A successful demonstration leaves further work: adapting the design, operating repeatedly, finding customers, and paying for development. Those tasks need their own institutions and funding.

### What remains uncertain

- How much extra work did the prize cause, and how much did it redirect work Allen and Rutan already wanted to do? Their ambitions and engineering capabilities predated the winning flights, so the achievement alone cannot settle that question.

No team met the competition’s deadline. SpaceIL later reached lunar orbit but did not land successfully: a team and its capabilities survived the award, while the original objective remained unmet.

### Research connections

No dedicated theory connection assessed.

- Define in advance how deadline failure differs from learning, team formation, and later procurement outcomes.

### What remains uncertain

Netflix offered $1 million for a ten-percent improvement in a movie-rating prediction metric. The challenge was won, but the final ensemble was not deployed as a complete production system. ### Outcome: Mixed outcomes

The benchmark target was met and earlier competition techniques entered production. Netflix’s engineers judged the final ensemble’s additional accuracy insufficient to justify its integration cost. Useful research emerged even though the complete winning system was not adopted.

### Documented observations

### Possible explanations

- **supported-in-case:** A fixed benchmark can reward complexity whose integration costs are absent from the scoring rule. [engineering](https://netflixtechblog.com/netflix-recommendations-beyond-the-5-stars-part-1-55838468f429) **Alternative:** A simpler winning system could still have become obsolete because Netflix’s business and interaction data changed.

- **plausible:** The competition may have been valuable as distributed research even when the final artifact was unsuitable. [engineering](https://netflixtechblog.com/netflix-recommendations-beyond-the-5-stars-part-1-55838468f429) [study](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1461444814538646) **Alternative:** Similar research might have emerged internally or publicly without the tournament; benefits must be compared with participant and organizer costs.

[study](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1461444814538646) **Transfer limit:** Predicting a rating is narrower than helping someone choose what to watch; the cultural interpretation alone cannot tell us whether a different funding method would have worked better.

### Questions to take into practice

- Include deployment constraints and changing product requirements in an evaluation plan.

- Track intermediate knowledge transfers separately from adoption of the final winning artifact.

### What remains uncertain

- The competition’s net value depends on organizer and entrant costs as well as useful techniques. Netflix’s engineers explained their deployment decision, but the comparable cost and output of an internal research program remain uncertain.

## Orteig Prize: the famous winner and the missing entrants

**Question:** What disappears when a prize is remembered only through its winner?

**Setting:** 1919–1927 · New York–Paris transatlantic aviation

**Technologies:** Prize competitions (T230), Patronage (T228)

The Orteig offer rewarded a nonstop flight between New York and Paris. Charles Lindbergh won in 1927 with financial backing from St. Louis businessmen. The familiar story of a clear target and celebrated breakthrough also contains less visible unsuccessful attempts, including the disappearance of Charles Nungesser during his bid.

### Outcome: Mixed outcomes

The specified flight was achieved and publicly celebrated. Other entrants faced catastrophic losses. These outcomes establish neither that the prize alone caused aviation’s expansion nor that the benefits outweighed the costs imposed across all competing teams.

### Documented observations

- With underwriting from St. Louis businessmen, Lindbergh completed the flight in May 1927 and won the $25,000 award. [loc](https://www.loc.gov/item/today-in-history/june-11)

- Charles Nungesser disappeared while attempting to win the Orteig Prize. [museum](https://airandspace.si.edu/stories/editorial/%E2%80%9Cknight-death%E2%80%9D-airplane-insignia)

- After his flight, Lindbergh became an influential spokesman for the emerging aviation industry. [loc](https://www.loc.gov/item/today-in-history/june-11)

### Possible explanations

- **plausible:** The prize’s legible route and public recognition may have helped a pilot mobilize backers. [loc](https://www.loc.gov/item/today-in-history/june-11) **Alternative:** Aircraft improvements, personal ambition, national rivalry, and commercial aviation opportunities also supplied powerful reasons to attempt the flight.

- **supported-in-case:** A winner-focused narrative can obscure the portfolio of risk taken by unsuccessful entrants. No dedicated theory connection assessed.

### Questions to take into practice

- Assess entrant losses and near misses alongside the winner’s achievement.

- Distinguish the prize payment from the up-front patronage that makes an attempt feasible.

### What remains uncertain

- The winner’s achievement does not reveal the total spending, losses or abandoned work across all entrants. Aviation was also advancing for other reasons, making the prize’s contribution to subsequent growth difficult to separate.

## Nupedia and Wikipedia: opening participation, then managing it

**Question:** What changes when contributors can improve a draft immediately rather than await permission?

**Setting:** 2000–2003 origins; contributor-retention evidence published 2013 · International online encyclopedia communities

**Technologies:** Peer review of work (T028), Open-source collaboration (T161), Core and peripheral participation (D008)

Nupedia required articles to pass an expert review workflow; Wikipedia let contributors edit immediately. But the contrast was not simply bureaucracy versus freedom. Nupedia also lacked sustained organizing support, while the growing Wikipedia developed quality controls that made it harder for newcomers to stay. Opening the door and keeping useful participation possible became different problems.

### Outcome: Mixed outcomes

Wikipedia achieved extensive collaborative production while Nupedia faded. Later analysis associated Wikipedia’s restrictive quality controls with declining newcomer retention. Rapid contribution and reliable review had to coexist, and rules introduced to protect the encyclopedia could also discourage its future editors.

### Documented observations

- Sanger describes Nupedia’s seven-step process as slow, but also attributes its decline to neglect and insufficient support for paid organizing. [memoir](https://features.slashdot.org/story/05/04/18/164213/the-early-history-of-nupedia-and-wikipedia-a-memoir)

- His history describes attempts to simplify Nupedia while organizational attention shifted toward Wikipedia. [memoir](https://features.slashdot.org/story/05/04/18/164213/the-early-history-of-nupedia-and-wikipedia-a-memoir)

- Halfaker and colleagues connect Wikipedia’s newcomer-retention decline with restrictive quality controls, automated rejection, and resistance to newcomers’ proposed rule changes. [study](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0002764212469365)

### Possible explanations

- **plausible:** Immediate editing may increase the number of small contributions people are willing to attempt. [memoir](https://features.slashdot.org/story/05/04/18/164213/the-early-history-of-nupedia-and-wikipedia-a-memoir) **Alternative:** Search visibility, recruitment, and the distribution of organizer attention also changed; the projects were not experimentally matched.

- **supported-in-case:** Controls introduced to preserve quality may inadvertently screen out future committed contributors. [study](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0002764212469365) **Alternative:** Changes in who arrived or in the remaining editing opportunities could also influence retention; not every rejected edit represents lost valuable participation.

### Research connections

- **Open-collaboration newcomer retention, Halfaker and colleagues — Observational case analysis.** Filtering contributions can protect the encyclopedia while making it harder to replenish the community that maintains it. [study](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0002764212469365) **Transfer limit:** Its historical Wikipedia mechanisms are not proof that every moderation system reduces useful participation.

### Questions to take into practice

- Measure how often promising newcomers return after their first correction.

- Budget for organizing and review instead of treating an editable website as a self-running institution.

### What remains uncertain

- Sanger’s retrospective account combines a founder’s experience with disputed questions of credit and responsibility. The two projects differed in organizing support and attention as well as workflow, so their trajectories cannot isolate the effect of immediate editing.

- **memoir:** [The Early History of Nupedia and Wikipedia: A Memoir](https://features.slashdot.org/story/05/04/18/164213/the-early-history-of-nupedia-and-wikipedia-a-memoir). Larry Sanger; Slashdot · participant-account · accessed as sections. Read corrections, seven-step workflow, simplification, and organizing-capacity passages.

- **study:** [The Rise and Decline of an Open Collaboration System](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0002764212469365). American Behavioral Scientist; Halfaker and colleagues · primary-research · accessed as abstract. Abstract identifies quality-control and retention findings; no independent causal replication reviewed.

## Python: replacing a founder’s final say

**Question:** Can a project replace personal authority without replacing its whole working community?

**Setting:** July 2018–February 2019 · International Python development community

**Technologies:** BDFL governance (T137), Steering councils (T138), Succession planning (T293)

After conflict over a language proposal, Guido van Rossum withdrew as Python’s final decision-maker and declined to appoint a successor. Core developers then selected a steering-council model and elected its first members. The transition changed the authority structure while retaining existing contributors, technical work, and even the founder’s participation in a different role.

### Outcome: Bounded success

Core developers adopted a successor decision structure and elected its first council. Authority moved from one final decision-maker to an elected body, while the electorate remained the core developer community rather than every Python user.

### Documented observations

- Van Rossum’s resignation said he would leave the decision process, remain available as a core developer, and not choose a successor. [resignation](https://www.mail-archive.com/python-committers%40python.org/msg05628.html)

- The adopted proposal provided a five-member council, core-team voting and safeguards against one employer dominating the council. [model](https://peps.python.org/pep-8016/)

- The first election drew 69 ballots from 96 eligible voters and elected five members, including van Rossum. [election](https://peps.python.org/pep-8100/)

### Possible explanations

- **plausible:** An established contributor community may be able to redistribute final authority while preserving routine production. [resignation](https://www.mail-archive.com/python-committers%40python.org/msg05628.html) [election](https://peps.python.org/pep-8100/) **Alternative:** Continuity may depend more on pre-existing maintainers and mature technical processes than on the council design.

- **plausible:** Keeping the founder involved without exclusive final authority may preserve knowledge while reducing dependence on one person. [resignation](https://www.mail-archive.com/python-committers%40python.org/msg05628.html) [election](https://peps.python.org/pep-8100/) **Alternative:** Informal influence might remain concentrated even after formal powers are redistributed; election results do not measure actual deliberative influence.

### Research connections

No dedicated theory connection assessed.

### Questions to take into practice

- Identify which decisions truly depend on the founder before redesigning every role.

- Specify electorate, replacement procedures, and employer conflicts before a vacancy becomes urgent.

### What remains uncertain

- The transition settled who formally held final authority. How far informal influence changed, and whether contributors found later decisions better or more satisfactory, require attention to the council’s subsequent practice.

### Sources

- **resignation:** [Transfer of power](https://www.mail-archive.com/python-committers%40python.org/msg05628.html). Guido van Rossum; python-committers · participant-account · accessed as full-text. Original July 2018 message mirrored by a mailing-list archive.

- **model:** [PEP 8016: The Steering Council Model](https://peps.python.org/pep-8016/). Python project · institutional-record · accessed as sections. Read council, election, term, and conflict provisions of the historical proposal.

## Heartbleed: widely used does not mean adequately maintained

**Question:** Who supplies maintenance and incident response for infrastructure everyone depends on?

**Setting:** 2014 disclosure and response · Global internet infrastructure

**Technologies:** Open-source collaboration (T161), Grants (T229), Free riding (D017)

Heartbleed exposed a serious vulnerability in the widely used OpenSSL library. The response required more than a public patch: operators needed to identify exposure and act. Industry subsequently organized pooled support for important open-source projects. The case connects maintenance funding, distributed responsibility, and the practical work of getting a repair adopted.

### Outcome: Mixed outcomes

The vulnerability produced widespread exposure, followed by patching and new support arrangements. Direct notification improved patching among contacted operators. Pooled funding committed support for developers and an audit, while maintaining the code and getting repairs deployed remained continuing responsibilities.

### Documented observations

- The vulnerability exposed protected memory, and affected operators varied substantially in how quickly they patched after disclosure. [measurement](https://faculty.cc.gatech.edu/~frankli/papers/li-imc2014.pdf)

- Operators receiving direct notifications patched more often in a staged notification experiment. [measurement](https://faculty.cc.gatech.edu/~frankli/papers/li-imc2014.pdf)

- The Linux Foundation announced support for two full-time OpenSSL developers and an external security audit through the Core Infrastructure Initiative. [funding](https://www.linuxfoundation.org/press/press-release/the-linux-foundations-core-infrastructure-initiative-announces-new-backers-first-projects-to-receive-support-and-advisory-board-members)

### Possible explanations

- **supported-in-case:** Direct notification can overcome some operational inattention after a widely publicized vulnerability. [measurement](https://faculty.cc.gatech.edu/~frankli/papers/li-imc2014.pdf) **Alternative:** The effect could depend on identifying reachable administrators; unreachable or abandoned services may require a different response.

- **plausible:** Users who benefit from shared code may underinvest in collective maintenance until a crisis makes dependencies visible. [funding](https://www.linuxfoundation.org/press/press-release/the-linux-foundations-core-infrastructure-initiative-announces-new-backers-first-projects-to-receive-support-and-advisory-board-members) [measurement](https://faculty.cc.gatech.edu/~frankli/papers/li-imc2014.pdf) **Alternative:** This incident could also reflect difficult code review and deployment coordination that additional funding alone would not solve.

### Research connections

- **Collective-action/free-rider problem in shared infrastructure — Conceptual fit; not causally isolated by these sources.** Many beneficiaries and concentrated maintenance work make financing responsibility a useful question. [funding](https://www.linuxfoundation.org/press/press-release/the-linux-foundations-core-infrastructure-initiative-announces-new-backers-first-projects-to-receive-support-and-advisory-board-members) **Transfer limit:** The case does not compare open and proprietary software security or establish that unpaid work caused this specific bug.

### Questions to take into practice

- Inventory critical dependencies and identify who maintains them before a crisis.

- Treat publication of a fix and verified downstream adoption as different milestones.

### What remains uncertain

- Support commitments for developers and audits did not themselves show whether long-term security improved. Successful notification addressed adoption of a repair, leaving open how to prevent similar defects during development.

### Sources

- **measurement:** [The Matter of Heartbleed](https://faculty.cc.gatech.edu/~frankli/papers/li-imc2014.pdf). ACM Internet Measurement Conference; Durumeric and colleagues · primary-research · accessed as sections. Read background and notification-result passages; measurement and intervention are distinct evidence.

- **funding:** [Core Infrastructure Initiative Announces First Projects to Receive Support](https://www.linuxfoundation.org/press/press-release/the-linux-foundations-core-infrastructure-initiative-announces-new-backers-first-projects-to-receive-support-and-advisory-board-members). Linux Foundation · participant-account · accessed as sections. Read funding commitments, project selection, and governance; not a subsequent effectiveness audit.

## Debian and Devuan: a vote can settle a rule without settling a dispute

**Question:** What happens when a formal decision leaves participants with incompatible visions?

**Setting:** 2014 init-system dispute and fork announcement · International Debian community

**Technologies:** Forking (T184), Maintainer delegation (T183), Open-source ecosystems (S029)

Debian debated whether packages should be required to support alternative initialization systems. Its formal resolution process produced a decision, but dissenting developers and administrators still regarded the project’s direction as unacceptable. They announced Devuan, a derivative intended to avoid systemd dependencies. Open licensing made exit with reusable code possible alongside continued disagreement.

### Outcome: Mixed outcomes

Debian reached a binding decision, and dissenters began a separate project. A procedure allowed the existing project to proceed without reconciling its participants’ competing priorities; open licensing gave those leaving a starting point for their own work.

### Documented observations

- “General Resolution is not required” defeated every other option in the 2014 preference vote. [vote](https://www.debian.org/vote/2014/vote_003)

- The rejected proposals concerned package dependencies and support obligations, a different question from the earlier choice of a default initialization system. [vote](https://www.debian.org/vote/2014/vote_003)

- Devuan’s founders described their fork as a response to disagreements about governance and mission as well as technical design. [fork](https://www.devuan.org/os/announce/)

### Possible explanations

- **supported-in-case:** A formal vote may preserve a project’s ability to proceed without making minority participants accept its priorities. [vote](https://www.debian.org/vote/2014/vote_003) [fork](https://www.devuan.org/os/announce/) **Alternative:** The departure might have happened even without this vote because technical commitments had already diverged.

- **plausible:** Forkability may convert an otherwise blocked internal dispute into parallel experimentation. [fork](https://www.devuan.org/os/announce/) **Alternative:** Parallel projects may instead duplicate scarce maintenance work and retain dependencies on the upstream community they leave.

### Research connections

No dedicated theory connection assessed.

### Questions to take into practice

- Ask whether a decision procedure promises a binding decision or genuine convergence; those are different outcomes.

- Before proposing a fork, identify the maintainers, infrastructure, and continuing upstream dependencies it would need.

### What remains uncertain

- Devuan’s founders presented their own account of the dispute, which does not establish how representative their grievances were across Debian. The fork’s longer-term value also depends on adoption, quality and the extra maintenance required.

## Linux and Android: the cost of freedom to diverge

**Question:** How can a shared codebase support variation without making repairs prohibitively expensive?

**Setting:** Pre-GKI Android kernels through Android 12-era restructuring · International Linux and Android ecosystems

**Technologies:** Open-source collaboration (T161), Maintainer delegation (T183), Forking (T184)

Linux integrates contributions through subsystem maintainers, while downstream organizations can adapt the code for particular products. Android’s device-specific kernels illustrate the maintenance cost of that variation. Google’s Generic Kernel Image project sought to reunify the core while moving vendor-specific support into modules, changing the coordination boundary instead of eliminating all specialization.

### Outcome: Mixed outcomes

Android introduced a shared kernel and module boundary in response to the difficulty of updating divergent device kernels. The architecture changed where customization could occur. Its practical security benefit still depends on manufacturers delivering repairs through to users’ devices.

### Documented observations

- Contributions pass through a chain of subsystem maintainers before integration into Linux’s mainline repository. [kernel](https://kernel.org/doc/html/latest/process/2.Process.html)

- Android’s account identifies custom device kernels as a source of difficult security backports, delayed upgrades, and costly upstream contribution. [android](https://source.android.com/docs/core/architecture/kernel/generic-kernel-image)

- GKI moves hardware-specific support into modules around a common kernel and stable module interface, with requirements applying to relevant Android 12 launches. [android](https://source.android.com/docs/core/architecture/kernel/generic-kernel-image)

### Possible explanations

- **plausible:** Shared integration boundaries may reduce duplicated adaptation work without removing product-specific variation. [android](https://source.android.com/docs/core/architecture/kernel/generic-kernel-image) [kernel](https://kernel.org/doc/html/latest/process/2.Process.html) **Alternative:** Benefits may depend on central platform enforcement and vendor resources rather than modularity alone.

- **plausible:** Permission to fork is valuable only when participants can sustain the integration work it creates. [android](https://source.android.com/docs/core/architecture/kernel/generic-kernel-image) **Alternative:** Some divergence is necessary for unsupported hardware or independent priorities; convergence could sacrifice useful autonomy.

### Research connections

No dedicated theory connection assessed.

### Questions to take into practice

- Estimate the lifetime cost of carrying a private branch, not just the cost of making the initial modification.

- Design explicit paths for downstream improvements and repairs to return to the shared core.

### What remains uncertain

- Google’s diagnosis explains the intended benefit of GKI. Measuring its realized effect requires following update delivery across manufacturers, including differences in their resources and implementation. The Linux maintainer hierarchy and Android’s downstream requirements exercise authority at different points in that process.

- **kernel:** [How the development process works](https://kernel.org/doc/html/latest/process/2.Process.html). Linux Kernel documentation · participant-account · accessed as sections. Read review and chain-of-trust sections; describes upstream governance rather than Android outcomes.

- **android:** [Generic Kernel Image project](https://source.android.com/docs/core/architecture/kernel/generic-kernel-image). Android Open Source Project · participant-account · accessed as sections. Read history, costs of fragmentation, and GKI design; claims of improvement are the platform’s account.

## Fagor: a cooperative can fail while its network still protects people

**Question:** Does institutional resilience mean saving every firm, or helping people survive a firm’s failure?

**Setting:** 2013 bankruptcy and subsequent relocation · Basque Country, Spain; subsidiaries in France and Poland

**Technologies:** Worker cooperatives (T251), Federations (T143), Risk-sharing pools (T336)

Fagor Electrodomésticos went bankrupt inside the Mondragon cooperative network. Preserving the enterprise and protecting its members proved to be different tasks. Relocation, retraining and support offered displaced workers other paths, but some placements were insecure and receiving cooperatives resisted taking workers permanently. Solidarity depended on agreements across organizations with their own pressures.

### Outcome: Mixed outcomes

Fagor failed, while the network helped absorb some consequences through relocation and support. Those protections depended on receiving cooperatives and workers’ circumstances; they did not erase the losses or reach everyone equally.

### Documented observations

- Fagor went bankrupt in November 2013 after international expansion and restructuring across its parent plants and European subsidiaries. [international](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/apce.12137)

- Relocation and retraining offered displaced workers routes into other cooperatives, but some workers objected to temporary placements and receiving cooperatives resisted making them permanent. [response](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/apce.12329)

- Mondragon and Lagun Aro could not simply impose relocations on other cooperatives. [response](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/apce.12329)

### Possible explanations

- **plausible:** Federated institutions may protect members against enterprise-specific failure more effectively than an isolated cooperative could. [response](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/apce.12329) **Alternative:** A conventional group with strong employment insurance and regional support might provide similar protection; ownership is not the only mechanism.

- **supported-in-case:** Solidarity becomes harder when helping displaced insiders competes with local hiring and another cooperative’s flexibility. [response](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/apce.12329) **Alternative:** Skills mismatch, market weakness, and existing workforce commitments can explain reluctance without assuming selfishness or ideological collapse.

### Research connections

- **Flexicurity in cooperative intercooperation — Qualitative case evidence, including adverse worker experiences.** Employment security depended partly on workers moving between cooperatives, linking protection from unemployment to flexibility about where and how they worked. [response](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/apce.12329) **Transfer limit:** Interview evidence does not establish how a comparable conventional network would have performed.

### Questions to take into practice

- Define whether a mutual-support promise protects jobs, income, membership, or a particular workplace.

- Ask who is excluded from protection when enterprises employ nonmembers or operate foreign subsidiaries.

### What remains uncertain

- Workers’ accounts reveal conflicting experiences of relocation without establishing how widespread satisfaction or dissatisfaction was. Protection for cooperative members also needs to be distinguished from the experience of nonmembers and employees in foreign subsidiaries.

### Sources

- **international:** [What Do Mondragon Coopitalist Multinationals Look Like? The Rise and Fall of Fagor](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/apce.12137). Annals of Public and Cooperative Economics; Errasti, Bretos and Etxezarreta · primary-research · accessed as abstract. Supports bankruptcy and multinational scope; detailed subsidiary outcomes not inferred from abstract alone.

- **response:** [Intercooperation, flexicurity and their impact on workers: The case of Fagor Electrodomésticos](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/apce.12329). Annals of Public and Cooperative Economics; Santos-Larrazabal and Basterretxea · primary-research · accessed as sections. ## Grameen II: rewriting the rules did not instantly change the meetings

**Question:** What happens when frontline habits preserve a practice that a formal reform tries to remove?

**Technologies:** Contracts (T267), Path dependence (D029)

Grameen II introduced more flexible loan arrangements while retaining borrower groups and regular meetings. Its rules rejected making members repay another borrower’s debt. Yet early fieldwork found meeting practices that pressured members to cover shortfalls. Changing the written terms did not immediately change how staff secured repayment or how borrowers experienced responsibility.

### Outcome: Mixed outcomes

The redesigned system offered repayment flexibility, but implementation was uneven. Formal individual responsibility coexisted with collective pressure in observed branches, making staff behavior crucial to what the new rules meant for borrowers.

### Documented observations

- Under the Grameen II design, borrowers encountering difficulty could renegotiate a flexible loan with a revised repayment schedule. [design](https://grameenfoundation.org/documents/GrameenGuidelines.pdf)

- Despite the explicit no-joint-liability rule, fieldworkers observed pressure on members to cover other borrowers’ repayments. [field](https://www.microsave.net/wp-content/uploads/2018/11/GRAMEEN_II_At_the_end_of_2003_A_grounded_view_of_how_Grameen_s_new_initiative_is_progressing_in_the_villages.pdf)

- In observed meetings, staff delayed closing or providing other services until dues were covered; some staff also resisted offering flexible loans. [field](https://www.microsave.net/wp-content/uploads/2018/11/GRAMEEN_II_At_the_end_of_2003_A_grounded_view_of_how_Grameen_s_new_initiative_is_progressing_in_the_villages.pdf)

### Possible explanations

- **supported-in-case:** Established routines may survive formal reform when staff believe flexibility threatens repayment discipline. [field](https://www.microsave.net/wp-content/uploads/2018/11/GRAMEEN_II_At_the_end_of_2003_A_grounded_view_of_how_Grameen_s_new_initiative_is_progressing_in_the_villages.pdf) **Alternative:** Some staff behavior may instead reflect local repayment conditions or incentives not adequately captured in the report.

- **participant-explanation:** Individual renegotiation could reduce the burden placed on other group members without eliminating mutual information and support. [design](https://grameenfoundation.org/documents/GrameenGuidelines.pdf) **Alternative:** Flexibility may be inaccessible in practice, or merely extend indebtedness; stated intentions do not establish borrower welfare improvements.

### Research connections

No dedicated theory connection assessed.

### Questions to take into practice

- Observe meetings and exception handling rather than inferring practice from a rulebook.

- Ask whether frontline incentives and training support the discretion a reform officially permits.

### What remains uncertain

- The observed meetings cover a limited early implementation sample. They leave open how widely these practices persisted as the reform developed, and whether accessible renegotiation improved borrowers’ welfare.

**Question:** Does expanded access to small business credit produce the broader changes its advocates expect?

**Setting:** 2005 assignment; 2006–2010 rollout and follow-up; published 2015 · Hyderabad, India

**Technologies:** Contracts (T267)

Spandana expanded group-lending services into randomly selected Hyderabad neighborhoods, allowing researchers to compare earlier access with later access. Borrowing and some business investment increased. But greater access did not produce significant average improvements in several wider measures of household welfare: the step from more credit to a transformed livelihood was far from automatic.

### Outcome: Mixed outcomes

Earlier access changed borrowing and some economic choices without significant average improvements in consumption, health, education, or the measured women’s-empowerment outcomes. A six-study synthesis found similarly modest rather than transformative effects across varied settings, while leaving room for heterogeneous benefits and costs.

### Documented observations

- The study randomized 52 of 104 neighborhoods to earlier Spandana access; actual borrowing increased rather than becoming universal. [trial](https://economics.mit.edu/sites/default/files/publications/spandana_Mar14_final.pdf)

- Researchers report increased investment and profits among existing businesses, but no significant average gain in consumption or several broader social outcomes. [trial](https://economics.mit.edu/sites/default/files/publications/spandana_Mar14_final.pdf)

- Across six randomized evaluations in varied settings, average effects were modest rather than the broad transformations often attributed to microcredit. [synthesis](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/sites/default/files/research-paper/AEJ%20Banerjee%20Intro.pdf)

### Possible explanations

- **plausible:** Credit can relax a constraint for some existing entrepreneurs without making most households successful new business owners. [trial](https://economics.mit.edu/sites/default/files/publications/spandana_Mar14_final.pdf) [synthesis](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/sites/default/files/research-paper/AEJ%20Banerjee%20Intro.pdf) **Alternative:** Demand, skills, risk, and repayment obligations may matter more than access to capital for many households.

- **supported-in-case:** Large universal poverty-reduction expectations were not supported by the outcomes and horizons evaluated here. [trial](https://economics.mit.edu/sites/default/files/publications/spandana_Mar14_final.pdf) [synthesis](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/sites/default/files/research-paper/AEJ%20Banerjee%20Intro.pdf) **Alternative:** Longer-run effects or concentrated gains for particular borrowers may be obscured by average estimates and later control-group access.

### Research connections

- **Credit constraints and heterogeneous returns to microenterprise investment — Randomized access evidence; mechanism only partly identified.** Separates expanded availability from guaranteed take-up or uniform entrepreneurial returns. [trial](https://economics.mit.edu/sites/default/files/publications/spandana_Mar14_final.pdf) [synthesis](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/sites/default/files/research-paper/AEJ%20Banerjee%20Intro.pdf) **Transfer limit:** Assignment estimates concern access under these conditions, not the effect of every loan or the quality of Grameen’s organizational model.

### Questions to take into practice

- Name the welfare outcome and time horizon before treating repayment or borrower counts as success.

- Compare effects for different participants without turning subgroup anecdotes into universal promises.

### What remains uncertain

- The synthesis includes this trial and is not a second independent replication of Hyderabad. Later control access changes the interpretation of follow-up differences. Migration exclusions and imperfect take-up narrow generalization.

### Sources

- **trial:** [The Miracle of Microfinance? Evidence from a Randomized Evaluation](https://economics.mit.edu/sites/default/files/publications/spandana_Mar14_final.pdf). Banerjee, Duflo, Glennerster and Kinnan; author manuscript · primary-research · accessed as sections. Read design, sample, results summary, and identification caveats; March 2014 manuscript underlying 2015 publication.

- **synthesis:** [Six Randomized Evaluations of Microcredit: Introduction and Further Steps](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/sites/default/files/research-paper/AEJ%20Banerjee%20Intro.pdf). American Economic Journal: Applied Economics; Banerjee, Karlan and Zinman · review · accessed as sections. Read synthesis and motivation; contains Hyderabad alongside other trials, not an independent study of the same rollout.

## Haifa daycare fines: paying to be late?

**Question:** Can a penalty change what people think they are allowed to do?

**Setting:** January–June 1998 field study; published 2000; later laboratory mechanism study · Haifa, Israel

**Technologies:** Social norms (S026), Norm drift (D030)

Researchers introduced a small late-pickup fine at six of ten daycare centers. Lateness increased instead of falling and remained elevated during the short period after removal. A charge intended to discourage delay may have made extra care feel like something parents could buy. The behavior changed clearly; the parents’ interpretation is harder to establish.

### Outcome: Failure on the stated objective

The fine failed to reduce lateness in these centers. A later laboratory experiment explored how penalties and payment recipients could change behavior, but used a different task and could not establish what the daycare parents had believed.

### Documented observations

- The field study observed ten centers for twenty weeks and randomly selected six for a fine introduced after the initial observation period. [field](https://www.ius.uzh.ch/dam/jcr%3Aed3f9a0b-ab68-4cf6-a18c-a480b33c9456/Gneezy%2520et%2520al%2520A%2520Fine%2520is%2520a%2520Price.pdf)

- The fine was NIS 10 for a qualifying late pickup; recorded lateness increased and did not return to baseline during four weeks after withdrawal. [field](https://www.ius.uzh.ch/dam/jcr%3Aed3f9a0b-ab68-4cf6-a18c-a480b33c9456/Gneezy%2520et%2520al%2520A%2520Fine%2520is%2520a%2520Price.pdf)

- A later real-effort experiment varied penalties and who received payment, finding different responses by social-value orientation rather than a uniform average crowding-out effect. [lab](https://www.econstor.eu/bitstream/10419/335576/1/fine-is-a-price-SSRN.pdf)

### Possible explanations

- **plausible:** Parents may have interpreted the fine as a defined price for extra care instead of a signal that lateness was unacceptable. [field](https://www.ius.uzh.ch/dam/jcr%3Aed3f9a0b-ab68-4cf6-a18c-a480b33c9456/Gneezy%2520et%2520al%2520A%2520Fine%2520is%2520a%2520Price.pdf) [lab](https://www.econstor.eu/bitstream/10419/335576/1/fine-is-a-price-SSRN.pdf) **Alternative:** A small specified penalty could instead reduce uncertainty about previously feared consequences; these explanations overlap but are not identical.

- **supported-in-case:** The meaning and recipient of payment can alter its effect alongside its monetary size. [lab](https://www.econstor.eu/bitstream/10419/335576/1/fine-is-a-price-SSRN.pdf) **Alternative:** The laboratory uses contract performance, not parents and caregivers; selection and subgroup classification constrain transfer to everyday sanctions.

### Research connections

- **Fine-is-a-price framing and motivational crowding — Field outcome plus a distinct laboratory mechanism test.** Distinguishes the observed increase in lateness from a proposed change in the perceived social contract. [field](https://www.ius.uzh.ch/dam/jcr%3Aed3f9a0b-ab68-4cf6-a18c-a480b33c9456/Gneezy%2520et%2520al%2520A%2520Fine%2520is%2520a%2520Price.pdf) [lab](https://www.econstor.eu/bitstream/10419/335576/1/fine-is-a-price-SSRN.pdf) **Transfer limit:** Neither study establishes that substantial fines, all cultural settings, or all participants respond the same way.

### Questions to take into practice

- Ask what a new charge communicates about permission, obligation, and who bears the harm.

- Measure behavior after removing an incentive rather than assuming the previous norm returns immediately.

### What remains uncertain

- The field sample was small and two initially observed centers were excluded for incomplete records. Post-removal observation was brief. The later laboratory study tests a related mechanism, not a direct daycare replication.

- **field:** [A Fine Is a Price](https://www.ius.uzh.ch/dam/jcr%3Aed3f9a0b-ab68-4cf6-a18c-a480b33c9456/Gneezy%2520et%2520al%2520A%2520Fine%2520is%2520a%2520Price.pdf). Journal of Legal Studies; Gneezy and Rustichini · primary-research · accessed as sections. University-hosted paper; read design, results, fine size, exclusions, and authors’ explicit limits.

- **lab:** [Testing a Fine Is a Price in the Lab](https://www.econstor.eu/bitstream/10419/335576/1/fine-is-a-price-SSRN.pdf). NYU Law and Economics working paper; Kornhauser, Lu and Tontrup · primary-research · accessed as sections. Read working-paper design and mechanism summary; related laboratory test, not a replication of the original setting.

## Blood donation incentives: one crowding-out story is not a universal law

**Question:** When do payments change participation, and when do they merely move it elsewhere?

**Setting:** Swedish experiment published 2008; US study published 2012 · Sweden and United States

**Technologies:** Gift economies (T218), Social norms (S026)

A Swedish experiment offered prospective blood donors no payment, a small payment, or payment with a charity option. The negative effect appeared among women, while differences across all participants were not statistically significant. US blood-drive research later found increased donations with incentives, partly offset by movement between drives. Payments changed participation differently across these settings and measures.

### Outcome: Mixed outcomes

The Swedish pooled differences were not statistically significant; the authors reported crowding out among women and reversal with the charity option. US evidence found increased donations at incentivized drives, partly offset by movement from neighboring drives. Context and the measured outcome change the conclusion.

### Documented observations

- Mellström and Johannesson report pooled participation of 43%, 33%, and 44% across treatments, with differences not statistically significant; the women’s subgroup showed a significant payment decline. [sweden](https://econweb.ucsd.edu/~jandreon/PhilanthropyAndFundraising/Volume%202/32%20Mellstrom%20Johannesson%202011.pdf)

- Their intervention concerned agreeing to become a donor, not a demonstrated long-term change in safe blood supply. [sweden](https://econweb.ucsd.edu/~jandreon/PhilanthropyAndFundraising/Volume%202/32%20Mellstrom%20Johannesson%202011.pdf)

- In US blood-drive data and a field experiment, incentives increased donations at participating drives, but some of that gain came from donors switching away from neighboring drives. [us](https://pubs.aeaweb.org/doi/10.1257/pol.4.1.186)

### Possible explanations

- **plausible:** A payment may alter the social meaning of an altruistic action differently for different participants. [sweden](https://econweb.ucsd.edu/~jandreon/PhilanthropyAndFundraising/Volume%202/32%20Mellstrom%20Johannesson%202011.pdf) **Alternative:** Small subgroups, pre-existing preferences, or context-specific interpretations could explain heterogeneity without implying a stable gender rule.

- **supported-in-case:** A local participation increase can overstate a system-wide increase when people switch locations. [us](https://pubs.aeaweb.org/doi/10.1257/pol.4.1.186) **Alternative:** Some additional attendance may represent genuinely new or better-timed donations; displacement need not eliminate all benefit.

### Research connections

- **Motivational crowding and displacement — Different field designs produce conditional, not universal, findings.** Separates reduced willingness in one setting from substitution and increased observed donations elsewhere. [sweden](https://econweb.ucsd.edu/~jandreon/PhilanthropyAndFundraising/Volume%202/32%20Mellstrom%20Johannesson%202011.pdf) [us](https://pubs.aeaweb.org/doi/10.1257/pol.4.1.186) **Transfer limit:** Cash versus other incentives, prospective versus established donors, and outcome definitions prevent treating these as identical interventions.

### Questions to take into practice

- Distinguish willingness, attendance, completed donation, and total supply before comparing studies.

- Track neighboring venues and later participation when evaluating a local incentive.

### What remains uncertain

- Agreement to become a donor, completed donations and sustained safe blood supply are different outcomes. Neither a small subgroup’s response nor an increase at selected drives settles the long-term effect of a payment policy on the whole supply system.

### Sources

- **sweden:** [Crowding Out in Blood Donation: Was Titmuss Right?](https://econweb.ucsd.edu/~jandreon/PhilanthropyAndFundraising/Volume%202/32%20Mellstrom%20Johannesson%202011.pdf). Mellström and Johannesson; university-hosted manuscript · primary-research · accessed as sections. Read intervention and results, including nonsignificant pooled effects and subgroup findings.

- **us:** [Will There Be Blood? Incentives and Displacement Effects in Pro-social Behavior](https://pubs.aeaweb.org/doi/10.1257/pol.4.1.186). American Economic Journal: Economic Policy; Lacetera, Macis and Slonim · primary-research · accessed as abstract. Indexed publisher abstract reports positive effects and neighboring-drive displacement; direct full-text access blocked.

## Cocoon Projects: contribution-weighted governance with LiquidO

**Question:** Can open participation coexist with authority weighted by assessed contribution?

**Setting:** Model documented in 2013; external mapping in 2016–2017 · Italy; distributed professional organization

**Technologies:** Shared task boards (T176), Pull systems (T177), Work-in-progress limits (T178), Majority voting (T119), Contribution records (T278), Reputation systems (T277), Meritocracy (S006)

Stelio Verzera describes LiquidO as Cocoon Projects’ operating model: people pull collectively prioritized work from a shared board, assess contributions after delivery, and receive credits convertible into compensation. Some consequential votes are weighted by earned credits. It is therefore a specific combination of open participation and differentiated influence, rather than equal-vote democracy or an absence of structure.

### Outcome: Outcome evidence uncertain

LiquidO combined open participation with contribution-weighted compensation and voting inside a privately owned company. Verzera reported close agreement between self-assessment and peer assessments, but agreement alone cannot show whether less visible work received fair credit or whether newcomers could gain influence.

### Documented observations

- The 2013 LiquidO design combined time-boxed voting, work-in-progress limits, voluntary task uptake, and peer evaluation of completed contributions. [design](https://www.managementexchange.com/hack/liquid-organizations-realizing-next-evolutionary-stage-anti-fragility?csort=likes)

- Credits determine compensation and, for decisions deemed important, differentiated voting weight. The founder reports assessment discrepancies below 10%, without publishing the underlying dataset. [design](https://www.managementexchange.com/hack/liquid-organizations-realizing-next-evolutionary-stage-anti-fragility?csort=likes)

- Cocoon remained privately owned while opening parts of its governance to participants. Participation in decisions did not itself give contributors ownership rights. [mapping](https://legacy.iftf.org/fileadmin/user_upload/downloads/ppj/PositivePlatformsIdentity_MapAnalysis_ItalianEcosystem_for_IFTF.pdf)

### Possible explanations

- **participant-explanation:** Visible contribution records could make informal influence discussable and reward neglected organizational work. [design](https://www.managementexchange.com/hack/liquid-organizations-realizing-next-evolutionary-stage-anti-fragility?csort=likes) **Alternative:** A closely selected professional group and repeated relationships may explain cooperative behavior independently of the credit system.

- **plausible:** Accumulated credits could also reinforce incumbents’ influence rather than keep authority responsive to current expertise. [design](https://www.managementexchange.com/hack/liquid-organizations-realizing-next-evolutionary-stage-anti-fragility?csort=likes) **Alternative:** Credit expiry or decision rules tied to relevant expertise could limit this effect. Whether those safeguards operated in LiquidO remains unclear.

### Research connections

- **Stigmergic coordination — Explicit participant framing, not a tested causal explanation.** The shared board provides visible traces that guide subsequent work without assigning every task centrally. [design](https://www.managementexchange.com/hack/liquid-organizations-realizing-next-evolutionary-stage-anti-fragility?csort=likes) **Transfer limit:** A shared task board can decentralize task choice while leaving evaluation, conflict resolution, and ownership concentrated.

### Questions to take into practice

- Which decisions are open to everyone, and which give more weight to past contributors?

- How are disputed assessments, essential invisible work, and newcomers’ influence handled?

### What remains uncertain

- How did voting weight change as credits accumulated, and how much influence could a newcomer acquire?

- The durability of the arrangement, the treatment of departures, and the experience of people dissatisfied with their assessments remain unclear.

### Sources

- **design:** [Liquid organizations: building the next evolutionary stage of anti-fragility](https://www.managementexchange.com/hack/liquid-organizations-realizing-next-evolutionary-stage-anti-fragility?csort=likes). Management Innovation eXchange / Stelio Verzera · participant-account · accessed as sections. Original 2013 founder account; detailed mechanisms and self-reported results, not independent validation.

- **mapping:** [Positive Platforms Identity: Italian ecosystem mapping](https://legacy.iftf.org/fileadmin/user_upload/downloads/ppj/PositivePlatformsIdentity_MapAnalysis_ItalianEcosystem_for_IFTF.pdf). Institute for the Future Positive Platforms Fellowship · primary-research · accessed as sections. Read scope and Cocoon sections, especially page 15; external descriptive mapping, with a direct citation to Cocoon for LiquidO.

## Enspiral: support networks can develop their own forms of pressure

**Question:** What replaces managerial control when work, friendship, and care overlap?

**Setting:** Network founded around 2010; an 18-month field study published in 2021 · Wellington, New Zealand, with international participants

**Technologies:** Buddy systems (T058), Community wikis (T032), Working groups (T164), Gift economies (T218), Volunteer burnout (D019), Core and peripheral participation (D008)

Enspiral connects people and ventures pursuing socially meaningful work through shared relationships, support practices, and organizational infrastructure. It is a network containing distinct ventures, not one uniformly managed company. Formal agreements coexist with editable guides, while work, friendship, personal growth, and belonging overlap in everyday participation.

### Outcome: Mixed outcomes

During an 18-month field study, participants experienced practical generosity and early opportunities to contribute, alongside exhaustion, pressure to disclose personal feelings, and strained boundaries. Support could open doors while expectations of openness made participation demanding. These experiences concern particular people and occasions, not every member or the network today.

### Documented observations

- Enspiral distinguishes agreements requiring formal change from guides contributors can edit, and combines nodes and working groups with a foundation holding formal roles. [handbook](https://handbook.enspiral.com/)

- The network included roughly 200–350 participants during the field-study period, spanning relationships beyond any one venture. [study](https://research.vu.nl/ws/files/222790823/Affective_Control_in_New_Collaborative_Work.pdf)

- Check-ins gave participants opportunities to share personal circumstances. Some also described excessive disclosure, overextended care, and discomfort about what participation seemed to require. [study](https://research.vu.nl/ws/files/222790823/Affective_Control_in_New_Collaborative_Work.pdf)

### Possible explanations

- **supported-in-case:** Relational openness can become an informal admission test for being trusted with important work. [study](https://research.vu.nl/ws/files/222790823/Affective_Control_in_New_Collaborative_Work.pdf) **Alternative:** Some discomfort may reflect particular meetings or facilitator choices rather than an unavoidable feature of network organizing.

- **plausible:** Clearer boundaries and named support responsibilities might preserve care without leaving a few people feeling responsible for everyone. [study](https://research.vu.nl/ws/files/222790823/Affective_Control_in_New_Collaborative_Work.pdf) [handbook](https://handbook.enspiral.com/) **Alternative:** Resource scarcity and uneven availability may still concentrate the work even when responsibilities are documented.

### Research connections

- **Affective control in collaborative work — Interpretive Lacanian analysis grounded in a multi-source field study.** Desires for purpose, growth, and belonging can generate commitment and pressure without a conventional boss. The term “affective control” draws attention to how those desires influence participation. [study](https://research.vu.nl/ws/files/222790823/Affective_Control_in_New_Collaborative_Work.pdf) **Transfer limit:** The same check-in may feel supportive to one person and intrusive to another; meeting design and relationships affect how pressure develops.

### Questions to take into practice

- Can someone decline emotional disclosure and remain a trusted participant?

- Who performs care and coordination, and what happens when those people stop?

- Separate the network’s support function from the governance and employment rules of individual ventures.

### What remains uncertain

- How do people who leave Enspiral experience these expectations, and which participants carry the greatest care burden?

- The fieldwork and later handbook describe different periods; changes in formal rules may also have changed everyday experiences.

### Sources

- **handbook:** [Enspiral Handbook: Introduction and Structure](https://handbook.enspiral.com/). Enspiral · institutional-record · accessed as sections. Documents the distinction between agreements, guides, ventures, and foundation; no outcome evaluation.

- **study:** [Affective Control in New Collaborative Work: Communal Fantasies of Purpose, Growth and Belonging](https://research.vu.nl/ws/files/222790823/Affective_Control_in_New_Collaborative_Work.pdf). Organization Studies / Resch, Hoyer and Steyaert · primary-research · accessed as sections. Read methods and findings on exhaustion and boundaries; fieldwork plus management texts, with an explicit interpretive framework.

## Enspiral’s Cobudget: open proposals still need timing and strategy

**Question:** Why can a transparent participatory budget leave money idle or miss shared priorities?

**Setting:** 2013–2019 account of successive funding models · Enspiral network, New Zealand and distributed

**Technologies:** Participatory budgeting (T232), Revenue sharing (T258), Membership dues (T227), Time-boxed commitments (T198)

Enspiral developed collaborative funding after a small support crew became overwhelmed by coordination. Members could propose funding buckets, while contributors allocated discretionary money after core costs. Later experience revealed that continuous proposal flows, scattered attention, and idle balances did not automatically produce a coherent shared budget, prompting facilitated rounds and strategic conversations.

### Outcome: Mixed outcomes

Early organizers reported broader participation and lower coordination costs. Later, steward Francesca Pick encountered low-priority spending, timing advantages, and unused allocations. She judged the projects more strategic after three facilitated rounds, although that judgment leaves open whether the funded work produced better long-term results.

### Documented observations

- The 2014 design reserved core expenses before discretionary allocation; anyone could propose a bucket, while allocation rights initially followed financial contributions. [origin](https://www.managementexchange.com/story/collaborative-funding-dissolve-authority-empower-everyone-and-crowdsource-smarter-transparent)

- During continuous funding, money remained unused in some accounts, proposals were difficult to compare, and attention was scattered across the network. [revision](https://medium.com/enspiral-tales/sharing-power-by-sharing-money-the-evolution-of-collaborative-funding-in-enspiral-a56643e9cd3)

- Time-boxed rounds added strategic discussion and a facilitator. Pick reported three rounds allocating about US$40,000; her favorable judgment concerned the strategic quality of the selected projects. [revision](https://medium.com/enspiral-tales/sharing-power-by-sharing-money-the-evolution-of-collaborative-funding-in-enspiral-a56643e9cd3)

### Possible explanations

- **participant-explanation:** Synchronized rounds may improve comparison by placing competing proposals in view at the same time. [revision](https://medium.com/enspiral-tales/sharing-power-by-sharing-money-the-evolution-of-collaborative-funding-in-enspiral-a56643e9cd3) **Alternative:** Different proposers, funding levels, or greater organizer experience could also account for the changed portfolio.

- **plausible:** Giving every contributor control over an allocation can spread power while still leaving coordination work concentrated. [origin](https://www.managementexchange.com/story/collaborative-funding-dissolve-authority-empower-everyone-and-crowdsource-smarter-transparent) [revision](https://medium.com/enspiral-tales/sharing-power-by-sharing-money-the-evolution-of-collaborative-funding-in-enspiral-a56643e9cd3) **Alternative:** A network with stable priorities and experienced participants may require less facilitation than this transition did.

### Research connections

No dedicated theory connection assessed.

### Questions to take into practice

- Distinguish permission to propose from rights to allocate money.

- Can members compare alternatives before committing their allocations?

- Provide a separate route for urgent proposals that cannot wait for a round.

### What remains uncertain

- Did the projects funded through facilitated rounds produce more lasting value than those selected through continuous allocation?

- How did unequal allocation rights and members who rarely participated affect the resulting budget?

### Sources

- **origin:** [Collaborative Funding: dissolve authority, empower everyone, and crowdsource a smarter, transparent budget](https://www.managementexchange.com/story/collaborative-funding-dissolve-authority-empower-everyone-and-crowdsource-smarter-transparent). Management Innovation eXchange / Alanna Krause · participant-account · accessed as sections. 2014 organizer account; mechanism, preceding support-crew overload, and self-reported metrics.

- **revision:** [Sharing power by sharing money: the evolution of collaborative funding in Enspiral](https://medium.com/enspiral-tales/sharing-power-by-sharing-money-the-evolution-of-collaborative-funding-in-enspiral-a56643e9cd3). ## Microsolidarity practice programs: from strangers to small support groups

**Question:** Can a short program help strangers form support groups that continue meeting?

**Setting:** 2018 proposal, 2020 update, 2020–2021 practice programs · Online, internationally distributed participants

**Technologies:** Learning cohorts (T020), Retrospectives (T027), Peer support groups (T099), Time-boxed commitments (T198)

Richard D. Bartlett’s microsolidarity proposal names a small recurring support group a crew and a wider environment for forming crews a congregation. Drawing partly on Enspiral experience, Bartlett and Nati Lombardo ran online practice programs that moved participants through paired encounters, small-group support, and reflection. The aim was to give people experience of offering and receiving support, with a path toward continuing relationships.

### Outcome: Outcome evidence uncertain

The organizers ran programs in 2020–2021 and described participants experiencing connection and insight. Whether those encounters became durable support relationships remains unclear: a satisfying session and a crew that continues exchanging practical help are different achievements.

### Documented observations

- Crews and congregations name patterns Bartlett recognized in Enspiral and other communities, rather than a single organization with uniform membership rules. [proposal](https://www.microsolidarity.cc/articles/2-update-2020)

- Programs used four weekly 90-minute sessions for about 20–30 participants, with at least two hosts and theoretical material outside the calls. [program](https://www.microsolidarity.cc/practices/how-to-run-a-practice-program)

- Sessions combined brief pair encounters, Troika Consulting, Case Clinic, and a retrospective, ending with feedback and consideration of subsequent actions. [program](https://www.microsolidarity.cc/practices/how-to-run-a-practice-program)

### Possible explanations

- **participant-explanation:** Repeated practice across progressively larger groupings may reduce the difficulty of approaching strangers for meaningful support. [program](https://www.microsolidarity.cc/practices/how-to-run-a-practice-program) **Alternative:** Self-selection, skilled hosts, or the novelty of the event may explain positive reactions more than the sequence itself.

- **participant-explanation:** A crew’s recurring commitment may help turn retreat enthusiasm into sustained support. [proposal](https://www.microsolidarity.cc/articles/2-update-2020) **Alternative:** Existing friendships, available time, and compatible work may determine continuity regardless of the crew label.

### Research connections

- **Microsolidarity’s nested relational scales — Practitioner framework and working proposal.** It distinguishes personal reflection, pairs, small crews, and a larger congregation rather than assuming one group size serves every purpose. [proposal](https://www.microsolidarity.cc/articles/2-update-2020) **Transfer limit:** The proposed sizes organize different kinds of interaction; they are not established optimal group sizes, and the program is peer support rather than clinical care.

### Questions to take into practice

- A crew becomes consequential through the help exchanged after the program, not just the closeness felt during it.

- Optional disclosure lets people seek peer support without being required to expose private experiences.

- Hosting and follow-up require time even when the eventual relationships are voluntary.

### What remains uncertain

- How many crews continued meeting, what help changed hands, and why did others dissolve?

- The experience of people who withdrew or felt uncomfortable is less visible than the organizers’ positive accounts.

### Sources

- **proposal:** [Update 2020: Three Essential Pillars for a New Mutual Aid Community](https://www.microsolidarity.cc/articles/2-update-2020). Microsolidarity / Richard D. Bartlett · participant-account · accessed as sections. Read proposal, Enspiral relationship, and crew integration hypothesis; assertions are explicitly personal guesses.

- **program:** [How To Run a Practice Program](https://www.microsolidarity.cc/practices/how-to-run-a-practice-program). Microsolidarity / Richard D. Bartlett and Nati Lombardo · participant-account · accessed as full-text. Complete short program account read; actual delivery described but no published outcome dataset.

## Buurtzorg: small nursing teams with a shared backbone

**Question:** What replaces middle management when a professional service scales?

**Setting:** Founded 2006; evaluations and organizational studies 2015–2025 · Netherlands

**Technologies:** Project teams (T163), Delegated authority (T115), Subsidiarity (T116), Standard operating procedures (T188)

Buurtzorg organizes home nursing around small teams that coordinate patients, schedules, recruitment, and local relationships. Coaches, software, and a central office support those teams. The distinctive arrangement is not simply removing supervisors: it redistributes managerial work while retaining shared infrastructure and relying on qualified professionals delivering a locally organized service.

### Outcome: Mixed outcomes

Buurtzorg grew substantially and attracted favorable patient and staff assessments. A historical KPMG comparison found relatively low adjusted home-care costs but roughly average total healthcare costs. Lower spending within the nursing service therefore did not translate into an equivalent reduction across all care, and the contribution of team autonomy remains difficult to separate from nursing expertise and patient differences.

### Documented observations

- Teams carried responsibilities normally assigned to managers, with coaches and a central office supplying support. [organization](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s41469-024-00184-y)

- Adjusted home-care expenditure was below the comparison average, while total healthcare expenditure was around average. [evaluation](https://www.commonwealthfund.org/publications/case-study/2015/may/home-care-self-governing-nursing-teams-netherlands-buurtzorg-model)

- Qualified nurses coordinated relatively separable local services, reducing the need for every daily decision to pass through a central office. [organization](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s41469-024-00184-y)

### Possible explanations

- **plausible:** Local decision rights may reduce handoffs between the person who knows a patient and the person authorizing care. [organization](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s41469-024-00184-y) **Alternative:** Professional selection, nursing skill, or continuity of staffing could account for benefits without the entire governance model.

- **supported-in-case:** Shared administrative support may be as important as team autonomy to keeping coordination manageable. [organization](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s41469-024-00184-y) **Alternative:** A different service with heavy cross-team dependencies may need more centralized scheduling and integration.

### Research connections

- **Functional substitutes for hierarchy — Case-based organizational explanation.** Managerial functions still need owners when managerial positions disappear. [organization](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s41469-024-00184-y) **Transfer limit:** Services with tightly interdependent teams may require more central integration than local home-nursing teams do.

### Questions to take into practice

- List the work performed by managers before deciding which positions to remove.

- Measure total costs and patient outcomes, not just the budget line that the new team controls.

### What remains uncertain

- How much of the cost difference reflected the patients served, staffing continuity, or nursing expertise rather than decision rights?

- The workload of coaches, teams that struggled, and care supplied informally by relatives may change the apparent balance of costs and benefits.

Journal of Organization Design / Martela and Nandram · primary-research · accessed as sections. Read organizational mechanisms and boundary conditions; descriptive case, not an intervention trial.

## East London: importing self-management into an NHS organization

**Question:** What happens when an autonomous-team model meets an existing institution?

**Setting:** Pilot 2017–2018; evaluation published 2019 · Tower Hamlets, London, United Kingdom

**Technologies:** Project teams (T163), Delegated authority (T115), Subsidiarity (T116), Institutional imitation (D012)

An East London community nursing pilot adapted Buurtzorg within an established NHS provider. Nurses sought more continuous relationships with patients and greater control of daily work, while the parent organization retained significant operational and financial authority. Changing the team format did not automatically transfer the surrounding permissions over staffing and spending.

### Outcome: Mixed outcomes

Participants valued continuity and accessibility but also encountered staffing difficulties and disagreement over how much autonomy nurses actually had. The evaluation included 23 interviews, only four with patients or carers, so those favorable experiences give a limited view of patient outcomes. Clinical and financial advantages over ordinary services remained unresolved.

### Documented observations

- Patient and carer perspectives came from four interviewees within a 23-interview evaluation, limiting how widely their experiences can be generalized. [pilot](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10088609/1/s12913-019-4804-8.pdf)

- Nurses described meaningful clinical discretion but limitations on operational and budgetary autonomy; managerial interpretations differed. [pilot](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10088609/1/s12913-019-4804-8.pdf)

- Buurtzorg-inspired teams in other settings have also encountered constraints from their parent organizations and national health systems, alongside reported communication benefits. [review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38745628/)

### Possible explanations

- **supported-in-case:** Unchanged organizational approval systems can limit autonomy even when teams are described as self-managing. [pilot](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10088609/1/s12913-019-4804-8.pdf) **Alternative:** Some retained controls may respond to statutory accountability or temporary implementation risks rather than simple resistance.

- **plausible:** Continuity may be a more transferable mechanism than the complete Dutch organizational structure. [pilot](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10088609/1/s12913-019-4804-8.pdf) [review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38745628/) **Alternative:** A small pilot with committed staff and favorable relationships may deliver attention that routine services cannot sustain.

### Research connections

No dedicated theory connection assessed.

### Questions to take into practice

- Specify which staffing, spending, and clinical decisions transfer before adopting a new team label.

- Evaluate the adaptation against local aims and constraints, not only fidelity to an admired foreign model.

### What remains uncertain

- Could the pilot sustain staffing, continuity, and patient outcomes as it expanded?

- How would total resource use compare with ordinary services once the support needed to establish the pilot was included?

## Haier: markets and microenterprises inside a giant company

**Question:** How much autonomy can small enterprises have within a large corporate platform?

**Setting:** RenDanHeYi from 2005; microenterprise reform from 2014 · China and international subsidiaries

**Technologies:** Autonomous microenterprise units (T167), Markets (T211), Revenue sharing (T258), Delegated authority (T115), Founder dependence (D013)

Haier reorganized parts of its business into small entrepreneurial units with substantial discretion over work, staffing, and remuneration. ### Outcome: Outcome evidence uncertain

Haier substantially redesigned its management, with different implementations across international businesses. By 2018 the newer microenterprise system was still young. Executives later associated it with growth and faster innovation, but acquisitions, investment, and market conditions also changed; the governance contribution is difficult to disentangle.

### Documented observations

- Small units gained discretion over recruitment and pay within a continuing corporate platform, with ownership stakes varying across arrangements. [case](https://oro.open.ac.uk/56044/1/Haier%27s%20Rendanheyi%20%28CMR%202018%29%20final%20accepted%20version.pdf)

- **participant-explanation:** Direct exposure to customer demand may help units test opportunities without routing every decision through a functional hierarchy. [case](https://oro.open.ac.uk/56044/1/Haier%27s%20Rendanheyi%20%28CMR%202018%29%20final%20accepted%20version.pdf) [later](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-032-36361-9_9) **Alternative:** Industry demand, access to capital, distribution, and acquisitions may explain commercial results independently of the structure.

- **plausible:** Local autonomy may depend on centrally maintained interfaces and resources rather than a general retreat of leadership. [case](https://oro.open.ac.uk/56044/1/Haier%27s%20Rendanheyi%20%28CMR%202018%29%20final%20accepted%20version.pdf) [later](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-032-36361-9_9) **Alternative:** Informal founder authority may resolve coordination problems that formal platform rules alone cannot handle.

### Research connections

- **Organizational modularity — Conceptual explanation consistent with the case.** Small units can experiment while shared platforms connect them. [later](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-032-36361-9_9) **Transfer limit:** When production dependencies are dense, a local experiment can disrupt other units; shared platforms still need ways to manage those connections.

### Questions to take into practice

- Distinguish a unit’s operating discretion, ownership, and exposure to financial losses.

- Identify which shared services and integration decisions must remain reliable as units multiply.

### What remains uncertain

- How were income risk, unsuccessful units, and disputes handled within the microenterprise system?

- Executive accounts leave workers’ experiences and unsuccessful transfers less visible than the intended advantages.

At the tomato processor Morning Star, individual missions and agreements among colleagues define responsibilities between people whose activities depend on one another. The commitment system gave individuals substantial responsibility while requiring time to persuade colleagues and resolve disagreements. - **plausible:** Writing mutual commitments may make dependencies visible before missed work becomes a supervisory problem. - **supported-in-case:** Removing routine command can shift coordination costs into negotiation rather than eliminate them. [visit](https://www.nuffieldscholar.org/sites/default/files/2021-04/Matthew%20Gunningham_Nuffield%20Report%20FINAL.pdf) **Alternative:** Longer negotiation could be worthwhile if it produces better decisions; the balance between those gains and the time spent remains unclear.

- Include coordination time when comparing self-management with conventional supervision.

- Compensation decisions, ownership authority, and the experiences of people who left remain important to understanding how much power the agreements distributed.

FAVI organized production around customer-focused teams, giving workers substantial room to coordinate and improve daily work. Control moved partly into relationships with customers and colleagues.

Workers participated in consequential production and team decisions while remaining subject to customer standards and pressure to fit their teams. Authority changed form rather than disappearing. Whether that change produced superior economic performance, and how its benefits and burdens were distributed among workers, remains uncertain.

[participation](https://www.annales.org/gc/GC-english-language-online-edition/2017/GC-2017-03-gilbert.pdf) **Alternative:** Some peer expectations may protect reliability and shared work rather than enforce arbitrary conformity.

Semco became a prominent example of participative management through employee involvement in work arrangements, supervisor selection, and business choices. Semco continued operating and grew commercially while expanding employee involvement. - **plausible:** Distributed operating knowledge may reduce dependence on the chief executive’s everyday availability. [profile](https://www.strategy-business.com/article/05408) **Alternative:** A temporary absence leaves ownership, prior strategy, and trusted senior colleagues intact; permanent succession is a harder test.

## GitLab: written context with one accountable decision-maker

**Question:** How can a distributed team combine wide input with clear decisions?

**Setting:** Public handbook practices documented through 2026 · Distributed international company

**Technologies:** Single accountable decision maker (T117), Standard operating procedures (T188), Decision logs (T034), Delegated authority (T115)

GitLab combines durable written context with a named person responsible for a decision. Its handbook asks workers to update documentation when practices change, while a directly responsible individual seeks relevant input and then decides. This pairs accessible organizational memory with differentiated authority in a company whose workers cannot rely on being in the same room.

### Outcome: Outcome evidence uncertain

GitLab makes decision ownership and documentation procedures publicly inspectable. Writing a durable update can initially take longer than sending a message, creating a recurring tension between immediate convenience and future usefulness. The written rules show how the arrangement is intended to work; how consistently people follow them and whether they save time overall remain open questions.

### Documented observations

- A directly responsible individual consults stakeholders but holds ultimate responsibility for the decision without needing everyone’s agreement. [dri](https://handbook.gitlab.com/handbook/people-group/directly-responsible-individuals/)

- The proposed workflow connects issues, edits, merging, and announcements so a changed practice has a durable written location. [handbook](https://handbook.gitlab.com/handbook/about/handbook-usage/)

- Quicker conversations can bypass the handbook, so keeping the shared record current requires continuing effort. [handbook](https://handbook.gitlab.com/handbook/about/handbook-usage/)

### Possible explanations

- **plausible:** A named decision owner can prevent consultation from becoming an unresolved demand for unanimity. [dri](https://handbook.gitlab.com/handbook/people-group/directly-responsible-individuals/) **Alternative:** If relevant knowledge is missed or authority is mistrusted, a faster decision may simply move conflict downstream.

- **participant-explanation:** Updating shared context at the time of change may reduce repeated explanation across locations and time zones. [handbook](https://handbook.gitlab.com/handbook/about/handbook-usage/) **Alternative:** The benefit depends on discoverability and reading habits; a large stale handbook could instead increase search costs.

- Treat documentation maintenance as work with owners rather than an optional cleanup activity.

- How often does documentation become stale, and who bears the effort of finding and repairing it?

## Loomio: different rooms have different decision rights

**Question:** What must an online discussion space specify before its decisions mean anything?

**Setting:** Handbook mainly circa 2016; explicit archive update in 2022 · Wellington, New Zealand; distributed collaboration

**Technologies:** Worker cooperatives (T251), Working groups (T164), Delegated authority (T115), Decision logs (T034)

Loomio is both decision-making software and a worker cooperative that uses it. Its archived handbook distinguishes spaces for co-owners, workers, directors, and public contributors, with different kinds of authority. The example shows how participation software depends on social agreements about membership, facilitation, privacy, and the consequences of a vote.

### Outcome: Outcome evidence uncertain

Loomio’s earlier arrangement separated binding decisions from advice and gave working groups a route to escalate matters beyond their mandates. The cooperative later became smaller and changed its practices; the circa-2016 rules describe that earlier design, not its current procedures. Their practical effect on disputed decisions and participation remains unclear.

### Documented observations

- Each online group was expected to specify its purpose, membership, facilitation, privacy, and the meaning of its decisions. [practice](https://www.loomio.coop/using_loomio.html)

- Co-owner, worker, and board spaces held different binding powers; public groups could be advisory or lack decision authority. Working groups could escalate decisions beyond their mandates. [practice](https://www.loomio.coop/using_loomio.html)

- By 2022, the operational team was smaller, and many procedures preserved in the handbook belonged to an earlier phase. [archive](https://www.loomio.coop/)

### Possible explanations

- **plausible:** Explicitly distinguishing recommendations from binding decisions may prevent apparent participation from creating false expectations. [practice](https://www.loomio.coop/using_loomio.html) **Alternative:** Participants may still infer authority from visibility or status unless the distinction is repeatedly explained.

- **participant-explanation:** Persistent discussion can include colleagues absent from a particular meeting, but participation still needs active facilitation. [practice](https://www.loomio.coop/using_loomio.html) **Alternative:** Digital access and available attention may reproduce exclusion even when conversations remain open longer.

### Research connections

No dedicated theory connection assessed.

### Questions to take into practice

- Label who a discussion includes and what authority its eventual result carries.

- Give small working groups a path for escalating decisions beyond their mandate.

### What remains uncertain

- How did participants resolve disputes about which group had authority?

- Which members participated consistently, and how did the later reduction in team size change decision-making?

<a id="occupy-wall-street-assemblies"></a>

## Occupy Wall Street: the assembly and the time it consumes

**Question:** When does open participation need smaller delegated structures?

**Setting:** New York occupation in 2011 and subsequent organizing · Zuccotti Park, New York City, United States

**Technologies:** Assemblies (T101), Consensus process (T123), Working groups (T164), Spokescouncils (T321)

Occupy Wall Street made a general assembly central to its political practice, using modified consensus and public discussion. Working groups carried out practical tasks, and organizers later introduced a spokescouncil to coordinate them. This was both a way to organize a protest and an attempt to enact the more participatory society participants wanted.

### Outcome: Mixed outcomes

The occupation created solidarity, participation, and a visible alternative political culture while also encountering long meetings, obstruction, and unequal ability to spend time in deliberation. Introducing a spokescouncil changed how working groups coordinated. Police eviction sharply interrupted the experiment, so organizational difficulties alone do not explain its end.

### Documented observations

- Modified consensus included a high-threshold fallback when a proposal remained blocked, rather than requiring unanimous approval in every instance. [analysis](https://escholarship.org/content/qt2fx026tj/qt2fx026tj_noSplash_1e64d552ca0f9bfbcf43d844a439cf95.pdf)

- Meeting duration and disruption became problems, and a spokescouncil was approved in late October 2011. [analysis](https://escholarship.org/content/qt2fx026tj/qt2fx026tj_noSplash_1e64d552ca0f9bfbcf43d844a439cf95.pdf)

- Assemblies and collective life drew on earlier radical traditions. Eviction ended the occupation of the site, interrupting the development of its institutions. [history](https://usabroad.unibo.it/article/download/9869/10504)

### Possible explanations

- **contested:** Direct participation may strengthen commitment among people sharing the risks of an occupation. [analysis](https://escholarship.org/content/qt2fx026tj/qt2fx026tj_noSplash_1e64d552ca0f9bfbcf43d844a439cf95.pdf) **Alternative:** Shared danger, material support, and the emotional force of protest may generate solidarity independently of consensus rules.

- **supported-in-case:** An open meeting can give disproportionate influence to people with abundant time. [analysis](https://escholarship.org/content/qt2fx026tj/qt2fx026tj_noSplash_1e64d552ca0f9bfbcf43d844a439cf95.pdf) **Alternative:** Speaking norms, facilitation skill, and existing social status may matter as much as time availability.

### Research connections

- **Prefigurative politics — Historical and participant interpretive framework.** Organizing practices are intended to embody desired social relations in the present. [history](https://usabroad.unibo.it/article/download/9869/10504) **Transfer limit:** Experiencing a different political relationship does not by itself establish durable institutional capacity or broader policy change.

### Questions to take into practice

- Measure the time cost of participation and who cannot afford it.

- Distinguish decisions needing a whole assembly from work that can be delegated with accountability.

### What remains uncertain

- Whose participation diminished as meetings lengthened, and did the spokescouncil change that pattern?

- Which relationships and organizing capacities survived the eviction? New York’s trajectory was only one part of the wider Occupy movement.

### Sources

- **analysis:** [What the Left Can Learn from Occupy Wall Street](https://escholarship.org/content/qt2fx026tj/qt2fx026tj_noSplash_1e64d552ca0f9bfbcf43d844a439cf95.pdf). Studies in Political Economy / James K. Rowe and Myles Carroll · history · accessed as sections. Read horizontalism, participation limits, and spokescouncil sections; interpretive political analysis.

- **history:** [The Occupy Wall Street Movement in the American Radical Tradition](https://usabroad.unibo.it/article/download/9869/10504). USAbroad · history · accessed as sections. Read radical traditions, horizontal movement, prefiguration, and occupation chronology; uses participant accounts and secondary literature.

## Alcoholics Anonymous: local groups and shared traditions

**Question:** What does evidence for a peer fellowship tell us about its governance?

**Setting:** Traditions endorsed in 1950; treatment evidence reviewed in 2020 · International fellowship; research largely in the United States

**Technologies:** Peer support groups (T099), Principle-based norms (T085), Subsidiarity (T116), Federations (T143)

Alcoholics Anonymous combines local peer groups with shared traditions concerning autonomy, service, anonymity, and financial independence. Local groups have room to organize their activities while remaining connected to a recognizable fellowship. Professional Twelve-Step Facilitation can help people engage with AA, but its clinical effects answer a different question from how the fellowship distributes authority.

### Outcome: Mixed outcomes

For adults with alcohol use disorder, a 2020 Cochrane review found strong evidence that manualized AA/Twelve-Step Facilitation improved continuous abstinence compared with some established alternatives, while other outcomes were often similar. That result concerns a defined route into participation, not a guarantee about any meeting or person. It also leaves open which organizational practices contributed to the benefit.

### Documented observations

- Shared traditions allow local autonomy within limits concerning other groups, place leaders in service to their groups, and call for financial self-support. [traditions](https://www.alcoholics-anonymous.org.uk/about-aa/what-is-aa/traditions/)

- Accountable service boards and employed specialist workers support the fellowship. Local autonomy and nonprofessional peer relationships coexist with organizational infrastructure. [traditions](https://www.alcoholics-anonymous.org.uk/about-aa/what-is-aa/traditions/)

- Clinical comparisons differed in their facilitation methods, alternatives, and measured outcomes. The strongest abstinence finding concerned structured, manualized AA/Twelve-Step Facilitation. [review](https://www.cochrane.org/evidence/CD012880_alcoholics-anonymous-aa-and-other-12-step-programs-alcohol-use-disorder)

### Possible explanations

- **plausible:** Shared traditions may help local groups reproduce a recognizable practice without requiring detailed central administration. [traditions](https://www.alcoholics-anonymous.org.uk/about-aa/what-is-aa/traditions/) **Alternative:** Informal norms, literature, and experienced members may do more coordinating work than formal statements alone.

- **plausible:** Peer relationships may help sustain participation, but the outcome evidence does not identify governance as the mechanism. [review](https://www.cochrane.org/evidence/CD012880_alcoholics-anonymous-aa-and-other-12-step-programs-alcohol-use-disorder) **Alternative:** Professional facilitation, motivation, social-network change, and selection into attendance may contribute in different combinations.

### Research connections

No dedicated theory connection assessed.

### Questions to take into practice

- Separate evidence for participation or facilitation from evidence for particular organizational rules.

- Study service infrastructure and local accountability alongside the rhetoric of autonomy.

### What remains uncertain

- How do differences in local governance affect participation, accountability, and experiences of exclusion?

- The clinical findings principally concern noncoerced treatment contexts for adults with alcohol use disorder; whether similar organizational arrangements help unrelated peer groups is a separate question.

### Sources

- **traditions:** [12 Traditions](https://www.alcoholics-anonymous.org.uk/about-aa/what-is-aa/traditions/). Alcoholics Anonymous Great Britain · institutional-record · accessed as full-text. Read complete traditions and historical introduction; normative organizational text, not outcome research.

- **review:** [Alcoholics Anonymous (AA) and other 12-step programs for alcohol use disorder](https://www.cochrane.org/evidence/CD012880_alcoholics-anonymous-aa-and-other-12-step-programs-alcohol-use-disorder). Cochrane / Kelly, Humphreys and Ferri · review · accessed as sections. Read review summary, study scope, and outcome distinctions; AA and professionally delivered TSF must not be conflated.

## Guerrilla Translation and DisCO: making care visible in the accounts

**Question:** Can a collective pay for commons work without ignoring the work of maintaining itself?

**Setting:** Guerrilla Translation/Media Collective model, redesigned in 2018; later DisCO documentation · Spain-based distributed collective

**Technologies:** Worker cooperatives (T251), Contribution records (T278), Revenue sharing (T258), Consent decision-making (T124), Trial membership (T061)

Guerrilla Translation and the wider Guerrilla Media Collective provided an early setting for DisCO governance. Their model distinguishes paid client work, freely shared translations, and the care and administration that sustain the collective. Contribution accounting and income allocation are intended to connect these activities, with membership commitments determining participation in binding decisions.

### Outcome: Outcome evidence uncertain

The collective developed and revised a governance model that connects commercial income, freely shared translations, and the care required to maintain the group. The accounting makes those commitments visible, but a recorded contribution is not cash in hand. Whether the arrangement consistently financed unpaid work or reduced unequal burdens remains unclear.

### Documented observations

- The model distinguishes livelihood, pro-bono, and care work, giving them different accounting treatment and linking participation rights to membership commitments. [model](https://mothership.disco.coop/Distributed_Cooperative_Organization_%28DisCO%29_Governance_Model_V_3.0)

- Guerrilla Media Collective became the first DisCO Lab and collaboratively redesigned its arrangements in 2018. [elements](https://disco.coop/wp-content/uploads/2023/10/DisCO_Elements_def_MONO.pdf)

- One version directs a defined share of available income toward previously recorded pro-bono contributions. The allocation rules can be revised as circumstances change. [elements](https://disco.coop/wp-content/uploads/2023/10/DisCO_Elements_def_MONO.pdf)

### Possible explanations

- **participant-explanation:** Recording maintenance and relational work may make unequal burdens discussable before they become invisible obligations. [model](https://mothership.disco.coop/Distributed_Cooperative_Organization_%28DisCO%29_Governance_Model_V_3.0) **Alternative:** Tracking can become another administrative burden, while the people doing care may still lack bargaining power.

- **participant-explanation:** Reserving income for commons contributions may sustain work that clients would not directly buy. [elements](https://disco.coop/wp-content/uploads/2023/10/DisCO_Elements_def_MONO.pdf) **Alternative:** The arrangement depends on adequate paid demand; accounting recognition cannot itself create money or guarantee timely payment.

### Research connections

- **Feminist economics and recognition of reproductive work — Explicit design commitment, not independently validated causal theory.** The model treats sustaining people and the collective as economically relevant activity. [elements](https://disco.coop/wp-content/uploads/2023/10/DisCO_Elements_def_MONO.pdf) **Transfer limit:** Recording care leaves difficult choices about its value, who judges it, and whether enough income exists to compensate the people doing it.

### Questions to take into practice

- Distinguish recognition of a contribution from cash actually available to compensate it.

- Track the cost of accounting and who decides what counts as care.

### What remains uncertain

- How long did recorded contributions remain unpaid, and what happened when members left or disputed their balances?

- Did tracking care redistribute its burden, or mainly give existing work a more visible record?

### Sources

- **model:** [Distributed Cooperative Organization (DisCO) Governance Model V 3.0](https://mothership.disco.coop/Distributed_Cooperative_Organization_%28DisCO%29_Governance_Model_V_3.0). DisCO Mothership / Guerrilla Media Collective · participant-account · accessed as sections. Read contribution streams, membership, decisions, and care accounting; versioned design documentation.

- **elements:** [Groove is in the Heart: The DisCO Elements](https://disco.coop/wp-content/uploads/2023/10/DisCO_Elements_def_MONO.pdf). DisCO.coop · participant-account · accessed as sections. Read chapter 4 historical origin and income allocation, pages 50–52; describes practice and normative ambitions without independent audit.

## ElderSpirit: choosing neighbors and organizing help

**Question:** How can a neighborhood make mutual support dependable without promising unlimited care?

**Setting:** Initial research published 2009; follow-up published 2013; current community guidance · Abingdon, Virginia, United States

**Technologies:** Cohousing communities (T071), Mutual aid networks (T065), Care circles (T066), Consensus process (T123), Working groups (T164)

ElderSpirit is an intentional elder cohousing community where residents organize shared life and neighborly assistance. Its support system asks a member to identify two coordinators who can help arrange practical assistance when needed. The case makes the social infrastructure of friendship concrete while explicitly distinguishing independent living and volunteer help from an assisted-living service.

### Outcome: Mixed outcomes

Residents described valued interdependence, comfort, and mutual support after an initially demanding period of community building. The early group was small and self-selected, with a narrow demographic composition. Its experience therefore leaves open how accessible the arrangement would be to people with different resources and needs. Volunteer support also had limits: some residents needed outside help or a different living arrangement.

### Documented observations

- The 33 charter residents studied in 2009 were all white and mostly women. They had actively chosen the community, with mutual support an important motivation. [initial](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/02763890903326970)

- Residents later described improvement after the initial setup work, alongside disputes about participation, privacy concerns, and physical-design barriers. [followup](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/257523024_Lessons_Learned_From_a_New_Elder_Cohousing_Community)

- Members identify two neighborly support coordinators to help arrange short-term practical assistance. The arrangement has limits when independent living is no longer feasible. [practice](https://elderspirit.org/mutualsupport)

### Possible explanations

- **plausible:** Named coordinators may lower the effort required to ask for help and distribute requests among neighbors. [practice](https://elderspirit.org/mutualsupport) **Alternative:** Pre-existing relationships and residents’ willingness to help may matter more than the coordinator role itself.

- **plausible:** An initially difficult formation period may build the agreements that make later cooperation easier. [followup](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/257523024_Lessons_Learned_From_a_New_Elder_Cohousing_Community) **Alternative:** Adaptation, selective departures, or changing expectations could also explain more favorable later reports.

### Research connections

No dedicated theory connection assessed.

### Questions to take into practice

- Agree on practical help, responsibility for coordinating it, and limits before a crisis occurs.

- Budget time for building community agreements rather than assuming shared housing produces support automatically.

### What remains uncertain

- What happened to people who left, and how evenly did remaining neighbors share the care work?

- Could people with fewer resources or different access needs obtain comparable support?

- The early accounts came from a self-selected group and a researcher previously involved in community planning, leaving dissatisfied and outside perspectives less visible.
