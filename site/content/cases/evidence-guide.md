# How to learn from these cases

A successful experiment can make a new way of organizing seem obvious. A spectacular failure can make it seem foolish. To learn from either, start with what people were trying to accomplish, what happened, and what else could explain the result. These cases explore particular arrangements; their selection does not tell us how often each method succeeds.

## Five things to keep separate

1. **Arrangement:** what participants actually did, including ownership, eligibility, informal authority, money, and unpaid work.
2. **Observed outcome:** what changed, over what period, for whom, and against which comparison. A demonstration, adoption, sustained operation, and public benefit are different outcomes.
3. **Explanation:** a participant's interpretation or a plausible causal hypothesis. Each dossier includes alternatives that could explain the same observations.
4. **Research connection:** a framework or body of studies that makes a mechanism worth considering. A well-studied mechanism does not establish that it caused this case's outcome.
5. **Transfer question:** what must be true in a new setting, and what observation would make us reconsider the idea?

Success, mixed, failure, and uncertain refer to the question stated in each case. They are not verdicts on whole organizations or societies. Successful coordination can serve harmful ends. Excluded participants, displaced costs, and burdens on maintenance workers matter alongside the organizer's objective.

## Research traditions worth using

### Governing shared resources

Cox, Arnold, and Villamayor Tomás reviewed 91 studies of Ostrom's design principles. The review supports taking boundaries, locally appropriate rules, participation, monitoring, sanctions, and conflict resolution seriously. Much of this evidence is comparative and observational; the principles are not a checklist that guarantees success. Ask which rights are enforceable and whether costs move outside the measured area. [Review and repository record](https://dlc.dlib.indiana.edu/dlc/items/aabc541a-fdc4-4b75-af1f-588d0fee2a78/full).

### Motivation and incentives

Cerasoli, Nicklin, and Ford's meta-analysis combines 183 studies. Intrinsic motivation and incentives can both predict performance, with different relationships to quality, quantity, and incentive salience. Neither “money always works” nor “payment destroys motivation” is a useful general rule. Much of this synthesis concerns associations, not a single randomized intervention. [Research abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24491020/).

### Psychological safety

Frazier and colleagues synthesized 136 independent samples on psychological safety and its relationships with workplace outcomes. This is a substantial research literature, but associations alone do not establish whether safety causes performance, performance creates safety, or leadership affects both. [Meta-analysis](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/peps.12183).

### Implementation and context

Durlak and DuPre reviewed more than 500 quantitative studies in prevention and health promotion, finding implementation consequential for outcomes. Their domain is not all organizations. The useful question is whether the intended practice actually occurred and what supported it. Specify implementation measures before seeing results; otherwise “it was implemented badly” can become an unfalsifiable excuse. [Review abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18322790/).

### Inducement prizes

Brunt, Lerner, and Nicholas studied historical agricultural competitions, connecting prize incentives with entry and subsequent patenting. The setting provides evidence beyond promotional accounts, but patents and entries are not equivalent to commercial adoption or social welfare. Compare this evidence with the catalog's demonstration-to-service gaps. [Research paper](https://www.hbs.edu/ris/Publication%20Files/IPI_0f59d8cc-752c-4606-8c4a-f55d46caa35c.pdf).

### Organizational learning

Argote and Miron-Spektor synthesize research on how experience becomes organizational knowledge, including the role of context. This is a conceptual synthesis of a research tradition, not a universal causal law. Ask whether capability resides in documents, people, tools, relationships, or repeated practice—and whether it survives turnover. [Research synthesis](https://doi.org/10.1287/orsc.1100.0621).

### Leadership succession

The Japanese adopted-heir and Danish family-succession studies use different settings and research designs. Their contrast is useful precisely because “family succession” bundles different selection and accountability arrangements. Neither settles whether adoption would improve leadership elsewhere. See the Japanese succession dossier for both sources and identification limits.

### Interpretive frameworks and emerging practices

Great Founder Theory is a source of questions about founders, succession, prestige, and institutional knowledge. Its selected historical examples are not independent tests of the theory. Extitutional theory draws attention to relationships and coordination beyond formal roles; this catalog does not establish it as a validated causal theory. LiquidO and microsolidarity offer concrete designs and practitioner interpretations. Their presence here does not imply an independent body of outcome evaluations. The relevant dossiers identify the evidence actually available.

## Competing explanations to investigate

| Observation | One hypothesis | A serious alternative | Useful next evidence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A self-managed organization performs well | Distributed decisions improve local judgment | Selective hiring, favorable markets, or founder intervention account for performance | Comparable teams, decision logs, departures, and performance before and after adoption |
| A community feels unusually supportive | Repeated small-group practice builds trust | People already inclined toward closeness select into it; dissatisfied members leave | Newcomer and leaver accounts, participation costs, repeated measures |
| A prize produces a prototype | The reward induces otherwise absent effort | Teams would have worked anyway; publicity or procurement matters more | Pre-announcement plans, unsuccessful entrants, subsequent customers and service delivery |
| A commons survives | Local rule-making creates legitimacy | State enforcement, favorable ecology, or restricted access is decisive | Rule changes, enforcement records, excluded users, neighboring resource outcomes |
| A founder's departure precedes decline | Tacit competence was not transmitted | A market shock or funding loss would have hurt any successor | Timing, comparable organizations, transfer records, and successor authority |
| A formal reform has no measurable benefit | The design is ineffective | Adoption was nominal, the measure missed benefits, or the study lacked precision | Implementation measures, confidence intervals, costs, and prespecified outcomes |

These are research questions, not findings established across every case.

## Different evidence answers different questions

Participant accounts are valuable evidence of design and lived experience. Institutional records can establish decisions, dates, and reported results. Neither automatically supplies an independent assessment. Several publications can repeat the same original evidence; source count is not a count of independent confirmations.

Randomized studies strengthen causal inference for the tested intervention and population. Historical comparison, ethnography, archival work, and process tracing answer other important questions. A null result is not proof of no possible effect; an average benefit does not mean everyone benefits. A systematic review is only as informative as its included studies, outcomes, and search scope.

## Bringing an idea into another setting

Before borrowing an arrangement, consider what made it possible: funding, experience, legal authority, trust, or years of work together. Which of those conditions exist in your setting? Decide what improvement you hope to see and how you will notice costs falling on other people. A case is most useful when it helps you ask better questions about a real choice.
