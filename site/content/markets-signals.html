Imagine that a storm interrupts a major supply of timber. As available stocks tighten, its price rises. A furniture maker looks for ways to use less wood. A builder considers another material. A supplier decides that bringing timber from farther away is now worth the cost.

These people do not need to know one another, or even know about the storm. The price carries enough information to change their decisions. Each combines that signal with knowledge of their own circumstances: what can be substituted, which orders can wait, and where more supply might be found.

This is the remarkable economy of knowledge coordinated through prices. A change in one place can prompt adjustments elsewhere without anyone having to understand or direct the entire response.
