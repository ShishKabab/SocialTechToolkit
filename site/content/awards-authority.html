The recipient is only one part of the story. Someone has to give the award, and other people have to care.

An award is a mutual endorsement. The organizer says, “This person deserves your respect.” By accepting, the recipient also lends their reputation to the organizer: “These people are qualified to judge work like mine.” The audience decides whether to believe either claim.

Imagine starting an award for outstanding cooking in your city. A website, a trophy, and a cash payment would let you announce a winner. But why should anyone trust your taste? Bringing in respected chefs as judges could give the award credibility. Honoring a cook whose food people already love could do the same. In both cases, the award begins by drawing on respect that people have already earned.

This also explains why a scientist can be highly regarded by colleagues yet almost unknown outside their field. Most people cannot evaluate the research themselves. An award can carry that recognition across the boundary: you may not understand the discovery, but you recognize the institution honoring it.

Before judges can recognize excellent work, they need a way to find it. Peer nominations let people familiar with a contribution bring it forward and explain its importance. A cook working outside the city’s fashionable restaurants may otherwise never enter consideration. The nomination supplies evidence and advocacy; it does not itself award the honor.

A peer jury then brings relevant experience to the comparison. Its remit should say whether it selects the recipient or recommends one to another decision maker, and when a judge must step aside because of a close connection. Credible recognition depends on both whose work reaches the judges and how they exercise their judgment.
