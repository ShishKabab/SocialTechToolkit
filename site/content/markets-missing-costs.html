Some people affected by a transaction never get to participate in it. A factory and its customers may agree on a price that covers production while leaving the people downstream to bear the cost of polluted water. The product looks cheap because part of its cost has been passed to someone else.

These are externalities: effects missing from the transaction’s price. They can also be benefits. Research may help many people who never pay its funders, leaving less incentive to support it than its wider usefulness would justify.

Shared benefits create a related difficulty. An effective flood-warning system can protect people whether or not they contribute to its upkeep. That makes it valuable, but can make charging for it difficult. Without another way to organize contributions, something people collectively need may remain underfunded.

The intelligence of buyers and sellers cannot fully account for consequences that their decisions give them no reason to consider. Future generations face this problem especially sharply: they cannot enter today’s transactions to defend their interests.
