Even if you select capable, public-spirited people, they now hold power over others. They may start protecting their position, favoring their associates, or treating criticism as evidence that outsiders do not understand the responsibilities of government.

The classical distinction between aristocracy and oligarchy turns on this issue. Rule by a few for the common benefit is the ideal; rule serving the wealthy rulers themselves is its oligarchic distortion. Calling the rulers excellent does not tell you which one you actually have.

Good intentions also leave room for serious disagreement. A council may favor a new dam because it will supply electricity to a region; the people whose homes would be flooded may reasonably oppose it. Technical knowledge helps establish the consequences. It does not, by itself, settle whose losses are acceptable or who should have a say.

People outside the governing circle can know things the rulers miss. Even the ancient discussion allowed that many people’s judgments might be better in combination than those of a few outstanding individuals. Excluding people from decisions can mean excluding knowledge as well as denying them influence.
