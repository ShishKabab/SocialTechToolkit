A researched example: who gets a meeting?

Kalla and Broockman’s field experiment involved meeting requests to 191 US congressional offices. Researchers randomly varied whether offices were told that prospective attendees were campaign donors. Revealing donor status made senior policymakers available more often.

What this establishes: in that experiment, information about donor status affected access. What it does not establish: that meetings changed votes, that donations purchased a particular policy, or that every form of lobbying operates through money.

A possible explanation: help allies do their work

Hall and Deardorff’s legislative-subsidy model describes lobbyists supplying information and work to officials who already share their objectives. Influence may operate by helping an ally act, rather than changing an opponent’s mind. This is an explanatory model; its abstract alone does not establish how often that mechanism dominates.
