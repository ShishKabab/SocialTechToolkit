An award recognizes an achievement; a prize competition promises a reward for reaching a specified goal. The names can be misleading. The Nobel Prize, for example, primarily honors achievements rather than setting contestants a challenge.

A prize says, “Do this and receive the reward.” An award says, “Look at what this person achieved.” The first gives participants a target; the second gives an audience an example of excellence. Both can motivate future work, but through different routes.

This distinction matters when the work is expensive. A payment available only after success does not supply the resources needed to begin. One approach is to fund promising work upfront, then honor what it achieves. Funding makes the work possible; recognition gives people a reason to take pride in doing it well.

A particular challenge can end when someone solves it. A recurring award can keep recognizing new achievements—and, if its judgment remains trusted, keep shaping what the community values.
