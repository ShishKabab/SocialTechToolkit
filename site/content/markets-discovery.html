People can also try different ways of meeting a need. Someone introduces a repair service, a cheaper material, or a more convenient delivery arrangement. Customers’ choices provide feedback, while the costs of labor and materials reveal what it takes to keep the offer going. Revenue and costs help determine which experiments can continue.

The coordination can work even when participants see very little of the whole. In early experimental markets, buyers and sellers who knew their own assigned values or costs—but not everyone else’s—traded toward prices and quantities close to the competitive prediction.

Those experiments demonstrate a real capacity under particular trading rules. They do not establish that every market finds the right answer. To understand its intelligence, we also have to ask what information it receives and what its feedback rewards.
