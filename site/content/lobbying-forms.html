• Direct lobbying: meeting officials, commenting on proposals, supplying evidence, or suggesting wording for a rule.
  • Collective representation: a union, trade association, or advocacy coalition pooling knowledge and resources to speak for shared interests.
  • Grassroots lobbying: mobilizing constituents to contact decision-makers themselves. A petition or a public campaign can be part of this.
  • Relationship-building: becoming a reliable contact whom officials consult as an issue develops.

These forms overlap. A coalition can mobilize supporters, meet officials, and provide specialist knowledge in the same campaign.

When lobbying goes wrong, it can turn into bribery: offering an improper benefit in exchange for a favorable decision. It can also contribute to capture, where the people meant to oversee an industry come to serve its interests. And sometimes the apparent public support is manufactured: a company funds a campaign that pretends to be an independent citizens’ movement. That is astroturfing.
