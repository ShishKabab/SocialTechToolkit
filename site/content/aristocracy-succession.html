A governing group that works well today still needs a way to renew itself. Its members grow old, circumstances change, and new people develop abilities the group needs.

Passing authority to children offers continuity and an opportunity to prepare them for responsibility. It does not establish that they have the judgment that justified their parents’ authority. Letting incumbents appoint successors can preserve experience, but can also reward loyalty over ability. Keeping a ruling circle closed makes it harder for outsiders to demonstrate what they could contribute.

This is why the ideal of rule by excellence should be distinguished from hereditary aristocracy. The latter makes descent a basis for membership. The two can overlap in a society’s beliefs about leadership, but an inherited position is not evidence of personal excellence. Ancient classifications already recognized mixtures of virtue, wealth, and birth, rather than a clean separation between an ideal and its compromises.

Making aristocracy live up to its promise would require more than finding good people once. It would require ways to discover overlooked ability, hear objections, replace failing leaders, and prepare successors without making office their entitlement. These are the arrangements that determine whether “rule of the best” remains a standard the rulers must meet or becomes a compliment they pay themselves.
