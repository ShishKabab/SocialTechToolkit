A market depends on other social technologies: ownership rules, contracts, standards, ways to settle disputes, and rules about who may trade what. Change these arrangements and you change the information and incentives the market works with.

Inspections and warranties can help buyers assess quality. Pollution charges or enforceable limits can make harmful effects harder to ignore. Public funding can support shared benefits that are difficult to sell. Rules that protect competition can make it easier for alternatives to emerge. Each response has its own costs and practical difficulties; improving the rules requires examining the particular problem.

The useful question is what we want this market to discover and coordinate—and what it needs help to see. Its ability to draw on dispersed knowledge is powerful. Choosing whose interests count, which harms are acceptable, and what everyone should have access to still requires collective judgment.
