Even participants may lack information that matters. A used-car seller knows about a recurring fault; a buyer cannot distinguish that car from a reliable one. Buyers who anticipate this risk offer less. Owners of good cars may then stop selling, making the remaining selection worse. Hidden quality can undermine the market itself.

Power also affects the signal. A dominant supplier may restrict output or keep competitors out. A high price can therefore reflect control over access as well as genuine difficulty in producing more. Counting participants is not enough to establish that competition is effective.

In markets for assets, people may buy because they expect to resell at a higher price. Rising prices can attract further buying, feeding the very expectation that drove the increase. Experimental asset markets have produced bubbles and crashes. Many people participating does not guarantee that their errors cancel out; they can reinforce one another.
