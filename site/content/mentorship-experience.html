Imagine someone organizing their first neighborhood project. They can read about running meetings and recruiting volunteers, but still struggle to recognize why people are losing interest, when a disagreement needs attention, or which responsibilities someone is ready to take on.

An experienced organizer can help them think through a difficult meeting afterward: who stopped speaking, which concern went unanswered, and what they might try next time. Over time, the newcomer begins to notice things they would previously have missed. This is tacit knowledge: judgment acquired through experience that is difficult to capture fully in instructions.

Mentorship gives that knowledge a route from one person to another. Conversations about real decisions, feedback on attempts, and opportunities to observe someone at work can shorten the distance between knowing the rules and knowing what to do.
