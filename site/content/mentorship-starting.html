You do not have to wait for someone to discover you. Asking for help can be the first demonstration of the initiative a mentor is looking for. It puts your interests, questions, and willingness to act in front of someone who might otherwise never encounter them.

A specific request gives the relationship somewhere to begin. You might ask an experienced organizer how they handled a disagreement, invite feedback on a proposal, or ask which book helped them understand a problem. The other person gets a manageable opportunity to help, and you get a chance to discover whether their guidance is useful.

The next conversation can grow out of what you did with the first: what you tried, what happened, and what you still cannot figure out. Over time, these exchanges can become mentorship without either person ever formally asking for that title.

Sometimes the route in is a contribution. If an organization has no role for you, a small, useful task—where help is welcome—can let people discover what you are capable of. Taking responsibility, following through, and gradually earning trust can open a path that an application alone did not. Persistence means looking for those opportunities while respecting a clear refusal.
