# Social Tech Toolkit: editorial guidance

This guidance applies throughout the repository. It records the owner's editorial decisions; follow their latest instructions when they refine it. Older outlines and research briefs are historical working documents and may contain superseded choices.

## What this project should communicate

Social technologies shape everyday life at every scale: making friends, learning, organizing a group, building institutions, and transforming society. Understanding them makes familiar arrangements visible and gives people more ways to make things happen.

These arrangements were designed, discovered through experimentation, and revised by people. We can invent, improve, combine, and use them ourselves. Convey that possibility through concrete explanations, without calling it “empowering.”

Keep the homepage introduction strong but relatively short. Its categories should help people move from society-wide arrangements down to small groups and everyday interactions. Titles can be lively, provocative, or funny when that helps understanding. Detailed analysis of democracy belongs on its own pages, not in the general introduction.

## Write for an interested general reader

- Explain why the idea matters and what makes its mechanism interesting. Show how it changes what people can accomplish together.
- Write a connected explanation. Each paragraph should follow naturally from the previous one; avoid abrupt jumps into disconnected lists of mechanics, examples, and caveats.
- Let the subject determine the structure and length. Do not generate articles from a repeated prose template or pad them to a word count. Use specific headings when useful; a short continuous narrative is also fine.
- Use concrete, varied examples that readers can imagine. Do not default to software or technical communities. Cooking is the established example on Awards, not a motif to carry through the catalog.
- Integrate difficulties into the explanation: how an arrangement fails, becomes distorted, excludes people, or depends on work that is easy to overlook. Explain the mechanism rather than appending generic warnings.
- Explain ideas directly. The reader is reading this website, not a narrated book summary. Avoid “Burja uses this example,” “the author argues,” and similar attribution that adds nothing to understanding. Attribution is appropriate when the origin itself matters.
- Preserve distinctions between related concepts. Similarity is not equivalence, and a useful practice is not automatically a proven solution.

Do not restore the generic technology-page sections **“How it works,” “In practice,” “Conditions & tradeoffs,” or “When to use it.”** Do not restore filler such as “Illustrative situations to help make the idea concrete” or “These sources explain the concept; they do not establish that it will work in every setting.” Explain relevant limitations specifically instead.

Avoid detached framing such as “Keep related concepts distinct.” Work the distinction into the narrative—for example, explain what can happen when lobbying goes wrong. Do not bring back “Start with a situation, not a favorite method” as a general site instruction.

## Build a web of ideas

- When another catalog concept matters to an explanation, link the relevant phrase to its internal page. Do not automatically link every keyword or every occurrence.
- Attach external citations to the supporting claim, explanation, or verb, leaving concept names available for internal links. The Capitalism pattern is deliberate: **“combines”** links to the external explanation; **“private ownership”** and **“markets”** link to their technology pages.
- Link meaningfully. A single gift is not necessarily a gift economy; ordinary training is not necessarily cross-training. If a connection requires explanation, name the relationship explicitly or put it only in “Explore alongside.”
- Choose “Explore alongside” entries as components, complements, alternatives, or relevant social dynamics. Record why each relationship matters. Do not use alphabetical or same-category fallback lists.
- Include every concept linked in the explanatory text in “Explore alongside.” Provide useful paths from broad concepts to concrete practices as well as the reverse; not every relationship needs to be reciprocal.
- Do not add link-arrow or external-link icons.

## Sources and evidence

Use real, linkable sources. Link directly from the prose where a citation fits naturally. A page-local Sources section is acceptable for additional sources that do not fit naturally in the text; do not create a separate sources directory or send readers through source codes.

Research further where the existing evidence is insufficient. Distinguish documented observations, possible explanations, and theories with stronger research support. Practitioner descriptions explain a method; they do not establish its effectiveness. Historical success does not establish that a method transfers unchanged to another setting.

Keep source-access notes honest: reused research notes, search excerpts, abstracts, and full-text reading are different. Do not invent sources, quotations, named outcomes, or personal experiences. Make illustrative scenarios recognizable as examples without repeatedly labeling every sentence “hypothetical.”

Keep the research process out of reader-facing explanations. Notes about failed page access, what a research pass checked, disagreements with an unintroduced claim in GFT, and instructions to find more sources belong in research records. Do not publish them as observations, caveats, source descriptions, or automatic sections. A reader should understand each sentence from the surrounding article without knowing our conversation or the research brief. Preserve consequential uncertainty by explaining what is unknown about the event or mechanism itself; attribution is still necessary for disputed estimates and participants' interpretations. Omit empty research sections rather than announcing that no theory was assessed. Review whole paragraphs and articles, not just flagged phrases.

## Technologies, systems, dynamics, and cases

Explain reusable arrangements and their component practices. Broad systems can combine several technologies; social dynamics can be lenses for understanding what happens rather than methods to prescribe.

Use a practical editorial boundary: reusable arrangements that change how people coordinate, cooperate, exercise authority, or transmit knowledge. Explain the relationship and collective capability the arrangement creates. Involving people or enabling a transaction is not sufficient reason for a standalone entry.

Before adding or retaining an entry, ask:

- What becomes possible together?
- What arrangement produces that change: a promise, rule, role, incentive, relationship, or shared practice?
- Does the mechanism deserve its own explanation, or is this better treated as a component or example within another page?

Ordinary practices can reveal powerful coordination mechanisms; grand scale is not a requirement. Prefer richer connected explanations over a growing inventory of names. Merge or nest overlapping entries when they do not warrant distinct explanations, updating links and discovery data together. Do not treat every commercial feature as a social technology merely because it has customers.

Subscriptions sit near this boundary. Explain them as one form of recurring funding: commitments can sustain work over time, but their duration, cancellation terms, and concentration determine how much planning they support. Distinguish customer subscriptions from membership dues, patronage, and the explicit sharing of production risk in some community-supported agriculture arrangements. A recurring charge alone does not imply membership, shared governance, or shared risk.


Historical organizations and events belong in case studies that connect the technologies involved. **Athenian democracy is a case study only.** Do not recreate it as a technology entry, technology search result, or technology URL. Its case connects assemblies, sortition, elections, juries, and forms of democracy.

Case studies should help readers understand what worked, what failed, and what remains uncertain. Separate outcomes from hypotheses about their causes. Assess success or failure against a stated objective rather than giving an entire institution a sweeping verdict.

## Preserve the editorial work already done

Awards, Mentorship, Aristocracy, Markets, and Lobbying were developed closely with the owner. Use them as examples of the intended explanatory quality. Do not flatten them into the autonomous-draft style. Make requested corrections and useful link changes without gratuitously rewriting their narratives.

- Awards should explain how recognition directs groups' efforts at different scales. Preserve the cooking example and the distinction between retrospective recognition and a promised prize.
- Mentorship should explain tacit knowledge, opportunities, connections, developing potential, allies, and successors. Preserve the interview's practical possibilities: initiating contact, contributing, hosting gatherings, advice growing into involvement, and support that leaves room for independent judgment. The interview is https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nlnytQRvQ-E.
- Aristocracy should explain the original ideal of rule by the best for the common good and the difficulties of recognizing, selecting, constraining, and succeeding those rulers.
- Markets should explain their capacity to coordinate dispersed knowledge alongside the blind spots of purchasing power, externalities, information, and power.

The homepage's personal picks express the owner's experiences, not the agent's. Preserve those reasons and do not invent new first-person claims. The complete phrase “explanation by Samo Burja” is the link in the Awards pick.

Every autonomously developed technology, dynamic, and case-study page must retain the notice **“AI-assisted draft — this page needs further editorial guidance.”** Case studies require an explicit `needs-guidance` status; automated review never counts as owner editorial approval. Keep the homepage notice explaining that the project uses AI, will change in content and structure, and may need significant editorial guidance. Do not silently remove these notices after an automated check or a copy edit.

## Editing and verification

- Edit source content, not generated files in `site/dist/`.
- The current autonomous articles live in `site/research/editorial/` batch JSON files. They override corresponding catalog descriptions and bodies. The five closely developed pages use `site/content/editorial.json`, catalog introductions, and the referenced HTML files.
- Maintain `*-relations.json` alongside article links; `reviewed-relations.json` covers the five closely developed pages. Each relationship has a target ID and an editorial reason.
- When adding or reclassifying entries, update the catalog, scale placements, editorial manifest, relationships, case references, and relevant import inputs together. Avoid reviving retired entries through an old outline.
- For content or linking changes, run `python3 scripts/build.py` and `npm test` from `site/`. The checks cover links, relationship coverage, notices, search, and generated pages. Passing checks does not establish editorial quality or factual truth; review the prose and link meanings too.
- Keep the site static and the technology simple. Preserve the search behavior: fetch the index after the full page loads, and keep the input disabled until the index is ready. Use localhost for local preview.

## Publication boundary for case studies

- Public cases live in `site/content/cases/articles.json`; the case renderer accepts only the declared public fields. Write case articles as reader-facing explanations, not automatic translations of observations, hypotheses, lessons, or research-gap fields.
- `site/research/cases/` contains internal dossiers. Never export these records, their metadata, or the research backlog into `dist/`, even behind a download link. Research metadata is not a public article.
- The public evidence guide lives in `site/content/cases/evidence-guide.md`. It explains how to interpret evidence; it must not narrate our research process.
- Draft notices are enforced on every case. Source separation and tests prevent structural leaks; they do not establish that prose is clear. Review full rendered articles for context, transitions, unnecessary qualifications, and copied research instructions. Do not report all editorial problems solved merely because a scan passes.
